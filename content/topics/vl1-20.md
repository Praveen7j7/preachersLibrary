# Overcoming Greed

> **VL1 · 45–60 min · review: sources checked, awaiting senior review.** A character talk for students and young professionals. Scripture light but
> exact: five verses carry the talk; the rest prepare the preacher. IYF's slides for this topic (VOICE, "Overcoming
> Greed", Problems → Causes → Solutions → Applications) give the spine; their statistics and attributed quotations
> are not reused (see J and O).

## A. Topic overview

| | |
|---|---|
| Batch / level | VL1 (Vedic Level 1), a stand-alone talk |
| Suggested duration | 45–60 min (G gives the 60-minute flow and a 30-minute cut) |
| Preparation time | About 90 min, the first time |
| Difficulty | Introductory |
| Prerequisites | None |
| Audience | Students comparing marks, phones and placements; young professionals comparing salaries; many with no settled faith |
| Teaching objective | Show that greed is a hunger that does not end when it is fed, explain why from the Gita and Bhagavatam without moralising, and give one practical way to grow contentment this week |

## B. Why this topic belongs at this level

Greed is the one "vice" nobody thinks they have. Everyone else is greedy; I am ambitious. A VL1 room will accept a
talk on greed only if it is honest about ambition, money and comparison, and only if the speaker is not preaching
poverty. The aim is **attraction through self-recognition**: the listener should see their own "a little more" in
the diagnosis and go home with a method, not a scolding.

IYF's slides open with definitions and quotations, then list eight "symptoms and consequences" of greed
(physiological, financial, corporate, social, familial, emotional, political, ecological) with death statistics,
then give the causes (BG 14.17, SB 3.12.26), the dynamics (SB 7.15.20–22) and seven tips. This page keeps the
**causes, dynamics and tips**, cuts the consequences list to one short segment, and drops the statistics and the
attributed quotations, which could not be sourced.

*Builds on vl1-17 (Overcoming anger; both are "gates" in BG 16.21) and vl1-11 (Eight tips for positive thinking; satisfaction as a mind habit): this talk keeps only greed's own dynamics (SB 7.15.20–22) and the quota, and does not repeat their examples.*

How the subject grows at other levels:

| Level | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **VL1** (here) | Greed as unending hunger (SB 7.15.20); its root in the mode of passion (BG 14.17); the practical cure, satisfaction as a trained habit (SB 7.15.22; BG 17.16); one experiment |
| VL2 | The modes of nature as a system (BG 14.17 within it); why body-based satisfaction stays incomplete |
| VL3 | Simple living as a practice; the quota (ISO 1) as a way of life |
| SPS | Greed turned toward Krsna: *laulyam* (intense eagerness), the one price of devotion (SB 7.15.20 purport) |
| DPS | Contentment as steadiness for those who earn and provide for others |
| BSS | Simple living in a student ashram; satisfaction in service |

**Not done here:**
- Telling anyone to earn less or give up a career. The Bhagavatam itself warns against poverty (SB 7.15.21 purport).
- Lists of sins and their punishments. Say once that greed, with lust and anger, is called a "gate" to degradation
  (BG 16.21) and move on.
- Moral statistics. IYF's slides carry death counts for malnutrition, smoking and pollution; none is sourced here
  and none is needed.

## C. Central message

Hunger ends when you eat; anger ends when it is spent; greed does not end even when a person has "enjoyed everything
in the world" (SB 7.15.20). The way out is not to want less by force but to **train the mind in satisfaction**: to
look honestly at what more possessions cost, and to find a fullness that does not depend on them.

## D. Learning outcomes

By the end, participants should be able to:
1. Say in their own words why greed differs from hunger and anger (SB 7.15.20): it has no built-in stopping point.
2. Name the Gita's source of greed, the mode of passion (BG 14.17), and recognise its symptoms in daily life
   (BG 14.12: "uncontrollable desire and hankering").
3. Explain the Bhagavatam's method for conquering greed, "discussing the disadvantages of accumulating wealth"
   (SB 7.15.22), and apply it to one thing they currently want.
4. Choose one practice for the week that trains satisfaction rather than acquisition.

## E. Scriptural foundation

**Use:** five references are enough for the talk. The rest prepare you for questions.

**SB 7.15.20**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/sb/7/15/20/
> "The strong bodily desires and needs of a person disturbed by hunger and thirst are certainly satisfied when he eats. Similarly, if one becomes very angry, that anger is satisfied by chastisement and its reaction. But as for greed, even if a greedy person has conquered all the directions of the world or has enjoyed everything in the world, still he will not be satisfied."
- **Context:** Narada Muni instructs King Yudhisthira on how civilised people should live (SB 7.15).
- **Teaching:** Hunger and anger have an end; greed has none.
- **Purport point:** Prabhupada links the verse to BG 3.37 (lust, anger and greed as causes of bondage) and calls
  greed "the greatest enemy caused by rajo-guṇa". He adds that greed "to enhance his Kṛṣṇa consciousness" is "a
  great boon".
- **Application (preacher's):** This is the whole diagnosis in one verse. Greed is not "wanting a lot"; it is a
  want with no finish line. Ask the room: when was the last time getting something ended the wanting?
- **Use:** in the talk; the anchor verse.

**BG 14.17**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/14/17/
> "From the mode of goodness, real knowledge develops; from the mode of passion, greed develops; and from the mode of ignorance develop foolishness, madness and illusion."
- **Teaching:** Greed has a source: the mode of passion.
- **Purport point:** "In the mode of passion, people become greedy, and their hankering for sense enjoyment has no
  limit." Even with "enough money and adequate arrangements", there is "neither happiness nor peace of mind". The
  passionate person's profession becomes "very troublesome" because of the schemes needed to keep up his status.
- **Application (preacher's):** Greed is a climate, not a character flaw. A city, an industry or a friend circle can
  run in the mode of passion, and the hankering comes with it. That is a relief: it is a condition, and conditions
  can change.
- **Use:** in the talk.

**BG 14.12**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/14/12/
> "…when there is an increase in the mode of passion the symptoms of great attachment, fruitive activity, intense endeavor, and uncontrollable desire and hankering develop."
- **Purport point:** One in passion "is never satisfied with the position he has already acquired; he hankers to
  increase his position." The example is the house: wanting "a palatial house, as if he would be able to reside in
  that house eternally."
- **Application (preacher's):** A checklist for self-recognition: attachment, constant activity, strain, hankering.
  Not a verdict on anyone else.
- **Use:** in the talk (with the diagram in N).

**SB 7.15.22**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/sb/7/15/22/
> "…by discussing the disadvantages of accumulating wealth one should give up greed, and by discussing the truth one should give up fear."
- **Teaching:** Each enemy has its own method. For greed, the method is honest analysis of what accumulation costs.
- **Purport point:** Following Visvanatha Cakravarti Thakura, Prabhupada gives the example of cash: "If one keeps a
  large amount of cash with him, he is always anxious about keeping it properly." Discussing such disadvantages,
  one "can naturally give up business without difficulty", that is, the drive to accumulate.
- **Application (preacher's):** This is the method of the talk. Not "stop wanting", but "count the full price". The
  purport's first sentences deal with lusty desires and are not read out at VL1.
- **Use:** in the talk.

**SB 8.19.24**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/sb/8/19/24/
> "One should be satisfied with whatever he achieves by his previous destiny, for discontent can never bring happiness. A person who is not self-controlled will not be happy even with possessing the three worlds."
- **Context:** Lord Vamana, as a small brahmana boy, replies to King Bali, who has offered Him anything He wants. He
  asks for three paces of land.
- **Purport point:** "If happiness is the ultimate goal of life, one must be satisfied with the position in which he
  is placed by providence." The purport then quotes BG 6.21 (printed on vedabase within BG 6.20–23) on happiness perceived through spiritual
  senses.
- **Application (preacher's):** The one who could have asked for everything asked for three steps. Tell the frame of
  the story (J) and let the verse land. Satisfaction is presented as the condition for happiness, not as resignation.
- **Use:** in the talk, near the end.

**For questions (K):**
- **SB 8.19.21** (Verified): "even the entirety of whatever there may be within the three worlds to satisfy one's senses
  cannot satisfy a person whose senses are uncontrolled." Purport: "Anyone who is in this material world is extremely
  anxious to get more and more things for sense gratification." For "won't more money fix it?"
- **SB 7.15.21** (Verified): many learned and eminent people "fall down into hellish life because of not being
  satisfied with their positions." Purport: "one should not be poverty-stricken, but one must try to be fully
  satisfied with the bare necessities of life and not be greedy." Guards against "so you want us to be poor?"
- **BG 2.70** (Verified): the person undisturbed by desires that flow in "like rivers into the ocean, which is ever
  being filled but is always still" alone achieves peace, "and not the man who strives to satisfy such desires."
  Purport: "As long as one has the material body, the demands of the body for sense gratification will continue."
  For "is it possible to have no desires?"
- **ISO 1** (Verified): everything is "controlled and owned by the Lord. One should therefore accept only those
  things necessary for himself, which are set aside as his quota". Purport: the cow gives milk but does not drink
  it. The idea of "a quota" is the positive alternative to greed; at VL1 present it as the Upanisad's proposal.
- **BG 17.16** (Verified): "satisfaction, simplicity, gravity, self-control and purification of one's existence are
  the austerities of the mind." Purport: "The more we think of sense enjoyment, the more the mind becomes
  dissatisfied." For "how do I actually train satisfaction?"
- **BG 16.21** (Verified): "There are three gates leading to this hell – lust, anger and greed. Every sane man should
  give these up". Purport: "One tries to satisfy his lust, and when he cannot, anger and greed arise." One sentence
  at VL1, no more.
- **BG 3.37** (Verified): lust, "born of contact with the material mode of passion", is "the all-devouring sinful
  enemy of this world." Background: the verse Prabhupada links to SB 7.15.20.
- **SB 3.12.26** (Verified): in the account of creation, greed appears "from between his lips" (Brahma's). IYF's slides use this as the
  "origin" of greed. Purport: "The theory of becoming desireless is untenable because the mind cannot be made
  desireless"; one should not desire "things which are destructive to spiritual values." Background only; the
  creation account is not for a VL1 room.

## F. Philosophical explanation

1. **The principle.** Greed (*lobha*) is desire without a stopping point (SB 7.15.20). It arises from the mode of
   passion (BG 14.17), whose symptoms are attachment, constant activity, strain and hankering (BG 14.12). Because the
   senses are never filled by sense objects (SB 8.19.21), accumulation cannot cure it. What cures it is a trained
   satisfaction: seeing the real cost of accumulation (SB 7.15.22), being content with what comes by one's lot
   (SB 8.19.24), and finding a fullness that does not depend on possessions (BG 2.70).
2. **Why it matters.** A generation is being taught to measure itself by package, phone and followers. Greed is the
   engine of comparison, and comparison is a large part of young people's unhappiness. Naming the engine gives them
   a handle.
3. **Connections.** The modes of nature (VL1-25 uses them for work styles); the pleasure hunt (VL1-21): greed is the
   hunt's fuel; work and results (VL1-10): "do your best, leave the rest" is the opposite of grasping.
4. **Consequences.** A person who grows satisfaction is calmer, more honest (less to cheat for), more generous and,
   in experience, better company. This is a claim about tendency, not a promise of success.
5. **Misunderstandings to prevent.**
   - **"Greed is just ambition."** Ambition has a goal and can rest when it is reached. Greed moves the goal
     (BG 14.12 purport).
   - **"Spirituality wants me poor."** No: the purport to SB 7.15.21 says poverty too destroys good qualities. The
     target is "bare necessities" with contentment, and "necessities" is honestly discussed, not dictated.
   - **"Desirelessness."** The mind cannot be made desireless (SB 3.12.26 purport); the Gita's picture is the full
     ocean, not the empty one (BG 2.70). Desire is redirected, not abolished.
   - **Judging others.** Greed in corporations and politics (IYF's slides) is real, but the talk is about the
     listener's own "a little more".
6. **Pitch for VL1.** Say "greed", "the hunger that does not end", "satisfaction", "the quota". *Lobha* and *rajo-guna*
   may be said once each and defined. No appeal to authority: "Here is a 5,000-year-old analysis of wanting. Test it
   against your own week."

## H. Opening and engagement

**H.1 How to start (first 5 minutes)**
1. Greet the room, say your name, and say the talk is an exploration, not a demand to believe or to give things up.
2. Write the title on the board: "Overcoming greed".
3. Ask H.2 question 1 by show of hands; thank the room.
4. Ask question 2; take two or three spoken answers and thank each speaker.
5. Give the transition line below and move to the diagnosis (SB 7.15.20).

**H.2 Opening questions** (about the room's own life)
1. Think of the last thing you really wanted and then got (a phone, a job offer, marks). Did the wanting stop when you got it?
2. Within a month, had it moved to the next thing?
3. When you compare yourself with a friend or colleague, what exactly do you compare (income, grades, possessions)?

- **Opening question:** "Think of the last thing you really wanted and then got: the phone, the offer, the marks.
  Raise your hand if the wanting stopped when you got it." Pause. "Now raise your hand if, within a month, it had
  moved to the next thing." Most hands go up twice.
- **Relatable scenario (invented):** Two flatmates, Dev and Sameer, get their first salaries. Dev upgrades his phone,
  then his bike, then his flat, and is somehow always short. Sameer keeps the same phone, sends money home, and has
  savings. Who is "doing better"? Who sleeps better? What does each think about on Sunday evening?
- **Transition:** "An ancient text makes a precise observation about this. It says hunger ends when you eat and anger
  ends when it is spent, but one thing does not end even if you 'conquer all the directions of the world'."
- **Why it matters to this audience:** Placement packages (starting salaries offered by companies) compared in the hostel corridor; EMIs (monthly loan
  instalments); the next upgrade; parents' expectations. Anywhere in the world the same pressure appears as the neighbour's car, a friend's promotion or a village family's wish for a bigger house. Greed is not a rich person's problem; it is the daily pressure to have "a little more".

## I. Core teaching points

1. **Greed has no finish line.** SB 7.15.20. *Plain:* hunger ends when you eat; greed does not end when you get.
   *Example:* the salary that felt like a fortune in the offer letter and "nothing" twelve months later.
   *Transition:* if it never ends, where does it come from?
2. **It comes from a mode, not from a bad heart.** BG 14.17; BG 14.12. *Plain:* passion is a climate; its symptoms
   are attachment, non-stop activity, strain and hankering. *Example:* a whole office running on "more": targets,
   appraisals, the next phone. *Transition:* so what does it actually cost?
3. **Count the full price.** SB 7.15.22 and purport. *Plain:* everything you acquire must be protected, maintained,
   insured, upgraded and worried about. *Example:* the purport's cash example; a car's EMI, parking, scratches and
   envy. *Transition:* if "more" cannot fill the gap, what can?
4. **Enough is a quota, not a cage.** ISO 1. *Plain:* the Upanisad proposes that each of us has a portion; taking
   it is right, grabbing beyond it is theft from the whole. *Example:* the cow and her milk (purport). *Transition:*
   is contentment possible without becoming passive?
5. **Satisfaction is a skill of the mind.** BG 17.16; SB 8.19.24. *Plain:* the Gita lists satisfaction among the
   austerities of the mind, something trained, and the Bhagavatam says discontent "can never bring happiness".
   *Example:* Vamana asking for three steps when He could have asked for everything (J). *Transition:* to the
   experiment.

## J. Stories and illustrations

- **Narada's analysis of the four enemies** (SB 7.15.20–22). Not a narrative but a teaching passage; present it as
  "a sage's diagnosis given to a king". *Connection:* diagnosis and method.
- **Vamana and Bali** (SB 8.19, the verse used is 8.19.24). Tell only the frame: the Lord appears as a small brahmana
  boy at King Bali's sacrifice; Bali offers Him anything; He asks for three paces of land and gives the reason in
  8.19.21–24. The later events of the story (the Lord's expansion, Bali's surrender) belong to another talk. Do not
  add dialogue beyond the verses. *Connection:* the one who could have everything chose "enough".
- **Contemporary (invented sketch):** Dev and Sameer, the two flatmates (H). Label it invented.
- **Not used:** IYF's slides and notes carry stories (a politician's wife's 30,000 pairs of shoes; a hospital where
  nurses bet on patients; a kidney theft; a jail "campus interview") and quotations attributed to Voltaire, Emerson,
  Publilius Syrus, Hsi Tang and Maurice Selter, and one to Srila Prabhupada ("Greed is potential, lust is an active
  outburst"). None could be sourced here. **Requires source review** before any is used; do not read them out as
  facts. The notes' parable of the three punishments (gold coins, lashes or ten kilos of ghee) is a traditional
  story of unknown origin; if told, say so.

## K. Anticipated questions and objections

1. **"Isn't wanting more just ambition? Without it nothing gets built."**
   - The talk does not oppose goals or effort; the Gita tells Arjuna to act with full energy (see VL1-10). It opposes
     the want with no finish line (SB 7.15.20) and the strain that comes with it (BG 14.12). A clear goal can rest
     when reached; greed moves the goalpost. Ask: does your "more" have a number?
2. **"Are you telling us to be poor? Our families depend on us."**
   - No. The purport to SB 7.15.21 says plainly that poverty too destroys good qualities, and that the adjustment is
     to be "fully satisfied with the bare necessities" rather than greedy. Earning for one's dependants is duty, not
     greed. The question is what "necessities" honestly means for me.
3. **"If I earned enough, I would stop. Greed is a poor person's problem."**
   - The Bhagavatam says the opposite: even "the entirety of whatever there may be within the three worlds" cannot
     satisfy uncontrolled senses (SB 8.19.21), and learned, eminent people fall "because of not being satisfied with
     their positions" (SB 7.15.21). Invite them to check their own history: did any raise end the wanting?
4. **"Isn't it unhealthy to suppress desire?"**
   - Agreed. The purport to SB 3.12.26 says the mind "cannot be made desireless". The Gita's picture is an ocean that
     stays full while rivers keep flowing in (BG 2.70): desires come, and the person is not disturbed because of
     fullness. The method is redirection and honest costing (SB 7.15.22), not suppression.
5. **"What about the greedy companies and politicians? Why lecture us?"**
   - Because the only greed any of us can treat is our own, and because systems are made of persons. IYF's slides
     list corporate and political consequences; mention them in one sentence and come back to the room.
6. **"Is wanting God also greed?"**
   - A good question for later levels. Prabhupada's purport to SB 7.15.20 says that greed "to enhance his Kṛṣṇa
     consciousness" is "a great boon". At VL1 say only: the capacity to want is not the problem; its object is.

## L. Case studies

*Invented cases for discussion. Not real incidents.*

**Case 1: The upgrade loop.** Ananya earns well. Every six months something new becomes "necessary": a better
phone, a weekend trip her friends took, a car because colleagues have one. She has no savings and a quiet feeling
that she is always behind.
- **Dilemma:** Is anything wrong here? She is not cheating anyone.
- **Principles:** SB 7.15.20 (no finish line); BG 14.12 (hankering as a symptom); SB 7.15.22 (count the full price).
- **Questions:**
  - Which of her purchases were needs, which were comparison?
  - What does each item cost beyond its price (time, worry, maintenance, the next upgrade)?
  - What would "enough" look like for her, in a number she chooses?
- **Suggested analysis:** Nothing immoral, and that is the point: greed is usually legal. The cost is internal
  (always behind) and relational (friends become benchmarks). Pricing each want honestly is the first step.
- **Takeaway:** Greed is diagnosed by its restlessness, not by its legality.

**Case 2: The father's advice.** Rohit's father tells him to take the higher-paying offer in another city even
though the lower-paying one is near home and in work he loves. "Money first; happiness later."
- **Dilemma:** Is the father greedy? Is Rohit naive?
- **Principles:** SB 7.15.21 (neither poverty nor greed); SB 8.19.24 (discontent never brings happiness); BG 14.17
  (greed as a climate one can be in without noticing).
- **Questions:**
  - What legitimate duties stand behind the father's advice?
  - What is the hidden assumption in "happiness later"?
  - How could Rohit decide this without either resentment or recklessness?
- **Suggested analysis:** The talk does not decide his career. It exposes the assumption that happiness is bought
  later with money earned now (SB 8.19.21 and 8.19.24 deny this). A decision that honours his duties and his honest
  "enough" is possible either way.
- **Takeaway:** "Later" is where greed hides.

## M. Discussion and reflection

**M.1 Closing check questions** (easy first; praise every try)
1. What ends when you eat? *Hunger (SB 7.15.20).*
2. Does greed end when a person gets what he wanted? *No; the wanting moves to the next thing.*
3. From which mode does the Gita say greed arises? *Passion (BG 14.17).*
4. Name two symptoms of increased passion. *Any two of: great attachment, fruitive activity, intense endeavour, uncontrollable desire and hankering (BG 14.12).*
5. Does the Bhagavatam tell us to be poor? *No; poverty also destroys good qualities (SB 7.15.21 purport). The aim is satisfaction with what is needed.*
6. What method does SB 7.15.22 give? *Count honestly the disadvantages of accumulating.*

**M.2 Activity (about 10 minutes): "Price it"**
1. 1 min: each person silently picks one thing they want now.
2. 4 min, in pairs: list its full price (money, time, worry, upkeep, what it will make you want next).
3. 3 min: volunteers say whether the list changed the want (sharing the item is optional). Thank each volunteer.
4. 2 min: link to SB 7.15.22 and to the quota (ISO 1): enough is a portion, not a cage.

**M.3 Reflection**
- **Reflection:** Think of one thing you want now. List its full price: money, time, worry, maintenance, what it
  will make you want next. Does the list change the want?
- **Application (private, optional):** Write one honest sentence beginning "Enough for me this year would be…".
  *(Nobody needs to share.)*

# Part 2: For students

## S1. Summary

Hunger ends when you eat. Anger ends when it is spent. But the Srimad-Bhagavatam, an ancient Sanskrit scripture, observes that greed does not end even for someone who has "enjoyed everything in the world" (SB 7.15.20). The wanting simply moves to the next thing: the phone, the salary, the bigger house. Most of us have seen this in our own lives.

The Bhagavad-gita says where this hunger comes from: the mode of passion, a climate of attachment, constant activity, strain and hankering (BG 14.17, 14.12). So greed is not proof of a bad heart; it is what a restless climate of mind produces. More possessions cannot cure it, because they feed the hunger without ending it. The Bhagavatam also warns against the opposite extreme: poverty destroys good qualities too (SB 7.15.21 purport). The aim is satisfaction with what is needed.

What works is training the mind in satisfaction. First, honestly count the full price of what we want: money, time, worry and upkeep (SB 7.15.22). Second, take our fair portion rather than grab (ISO 1, the *Sri Isopanisad*, which says everything belongs to the Lord and each of us has an allotted share). Third, find a fullness that does not depend on getting: desires flow in like rivers, but the person like the ocean stays steady (BG 2.70). The scripture says discontent "can never bring happiness" (SB 8.19.24). This week, try pricing one thing you want.

## S2. Key ideas

- Greed is the want with no finish line (SB 7.15.20).
- It comes from a mode, the mode of passion, not from being a bad person (BG 14.17).
- Its symptoms: attachment, non-stop activity, strain, hankering (BG 14.12).
- The cure is not to want less by force but to count the full price of what we want (SB 7.15.22).
- "Enough" is a portion, not a cage (ISO 1); satisfaction is a skill of the mind (BG 17.16).

## S3. Verses to remember

- **SB 7.15.20:** "…as for greed, even if a greedy person has conquered all the directions of the world or has
  enjoyed everything in the world, still he will not be satisfied." *For me:* the next thing will not end the wanting;
  something else must.
- **BG 14.17:** "…from the mode of passion, greed develops…" *For me:* when I feel the hankering, I can ask what
  climate I am in, rather than blaming myself.
- **SB 8.19.24:** "…discontent can never bring happiness." *For me:* satisfaction is the condition of happiness, not
  its reward.

## S4. Words to know

- **Greed (lobha):** desire that keeps moving its goal; the hunger that does not end when fed.
- **Mode of passion (rajo-guna):** one of nature's three "climates"; its signs are attachment, restless activity,
  strain and hankering (BG 14.12).
- **Quota:** the Isopanisad's idea that each of us has a fair portion of what exists; taking it is right, grabbing
  beyond it is not (ISO 1).

## S5. Questions and answers

1. **Isn't wanting more just ambition?**
   A goal can rest when it is reached. Greed moves the goalpost (SB 7.15.20). Ask whether your "more" has a number.
2. **Are you asking me to be poor?**
   No. The Bhagavatam's teacher says poverty too destroys good qualities; the aim is to be satisfied with what is
   needed, not greedy (SB 7.15.21 and purport). Supporting your family is duty, not greed.
3. **If I earned enough, I would stop.**
   Scripture says that even everything in the three worlds cannot satisfy uncontrolled senses (SB 8.19.21). Check
   your own history: did any raise end the wanting?
4. **Isn't suppressing desire unhealthy?**
   Yes. The Gita's picture is an ocean that stays full while rivers keep flowing in (BG 2.70). Desires come; the
   full person is not disturbed. We redirect and honestly price desires; we do not crush them.
5. **What about greedy companies and politicians?**
   Real, but the only greed I can treat is my own, and systems are made of persons.

## S6. Check your understanding

1. According to SB 7.15.20, which of these has no natural end: (a) hunger, (b) anger, (c) greed.
2. From which mode of nature does the Gita say greed develops (BG 14.17)?
3. Name two of the four symptoms of increased passion in BG 14.12.
4. True or false: the Bhagavatam recommends poverty as the cure for greed.
5. In your own words: what method does SB 7.15.22 give for giving up greed?

**Answers:** 1. (c) (SB 7.15.20). 2. The mode of passion (BG 14.17). 3. Any two of: great attachment, fruitive
activity, intense endeavour, uncontrollable desire and hankering (BG 14.12). 4. False: the purport to SB 7.15.21 says
poverty too destroys good qualities; the aim is satisfaction with necessities. 5. Discussing, or honestly counting,
the disadvantages of accumulating wealth (SB 7.15.22).
