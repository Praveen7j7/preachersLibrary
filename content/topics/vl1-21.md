# Pleasure Hunt

> **VL1 · 45–60 min · review: sources checked, awaiting senior review.** A relevance talk for students and young professionals. Scripture light but
> exact: five verses carry the talk, the rest prepare the preacher. IYF's slides ("Pleasure Hunt", five parts: who
> hunts, why, how, what we experience, conclusion) give the spine; their unsourced verses and anecdotes are not
> reused (see J).

## A. Topic overview

| | |
|---|---|
| Batch / level | VL1 (Vedic Level 1), a stand-alone talk |
| Suggested duration | 45–60 min (G gives the 60-minute flow and a 30-minute cut) |
| Preparation time | About 90 min, the first time |
| Difficulty | Introductory |
| Prerequisites | None |
| Audience | Students and young professionals who organise weekends, feeds and purchases around pleasure and feel oddly flat; many with no settled faith |
| Teaching objective | Take the universal search for pleasure seriously, show from the Gita why the usual places disappoint (beginning and end; nectar then poison), and open the possibility of a pleasure that does not fade, without demanding faith or asceticism |

## B. Why this topic belongs at this level

Nobody has to be persuaded that they want to be happy. A VL1 room will listen to anyone who talks honestly about
pleasure: why the party, the series, the trip and the purchase keep promising more than they give. The aim is
**attraction through recognition**: the listener should hear their own weekend described, agree with the diagnosis,
and want to know what the Gita means by "a higher taste".

IYF's slides ask five questions: who hunts for pleasure (every living being), why (the soul is by nature joyful),
how (senses, mind, intellect, ego), what we experience (painful, addictive, risky, illusory, stale, flickering), and
the conclusion (we hunt in the wrong place with the wrong tools). This page keeps the **five questions and the six
adjectives**, but builds each adjective on a verse that could be verified, and keeps the sexual material of the
slides for the preacher's background only.

*Builds on vl1-01 (Happiness), which introduces the beginning and end of sense pleasure (BG 5.22) and the deeper happiness: here BG 5.22 gets one minute; this talk keeps the hunt itself (the five questions, the six adjectives), the three kinds of happiness (BG 18.37–39) and the higher taste, with different examples.*

How the subject grows at other levels:

| Level | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **VL1** (here) | Pleasure from sense contact has a beginning and an end (BG 5.22); three kinds of happiness (BG 18.37–39); "chewing the chewed" (SB 7.5.30); the higher taste as an invitation (BG 2.59) |
| VL2 | Who is the enjoyer: the soul's nature; the body as instrument; why *dukhalayam* (BG 8.15) is a precise, not a gloomy, description |
| VL3 | How the higher taste is cultivated: hearing, chanting, *prasadam* (food offered with devotion), association; transcendental senses (BG 6.20–23) |
| SPS | Keeping the higher taste when old pleasures return; standards that protect it |
| DPS | Pleasure and duty for those who serve others: enjoyment without being driven by it |
| BSS | Sense control in a student ashram as an experiment in higher taste |

**Not done here:**
- Condemning the audience's pleasures or asking anyone to give anything up. The talk diagnoses and invites.
- Sexual ethics. IYF's slides spend time on sex pleasure (King Bhartrhari's verse, SB 7.9.45). At VL1, say once that
  the Gita's own example of "nectar at first, poison at the end" is romantic attraction (BG 18.38 purport) and move
  on; detailed treatment belongs to later levels.
- Attacking science or religion. The slides' asides about scientists and "religious horses" are not reused.

## C. Central message

Everyone is hunting for pleasure, and the hunt is not wrong: joy is our nature. But pleasure that comes from the
senses touching their objects "has a beginning and an end" (BG 5.22), and it tastes like nectar first and poison
later (BG 18.38). The Gita does not say "stop hunting". It says we are hunting in the wrong place, and it points to a
"higher taste" that, once experienced, changes what we want (BG 2.59).

## D. Learning outcomes

By the end, participants should be able to:
1. State the Gita's two marks of sense pleasure: it comes from contact of the senses with their objects, and it has
   a beginning and an end (BG 5.22).
2. Distinguish the three kinds of happiness in BG 18.37–39 by their beginning and end (poison → nectar; nectar →
   poison; delusion throughout) and sort two pleasures from their own week into them.
3. Explain "chewing the chewed" (SB 7.5.30) and "butter on fire" (SB 9.19.14) in plain words, as descriptions of
   repetition and escalation.
4. Describe what the Gita means by a higher taste (BG 2.59) as an experience to test, not a rule to obey.

## E. Scriptural foundation

**Use:** five references are enough for the talk. The rest prepare you for questions.

**BG 5.22**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/5/22/
> "An intelligent person does not take part in the sources of misery, which are due to contact with the material senses. O son of Kuntī, such pleasures have a beginning and an end, and so the wise man does not delight in them."
- **Context:** Krsna has been describing the person who finds pleasure within rather than in the senses, and now
  gives the reason.
- **Teaching:** Sense pleasure is defined by its source (contact) and its shape (a beginning and an end).
- **Purport point:** Such pleasures are "temporary because the body itself is temporary". The purport quotes SB 5.5.1
  (sense pleasure is available even to hogs; human life is for purifying one's existence) and closes: "The more one
  is addicted to material pleasures, the more he is entrapped by material miseries."
- **Application (preacher's):** This is IYF's "flickering" and "painful" in one verse. The verse's reason for not
  delighting in sense pleasure is its shape (a beginning and an end). Let the room confirm the shape from experience.
- **Use:** in the talk; the anchor verse.

**BG 18.38**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/18/38/
> "That happiness which is derived from contact of the senses with their objects and which appears like nectar at first but poison at the end is said to be of the nature of passion."
- **Purport point:** Prabhupada's example is a young man and woman drawn together by the senses; "in the beginning
  this may be very pleasing to the senses, but at the end, or after some time, it becomes just like poison",
  with separation, lamentation and sorrow. "Happiness derived from a combination of the senses and the sense objects
  is always a cause of distress".
- **Application (preacher's):** The phrase "nectar first, poison later" is the talk's refrain. At VL1, name the
  purport's example in one sentence and let the room supply their own (the binge, the splurge, the all-nighter).
- **Use:** in the talk.

**BG 18.37**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/18/37/
> "That which in the beginning may be just like poison but at the end is just like nectar and which awakens one to self-realization is said to be happiness in the mode of goodness."
- **Purport point:** The rules for controlling mind and senses "are very difficult, bitter like poison, but if one is
  successful… he begins to drink real nectar, and he enjoys life."
- **Application (preacher's):** The room already knows this shape from study, fitness and music practice. The Gita
  says the same shape governs the deeper happiness. This is the bridge from diagnosis to invitation.
- **Use:** in the talk.

**BG 18.39**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/18/39/
> "And that happiness which is blind to self-realization, which is delusion from beginning to end and which arises from sleep, laziness and illusion is said to be of the nature of ignorance."
- **Purport point:** For the person in ignorance "there is only distress both in the beginning and at the end."
- **Application (preacher's):** The scroll at 2 a.m., the fourth episode nobody is enjoying. Said lightly; the room
  will laugh in recognition.
- **Use:** in the talk (completes the three-row table in N).

**BG 2.59**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/2/59/
> "Though the embodied soul may be restricted from sense enjoyment, the taste for sense objects remains. But, ceasing such engagements by experiencing a higher taste, he is fixed in consciousness."
- **Teaching:** Restriction alone leaves the taste intact; only a higher taste changes it.
- **Purport point:** Rules are "something like restricting a diseased person from certain types of eatables"; the
  patient "neither likes such restrictions nor loses his taste". One who "has tasted the beauty of the Supreme Lord
  Kṛṣṇa… no longer has a taste for dead, material things."
- **Application (preacher's):** The talk's conclusion is this verse, offered as an experiment: not "give up", but
  "taste something and see what happens to the old wants". Present it as what practitioners report, not as a
  demand.
- **Use:** in the talk, at the end.

**For questions (K):**
- **SB 7.5.30** (Verified): persons "too addicted to materialistic life… repeatedly chew that which has already been
  chewed." IYF's "stale". Purport: the materialist "tries to enjoy sense gratification in different bodily forms,
  life after life, but is still unsatisfied." Use the phrase; leave the purport's remarks on politicians aside.
- **SB 9.19.13** (Verified): a lusty person "cannot satisfy his mind even if he has enough of everything in this
  world… Nothing can satisfy him." Purport: "there is no end to this material advancement". IYF's "illusory".
- **SB 9.19.14** (Verified): "As supplying butter to a fire does not diminish the fire but instead increases it more
  and more, the endeavor to stop lusty desires by continual enjoyment can never be successful." IYF's "addictive".
  The verse's own word is "lusty desires"; at VL1 apply it to appetite in general and say that you are doing so.
- **BG 6.20-23** (Verified; vedabase prints the four verses on one page): in the perfected state "one is situated in
  boundless transcendental happiness, realized through transcendental senses… upon gaining this he thinks there is
  no greater gain." Purport: "transcendental pleasure – realized through transcendental senses – is accepted."
  For "is there any pleasure that does not fade?"
- **BG 8.15** (Verified): great souls who attain Krsna "never return to this temporary world, which is full of
  miseries". IYF cites *duḥkhālayam aśāśvatam*. Purport: "full of the miseries of birth, old age, disease and
  death". Background; at VL1 use the two adjectives, temporary and full of miseries, not the doctrine of return.
- **BG 2.14** (Verified): happiness and distress come and go like seasons; "one must learn to tolerate them without
  being disturbed." For "so should I just feel nothing?"
- **SB 7.9.45** (Verified): sex pleasure "compared to the rubbing of two hands to relieve an itch"; the sober "who
  tolerate this itching, are not subjected to the sufferings of fools". Prahlada's words. **Background only**: it
  is the verse behind IYF's "most intense sense pleasure" slide. Not for the VL1 talk.

## F. Philosophical explanation

1. **The principle.** The search for pleasure is universal and legitimate; the Gita itself describes a "boundless"
   happiness as the goal (BG 6.20–23). The problem is the place. Pleasure that arises from the senses touching their
   objects has a beginning and an end (BG 5.22) and runs nectar-to-poison (BG 18.38). Repetition does not fix it:
   it becomes "chewing the chewed" (SB 7.5.30), and feeding it makes it grow like butter on fire (SB 9.19.14). The
   deeper happiness has the opposite shape, poison-to-nectar (BG 18.37), and is reached by a higher taste, not by
   restriction alone (BG 2.59).
2. **Why it matters.** The audience is the most entertained generation in history and reports feeling flat. The Gita
   offers a diagnosis that is neither "enjoy more" nor "enjoy less" but "look at the shape of what you enjoy".
3. **Connections.** Greed (VL1-20) is the hunt's fuel; the modes of nature (VL1-25) classify the pleasures; "who
   am I?" (VL2) explains why a spiritual being is not filled by material contact.
4. **Consequences.** A person who can name nectar-to-poison before, not after, makes fewer regretted choices and
   enjoys legitimate pleasures with less anxiety. A person who has tasted something deeper finds the old wants
   quieter without a fight (BG 2.59 purport). This is a report of tendency, not a promise.
5. **Misunderstandings to prevent.**
   - **"The Gita is against pleasure."** It is against disappointment. It names a happiness with "no greater gain"
     (BG 6.22) and compares the deeper path to nectar (BG 18.37).
   - **"So I should suppress."** BG 2.59 says the taste remains under suppression; only a higher taste works.
   - **"Life is miserable."** *Duḥkhālayam* (BG 8.15) describes the place, not the person; BG 2.14 asks for
     tolerance, not gloom.
   - **Preaching asceticism to beginners.** No rule is imposed at VL1.
6. **Pitch for VL1.** Say "pleasure", "the shape of it", "nectar first, poison later", "a higher taste". *Sukha* and
   *duḥkha* may be said once. Present Krsna as the speaker of the Gita without arguing His divinity; "who gives the
   higher taste?" is a VL2 door, and the practice (chanting, prasadam, association) is mentioned as what
   practitioners do.
7. **Srila Prabhupada's opening of the same subject** (*Kṛṣṇa, the Reservoir of Pleasure*, BBT booklet; copy in IYF's
   Drive, folder "3. VL 3", item 5): "Kṛṣṇa means the highest pleasure. All of us, every living being, seeks pleasure.
   But we do not know how to seek pleasure perfectly." The talk's thesis in three sentences: the hunt is right, the
   knowledge is missing. Quote it in P if the room is ready to hear the name Krsna; otherwise paraphrase.

## H. Opening and engagement

**H.1 How to start (first 5 minutes)**
1. Greet the room, say your name, and say the talk is about enjoying life, not giving it up.
2. Write the title on the board: "Pleasure hunt".
3. Ask H.2 question 1 by show of hands; thank the room.
4. Ask question 2; take two or three spoken answers and thank each speaker.
5. Give the transition line below and go to "who hunts, and why".

**H.2 Opening questions** (about the room's own life)
1. Think of the best thing you did for fun this month. Is the pleasure of it still with you now?
2. Have you already planned the next one?
3. What do people around you (friends, colleagues, neighbours) do to relax, and does it work?

- **Opening question:** "Think of the best thing you did for fun this month. Raise your hand if the pleasure of it is
  still with you now." Pause. "Raise your hand if you have already planned the next one." The second count is
  larger. "That gap is tonight's subject."
- **Relatable scenario (invented):** Aarav saves for months for a holiday trip. The first days are bliss. Three weeks after
  coming home it is a folder of photos, and he is already looking at the next destination. He is not unhappy; he is
  just already looking. Then the same with the series, the party, the relationship.
- **Transition:** "The Gita noticed this shape five thousand years ago and gave it a name: nectar at first, poison at
  the end. It also says there is a pleasure with the opposite shape."
- **Why it matters to this audience:** Weekends built around escape; feeds built around other people's pleasure;
  the suspicion that "having fun" has become a second job.

## I. Core teaching points

1. **Everyone is hunting, and the hunt is not wrong.** *Plain:* joy is our nature; the Gita describes a boundless
   happiness as the goal (BG 6.20–23). *Example:* no one has to be taught to want to be happy. *Transition:* so why
   does the hunt disappoint?
2. **Sense pleasure has a shape.** BG 5.22. *Plain:* it comes from contact, and it has a beginning and an end.
   *Example:* Aarav's holiday (H). *Transition:* the Gita is more precise still about the shape.
3. **Nectar first, poison later.** BG 18.38; the three kinds in BG 18.37–39. *Plain:* passion's pleasure starts sweet
   and ends bitter; goodness's pleasure starts bitter and ends sweet; ignorance's is dull throughout. *Example:*
   the all-nighter versus the training schedule versus the 2 a.m. scroll. *Transition:* can't we just repeat the
   good part?
4. **Repetition goes stale; feeding it makes it grow.** SB 7.5.30; SB 9.19.14. *Plain:* "chewing the chewed";
   butter on a fire. *Example:* the fourth episode nobody is enjoying; the "one more" that never is. *Transition:*
   if more does not work and less does not work, what does?
5. **A higher taste.** BG 2.59. *Plain:* restriction leaves the taste; a better taste changes it. *Example:* the
   purport's patient and the diet. *Transition:* to the experiment: practitioners report that hearing, music of
   a certain kind (kirtan) and food offered with intention give this taste; try one.

## K. Anticipated questions and objections

1. **"So you are saying pleasure is bad?"**
   - Not pleasure as such. The Gita says a happiness exists with "no greater gain" (BG 6.20–23). What it questions is
     sense pleasure, because of its shape (beginning and end, BG 5.22; nectar then poison, BG 18.38). The talk is
     against disappointment, not against joy.
2. **"Should I give up films, music, food, friends?"**
   - Nothing is asked of you tonight. BG 2.59 itself says that restriction without a higher taste does not work. The
     proposal is to taste something new and watch what happens to the old wants.
3. **"Isn't 'this world is full of misery' just pessimism?"**
   - BG 8.15 describes the world as temporary and full of the miseries of birth, old age, disease and death; that is
     a description, not a mood. BG 2.14 asks for tolerance, like weathering seasons. The optimistic part is BG 18.37:
     a happiness that is bitter at first and "just like nectar" at the end.
4. **"What about the pleasure of achievement or art? That is not the senses."**
   - IYF's slides grade pleasures from sense to mind to intellect to ego and say all are obtained "by manipulating at
     the physical and subtle level". BG 18.37–39 sorts by beginning and end, not by refinement: an achievement that
     ends in emptiness still has the nectar-to-poison shape. Invite them to check.
5. **"What is this higher taste, concretely?"**
   - Practitioners describe it as the taste that comes from hearing this knowledge, from kirtan, from food offered
     with intention, and from the company of people who live this way. The Gita speaks of happiness "realized through
     transcendental senses" (BG 6.21). At VL1, offer one to try; VL3 teaches the practice.
6. **"My friend says pleasure is just chemistry in the brain."**
   - The Gita does not dispute that bodily pleasure involves the body; it says the enjoyer is not the body, which is
     VL2's question ("who am I?"). For tonight, the observation about shape holds whatever the mechanism.

## L. Case studies

*Invented cases for discussion. Not real incidents.*

**Case 1: The perfect weekend.** Nikhil plans every weekend months ahead: trips, concerts, restaurants. His
Instagram is envied. On Sunday nights he feels empty and starts planning the next one. He asks whether he is doing
something wrong.
- **Dilemma:** Nothing he does is harmful. Why the emptiness?
- **Principles:** BG 5.22 (beginning and end); BG 18.38 (nectar then poison); SB 7.5.30 (chewing the chewed).
- **Questions:**
  - Which of his pleasures has the nectar-to-poison shape? Which, if any, has the reverse?
  - Is the problem the pleasures or the expectation placed on them?
  - What one thing with the BG 18.37 shape (hard first, sweet later) could he add?
- **Suggested analysis:** The weekends are not wrong; they are carrying a weight they cannot bear. Adding one
  poison-to-nectar activity (learning, service, a practice) changes the balance without a renunciation drama.
- **Takeaway:** Ask what shape a pleasure has before asking whether it is allowed.

**Case 2: The quiet friend.** Meera notices that her friend Divya, who goes to a weekly spiritual programme, enjoys
simple things, laughs easily and does not seem to need the weekend escape. Meera is curious but afraid she will be
asked to give things up.
- **Principles:** BG 2.59 (higher taste changes wants without suppression); BG 18.37 (poison then nectar).
- **Questions:**
  - What is Meera actually afraid of?
  - According to BG 2.59, what would change first: Divya's rules or her taste?
  - What could Meera try once, with no commitment?
- **Suggested analysis:** The fear assumes restriction comes first. BG 2.59 says taste comes first and restriction
  takes care of itself. One visit, one hearing, is the experiment.
- **Takeaway:** You do not have to give anything up to find out whether the higher taste is real.

## M. Discussion and reflection

**M.1 Closing check questions** (easy first; praise every try)
1. Is the search for pleasure wrong? *No; joy is our nature. The question is where we look.*
2. What does BG 5.22 say about sense pleasure? *It has a beginning and an end.*
3. Which kind of happiness is nectar first and poison later? *The kind born of passion (BG 18.38).*
4. What does "chewing the chewed" mean? *Repeating what is already used up (SB 7.5.30).*
5. What does butter poured on a fire show? *Feeding desire makes it grow (SB 9.19.14).*
6. What does BG 2.59 say changes our taste? *Experiencing a higher taste, not restriction alone.*

**M.2 Activity (about 10 minutes): "Sort your week"**
1. 2 min: each person silently picks three pleasures from their own week.
2. 4 min, in pairs: sort them into the three rows of BG 18.37–39 (use the table in N); say which one lasted longest.
3. 3 min: volunteers share what surprised them (naming the pleasure is optional). Thank each volunteer.
4. 1 min: link to BG 2.59: a higher taste, tried as an experiment.

**M.3 Reflection**
- **Reflection:** Sort three pleasures from your own week into the three rows of BG 18.37–39. Was any sorting
  uncomfortable?
- **Application (private, optional):** Name one pleasure you repeat even though it no longer gives much. What is the
  "one more" that never arrives? *(Nobody needs to share.)*

# Part 2: For students

## S1. Summary

Everyone is hunting for pleasure, and that is not wrong: joy is our nature, and the Bhagavad-gita (an ancient Sanskrit scripture, a conversation between Krsna and the warrior Arjuna) itself speaks of a "boundless" happiness as the goal (BG 6.20–23). The trouble is where we hunt. Pleasure that comes from the senses touching their objects "has a beginning and an end" (BG 5.22). Think of any trip, purchase or evening out: the build-up is sweet, the end leaves us looking for the next one.

The Gita gives this a shape. The pleasure of passion tastes like nectar at first and poison later (BG 18.38). Repeating it goes stale, "chewing the chewed" (SB 7.5.30), and feeding desire makes it grow, like butter poured on a fire (SB 9.19.14). So the answer is not "more", and not a moral ban either.

The Gita describes another kind of happiness with the opposite shape: hard at first, sweet later (BG 18.37). And it says the way to it is not to suppress our wants but to experience "a higher taste" that changes them (BG 2.59). This week, try to predict the shape of one pleasure before you take it, and then try one thing of the other kind.

## S2. Key ideas

- Wanting to be happy is natural. The question is where we look.
- Sense pleasure has a shape: a beginning and an end (BG 5.22).
- Three kinds of happiness: poison then nectar; nectar then poison; dull throughout (BG 18.37–39).
- More of the same goes stale (SB 7.5.30) and feeds the fire (SB 9.19.14).
- A higher taste, not suppression, is what changes our wants (BG 2.59).

## S3. Verses to remember

- **BG 5.22:** "…such pleasures have a beginning and an end, and so the wise man does not delight in them." *For me:*
  before chasing something, I can ask what its end will feel like.
- **BG 18.38:** happiness from the senses "appears like nectar at first but poison at the end". *For me:* I know this
  shape already; now I have a name for it.
- **BG 2.59:** "…ceasing such engagements by experiencing a higher taste, he is fixed in consciousness." *For me:* I
  do not have to fight my wants; I can taste something better and watch them change.

## S4. Words to know

- **Higher taste:** a happiness deeper than sense pleasure which, once experienced, makes the old wants quieter
  (BG 2.59).
- **Nectar then poison:** the Gita's description of pleasure in the mode of passion: sweet start, bitter end
  (BG 18.38).
- **Chewing the chewed:** repeating a pleasure that has already lost its taste (SB 7.5.30).

## S5. Questions and answers

1. **Is pleasure bad?**
   Not pleasure as such. The Gita describes a happiness with "no greater gain" (BG 6.20–23). What it questions is
   sense pleasure, because it has a beginning and an end (BG 5.22). The talk is against disappointment, not joy.
2. **Do I have to give things up?**
   Nothing is asked tonight. BG 2.59 says restriction alone does not change taste; a higher taste does. Try something
   new and watch what happens.
3. **Isn't "the world is full of misery" pessimistic?**
   It is a description of a temporary place (BG 8.15), not a mood. BG 2.14 asks us to weather happiness and distress
   like seasons, and BG 18.37 describes a happiness that is "just like nectar" at the end.
4. **What is the higher taste, concretely?**
   People who practise describe it as the taste that comes from hearing this knowledge, from kirtan, from food
   offered with intention, and from good company. Try one, once.
5. **Isn't pleasure just brain chemistry?**
   The body is involved in bodily pleasure, yes. Who the enjoyer is becomes the question at the next level. The
   observation about shape holds either way.

## S6. Check your understanding

1. BG 5.22 says sense pleasures have: (a) no limit, (b) a beginning and an end, (c) only a beginning.
2. Which kind of happiness is "nectar at first but poison at the end" (BG 18.38): goodness, passion or ignorance?
3. Which kind is "poison in the beginning but nectar at the end" (BG 18.37)?
4. What does the image of butter on fire (SB 9.19.14) describe?
5. In your own words: according to BG 2.59, what changes a person's taste for sense pleasure?

**Answers:** 1. (b) (BG 5.22). 2. Passion (BG 18.38). 3. Goodness (BG 18.37). 4. Trying to end desire by enjoying
more only increases it (SB 9.19.14). 5. Experiencing a higher taste, not restriction alone (BG 2.59).
