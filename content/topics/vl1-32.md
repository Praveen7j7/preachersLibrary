# Overcoming bad habits

> **VL1 · 45–60 min · review: sources-checked.** IYF holds two near-identical decks for this topic (*OVERCOMING BAD HABITS*
> and *OBH2*), both built on James Clear's *Atomic Habits* (cue → craving → response → reward, and the four
> "inversions"), with BG 2.62 and BG 6.34 as the two verses. This page keeps the deck's practical spine but
> puts the Gita's own account of the habit loop first (BG 2.62–63, 3.36–37) and treats the book as a supporting
> source, cited as a book. VL1-04 *Power of habits* explains what a habit is; VL1-26 *Atomic Habits* covers the
> book; this talk is about **breaking** one. No addiction is diagnosed here (see K).

## B. Why this topic belongs at this level

Nobody in the room needs convincing that bad habits exist; they need a method that is not "try harder". The
Gita's second and third chapters give one: a causal chain (BG 2.62–63), a candid question about compulsion
(BG 3.36), its answer (3.37), the right place to intervene (3.41) and the replacement principle (2.59). That
matches what the audience already half-knows from popular books, which is why IYF's deck leans on *Atomic
Habits*. The talk lets the book illustrate and the Gita explain.

**Builds on vl1-26 and vl1-04:** those pages give the *Atomic Habits* framework and what a habit is. This page keeps only its own angle: the Gita's chain (BG 2.62–63) as a method for breaking one habit. Do not re-teach the framework; link [vl1-26](vl1-26.md) and [vl1-04](vl1-04.md).

- Shame. The Gita's own hero asks "why do I do what I don't want to do?" (3.36). The tone is "the mechanism is
  known, so it can be worked with".
- The four regulative principles as a list of rules (VL3-02).

## C. Central message

A bad habit is not a character flaw; it is a loop the Gita describes exactly: looking leads to attachment,
attachment to craving, craving to compulsion. Willpower loses because it fights at the end of the chain. We win
by moving the fight to the beginning (what we look at and keep near), by making the habit harder to do, and by
replacing the taste with a better one rather than starving it.

## D. Learning outcomes

By the end, participants should be able to:
1. Trace the Gita's chain from contemplation to compulsion (BG 2.62–63) in one of their own habits.
2. Explain why "I didn't want to, but I did it anyway" is a real experience and not a moral failure (BG 3.36–37).
3. Name the three interventions (early cue, friction, replacement) and connect each to a verse (3.41, 2.67, 2.59).
4. Distinguish suppression from replacement, and say why the Gita prefers the second (2.59, 6.35).
5. Choose one habit, one cue to remove and one small replacement to try this week.

## E. Scriptural foundation

**Use:** BG 2.62–63 and 3.36–37 carry the talk (the loop and the compulsion). BG 3.41, 2.67 and 2.59 give the
three interventions. 6.35, 6.26 and 18.37–38 support the method. 3.43 is background.

**BG 3.36**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/3/36/
> "Arjuna said: O descendant of Vṛṣṇi, by what is one impelled to sinful acts, even unwillingly, as if engaged by force?"
- **Context:** End of chapter 3, after Krsna has said that even a man of knowledge acts according to his nature.
- **Teaching:** Arjuna asks why a person does wrong even when he does not want to, "as if engaged by force".
- **Purport point:** The living being is originally pure, but in contact with material nature "he acts in many
  sinful ways without hesitation, and sometimes even against his will." The purport calls the question "very
  sanguine", that is, well aimed.
- **Application (preacher's):** This is every habit-breaker's question, asked in the Gita itself. Read it and let
  the room recognise it. It removes the shame before the analysis starts.
- **Use:** in the talk (opening verse).

**BG 3.37**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/3/37/
> "It is lust only, Arjuna, which is born of contact with the material mode of passion and later transformed into wrath, and which is the all-devouring sinful enemy of this world."
- **Teaching:** The force Arjuna asked about is lust (*kama*), born of the mode of passion and later transformed
  into wrath; it is "the all-devouring sinful enemy of this world".
- **Purport point:** "when lust is unsatisfied, it turns into wrath; wrath is transformed into illusion, and
  illusion continues the material existence." The purport adds that desire is love of God "transformed into
  lust, as milk in contact with sour tamarind is transformed into yogurt", and that "when completely baffled by
  prolonged lustful activities, the living entities begin to inquire about their real position."
- **Application (preacher's):** Two useful lines for a VL1 room: the craving is a *transformed* form of
  something good (so it can be transformed back), and the frustration of a habit is often what makes a person
  start asking real questions. Many in the room are here because a habit defeated them.
- **Use:** in the talk.

**BG 2.62**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/2/62/
> "While contemplating the objects of the senses, a person develops attachment for them, and from such attachment lust develops, and from lust anger arises."
- **Context:** Chapter 2, Krsna describing how a person with controlled senses nevertheless falls.
- **Teaching:** The chain begins with *contemplating* an object: looking, dwelling, replaying. Attachment,
  craving and anger follow in order.
- **Purport point:** "The senses require real engagements, and if they are not engaged in the transcendental
  loving service of the Lord, they will certainly seek engagement in the service of materialism." Artificial
  repression fails, "for the slightest thought of sense pleasure will agitate him".
- **Application (preacher's):** The Gita puts the *cue* first. Clear's "make it invisible" is this verse in
  practice: what I do not contemplate, I do not crave. The purport's "The senses require real engagements" is
  the replacement principle: an idle sense will find its own employment.
- **Use:** in the talk (the spine).

**BG 2.63**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/2/63/
> "From anger, complete delusion arises, and from delusion bewilderment of memory. When memory is bewildered, intelligence is lost, and when intelligence is lost one falls down again into the material pool."
- **Teaching:** The chain ends in delusion, bewilderment of memory and loss of intelligence, and one falls down
  again into the material pool.
- **Purport point:** The purport explains that artificial renunciation is called *phalgu*, "less important";
  the devotee "knows how to use everything in the service of the Lord" and "does not become a victim of material
  consciousness", whereas for the artificial renunciant "a slight agitation of the mind pulls him down again".
- **Application (preacher's):** "I forgot I had decided to stop" is bewilderment of memory. The verse explains
  why the resolution made on Sunday is gone by Wednesday: willpower acts at the end of the chain, where
  intelligence is already weak.
- **Use:** in the talk.

**BG 2.67**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/2/67/
> "As a strong wind sweeps away a boat on the water, even one of the roaming senses on which the mind focuses can carry away a man's intelligence."
- **Teaching:** One unguarded sense is enough to carry the intelligence away.
- **Purport point:** "Unless all of the senses are engaged in the service of the Lord, even one of them engaged
  in sense gratification can deviate the devotee"; the purport points to Maharaja Ambarisa, who engaged every
  sense.
- **Application (preacher's):** One sense, one app, one shelf in the kitchen: the boat image is the case for
  **friction**. Do not fight the wind; get the boat out of it (move the phone, delete the app, do not buy the
  packet).
- **Use:** in the talk.

**BG 3.41**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/3/41/
> "Therefore, O Arjuna, best of the Bhāratas, in the very beginning curb this great symbol of sin [lust] by regulating the senses, and slay this destroyer of knowledge and self-realization."
- **Teaching:** Curb desire "in the very beginning" by regulating the senses.
- **Purport point:** "from any stage of life, or from the time of understanding its urgency, one can begin
  regulating the senses"; "even a late beginner can become a lover of God".
- **Application (preacher's):** "In the very beginning" is the timing instruction: intervene at the cue, not at
  the craving. The purport's "late beginner" line is for the person who thinks it is too late.
- **Use:** in the talk.

**BG 2.59**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/2/59/
> "Though the embodied soul may be restricted from sense enjoyment, the taste for sense objects remains. But, ceasing such engagements by experiencing a higher taste, he is fixed in consciousness."
- **Teaching:** Restriction leaves the taste intact; a higher taste ends the engagement.
- **Purport point:** Restriction by rules is "like restricting a diseased person from certain types of eatables.
  The patient, however, neither likes such restrictions nor loses his taste for eatables." Restrictions "are
  only good until one actually has a taste".
- **Application (preacher's):** This is why diets and "no-phone months" relapse: the taste is untouched. The
  verse asks us to *replace*, not only remove. At VL1 the higher taste is offered as an invitation: a better
  book, a better morning, a kirtan, ten minutes of mantra meditation; not as a demand.
- **Use:** in the talk (the replacement principle).

**BG 6.35**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/6/35/
> "…it is undoubtedly very difficult to curb the restless mind, but it is possible by suitable practice and by detachment."
- **Context:** Krsna's reply to Arjuna's "more difficult than controlling the wind" (6.34, on IYF's slide).
- **Purport point:** The practice named is hearing about Krsna: "The more one hears about Kṛṣṇa, the more one
  becomes enlightened and detached from everything that draws the mind away". The purport compares it to
  "curing a disease by expert treatment and appropriate diet".
- **Application (preacher's):** Krsna agrees it is hard, then says it is possible by *practice*, not by a single
  heroic act. A habit is undone the way it was made: by repetition.
- **Use:** in the talk.

**BG 6.26**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/6/26/
> "From wherever the mind wanders due to its flickering and unsteady nature, one must certainly withdraw it and bring it back under the control of the Self."
- **Purport point:** One who controls the mind is called *gosvami*; one controlled by it is *go-dasa*, "the
  servant of the senses".
- **Application (preacher's):** The verse expects wandering. The practice is "bring it back", again and again,
  without drama. Relapse is a wander, not a verdict.
- **Use:** in the talk, one line.

**BG 18.37–38**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/18/37/ · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/18/38/
> 18.37: "That which in the beginning may be just like poison but at the end is just like nectar…"
> 18.38: "That happiness which is derived from contact of the senses with their objects and which appears like nectar at first but poison at the end…"
- **Teaching:** Two kinds of happiness, distinguished by their timing: poison-then-nectar (goodness) and
  nectar-then-poison (passion).
- **Purport point (18.38):** "In the beginning this may be very pleasing to the senses, but at the end, or after
  some time, it becomes just like poison."
- **Application (preacher's):** Every bad habit is 18.38 and every good one is 18.37. This is the Gita's version
  of "immediate reward, delayed cost". It gives the audience a one-question test for any habit: *when* does it
  feel good?
- **Use:** in the talk (the test).

**BG 3.43**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/3/43/
> "…one should steady the mind by deliberate spiritual intelligence…"
- **Purport point:** "artificial attempts to control the senses by the so-called practice of yogic postures can
  never help a man toward spiritual life. He must be trained in Kṛṣṇa consciousness by higher intelligence."
- **Use:** background; the chapter's conclusion, for the preacher's own understanding.

## F. Philosophical explanation

1. **The principle.** Habit is the loop of BG 2.62–63 run many times: contemplation → attachment → desire →
   (frustration → anger) → loss of memory and judgement. The force Arjuna felt (3.36) is *kama* (3.37), a
   transformed form of the soul's natural love, which is why it is so strong and why it can be re-transformed.
   The Gita's method has three parts: intervene at the beginning (3.41), engage every sense so none carries the
   boat away (2.67), and replace the taste rather than starve it (2.59), through practice (6.35).
2. **Why it matters.** The audience has tried willpower and lost. The Gita explains the loss: the fight was at
   the wrong point. Knowing where the chain starts gives them back agency without shame.
3. **Connections.** The mind as friend or enemy and the chariot (VL1-16); anger as frustrated desire (VL1-17);
   the taste for a higher happiness (VL1-01, VL1-21). At VL3 the replacement becomes concrete: chanting, hearing,
   regulated life; at SPS the urges are tolerated as a discipline (NOI 1).
4. **Consequences.** A listener who takes this in stops asking "why am I so weak?" and asks "what is my cue, and
   what am I going to put in its place?" They move the phone, delete the app, keep the book on the pillow, and
   measure the habit by 18.37–38 rather than by how it feels tonight.
5. **Misunderstandings to prevent.**
   - **"Just decide and stick to it."** Decisions are made by the intelligence, which the chain destroys by its
     end (2.63). Decide, and then change the cue.
   - **"Suppress it; that's discipline."** Suppression leaves the taste (2.59) and is undone by "the slightest
     thought" (2.62 purport). Replacement is the Gita's discipline.
   - **"A relapse means I've failed."** The text expects wandering and prescribes "bring it back" (6.26). The
     purport to 3.41 says even a late beginner can succeed.
   - **"The Gita says desire is evil, so I should want nothing."** It says desire is love misdirected (3.37
     purport). The cure is redirection, not emptiness.
   - **"This method cures addiction."** It helps everyone; a clinical addiction also needs clinical help (K).
6. **Pitch for VL1.** Say "craving", "trigger", "loop", "replacement". Keep Sanskrit to *kama* (desire), defined
   once. Use the audience's own examples (phones, snacks, procrastination), not only intoxicants.

## H. Opening and engagement

**H.1 How to start (first 5 minutes)**
1. Greet, give your name, and say this is a talk about method, not about blame.
2. Write "Habit I decided to drop" on the board; do not ask anyone to say theirs aloud.
3. Ask H.2 question 1 by show of hands; thank the room.
4. Read BG 3.36 (below) slowly.
5. Give the transition line and begin the loop (segment 2).

**H.2 Opening questions** (about the room's own life)
1. Think of one thing you decided to stop and did anyway this month (phone, sweets, snoozing). Hands only.
2. At what moment did the habit start: when you saw it, or when you were already doing it?
3. What have you tried that worked even for a week?

- **Opening:** Read BG 3.36 slowly: "by what is one impelled to sinful acts, even unwillingly, as if engaged by
  force?" Then: "This is the greatest warrior of his age asking his closest friend: why do I do what I have
  decided not to do? If that is in the Gita, it is allowed to be in this room."
- **Relatable scenario (invented):** Rohan decides every Sunday night that this week he will sleep by eleven.
  Every night he picks up the phone "for five minutes" and it is one-thirty. He has decided this at least forty
  times. Is Rohan weak, or is something else going on?
- **Transition:** "Let's look at what the Gita says is going on, in four steps."
- **Why it matters to this audience:** Exams, careers and relationships are decided by what we do when nobody is
  watching, which is what habits are.

## I. Core teaching points

1. **The question is in the Gita.** "Why do I do what I don't want to?" (3.36). The answer is a force,
   desire, that works "as if by force" (3.37). *Transition:* how does that force operate?
2. **The loop.** Contemplate → attach → crave → compel → forget your own decision (2.62–63). *Example:* Rohan's
   phone: the screen in view (contemplation), the scroll (attachment), the "five minutes" (craving), one-thirty
   (loss of memory). *Transition:* where in that chain did he fight?
3. **Willpower fights at the wrong end.** The resolution lives in the intelligence; the chain ends by removing
   intelligence (2.63). *Example:* "I'll just check one thing" is the intelligence already going. *Transition:*
   so fight earlier.
4. **Intervene at the cue; raise the friction.** "In the very beginning" (3.41); one sense can carry the boat
   (2.67). *Example:* phone charges in the kitchen; app deleted; snacks not in the house. *Transition:* but the
   taste remains.
5. **Replace, don't starve.** Restriction leaves the taste; a higher taste ends it (2.59); the practice is
   hearing (6.35). *Example:* the book on the pillow, ten minutes of mantra meditation before bed, one morning
   programme a week. Start small. *Transition:* how do I know which habits to keep?
6. **The timing test.** Nectar-then-poison or poison-then-nectar (18.37–38). *Example:* sort your own habits.
   *Transition:* to one habit and one plan.

## J. Stories and illustrations

  *Connection:* the problem is ancient and admitted.
- **Maharaja Ambarisa** (named in the BG 2.67 purport; the story is in Srimad-Bhagavatam Canto 9, chapter 4, told fully in VL1-17). One line here:
  a king who engaged every sense in service and so could not be carried away. *Connection:* replacement, not

- **Contemporary (from *Atomic Habits*, labelled as the book's accounts):** Clear's retelling of Thorndike's cats

- **The hospital cafeteria** (Thorndike et al. 2012, O): bottled water placed at eye level, soda moved; sales

  *Connection:* "make it invisible / make it obvious" and BG 2.62.
- **Invented, labelled:** Rohan (H).
- **Traditional saying (origin uncertain):** "Watch your thoughts, they become words…" Fine as a saying; never

## K. Anticipated questions and objections

> **Before you teach:** some habits in the room are addictions: alcohol, drugs, gambling, compulsive
> pornography, self-harm. The method here helps everyone, and an addiction also needs professional help, which
> is fully compatible with spiritual life. Say once, plainly: "If a habit is damaging your health, money,
> studies or relationships and you have tried seriously and repeatedly to stop, please talk to a doctor or
> counsellor, and tell someone you trust. Tele-MANAS, the Government of India's free 24×7 helpline, is 14416."
> *(Pastoral guidance, not a scriptural claim.)* Never name a person's habit publicly; talk afterwards.

1. **"Isn't a bad habit just weakness of character?"**
   - Arjuna, the Gita's hero, asks why he is impelled "as if engaged by force" (3.36) and is not called weak; he
     is given a mechanism (3.37, 2.62–63). Character is what you do with the mechanism once you know it.
2. **"I've tried everything. Nothing works."**
   - Most "everything" is willpower at the end of the chain (2.63). Ask: have you changed the *cue* (3.41), the
     *friction* (2.67) and the *replacement* (2.59)? Usually one of the three is missing, most often the third.
3. **"If I remove the trigger I'm just avoiding the problem."**
   - The Gita says to curb desire "in the very beginning" by regulating the senses (3.41). Not walking into the
     wind is not cowardice; it is seamanship (2.67).
4. **"Why 'higher taste'? Can't I just stop?"**
   - You can stop the action; the taste remains (2.59) and "the slightest thought" revives it (2.62 purport).
     A replacement is not optional. It need not be religious to begin with: a sport, a book, a morning walk;
     the Gita's own candidate is hearing (6.35 purport), which you are welcome to sample.
5. **"I relapsed. Does that undo everything?"**
   - No. The text expects the mind to wander and prescribes "bring it back" (6.26). The purport to 3.41 says
     even a late beginner can succeed. Count the days you kept, not only the day you broke.
6. **"Does chanting cure addiction?"**
   - Hearing and chanting are a replacement taste and a practice (6.35, 2.59); for an addiction they support
     treatment, they do not replace it. Taking treatment is itself a good habit.
7. **"Is *Atomic Habits* a spiritual book?"**
   - No, and it does not claim to be. It describes the loop well and gives good tactics. The Gita gives the
     diagnosis and the goal; the book is a useful toolbox. Keep the two in their places.

## L. Case studies

*Invented cases for discussion. Not real incidents.*

**Case 1: Rohan's phone.** Rohan has decided forty Sunday nights to sleep by eleven. The phone is on his
pillow; the first thing he sees in bed is the lock screen.
- **Dilemma:** He blames his willpower. Is that the right diagnosis?
- **Principles:** BG 2.62 (contemplation first); 3.41 (in the very beginning); 2.67 (one sense, the boat);
  2.59 (replacement).
- **Questions:** Where is Rohan's cue? What would "make it invisible" mean for him? What goes on the pillow
  instead?
- **Suggested analysis:** The phone charges in the kitchen; a book or a small set of beads is on the pillow;
  the first ten minutes in bed have a replacement. Willpower is needed only once, at eight p.m.
- **Takeaway:** Change the cue and the environment; use will at the beginning of the chain.

**Case 2: Priya's snack drawer.** Priya eats chips every evening while studying and feels sluggish and guilty.
She has tried "no junk food" three times.
- **Principles:** BG 18.38 (nectar first, poison later); 2.59 (the taste remains); 6.35 (practice, small steps).
- **Questions:** What does the chip drawer provide that the study session lacks? What replacement would meet
  the same need? What is the smallest first step?
- **Takeaway:** Identify the craving behind the habit, replace it (fruit, a short walk, a cup of something
  warm), and start smaller than feels worthwhile.

## M. Discussion and reflection

**M.1 Closing check questions** (easy first; praise every try)
1. Is a bad habit a sign of a weak character, according to the talk? *No: the Gita gives a mechanism, not a scolding (3.36–37).*
2. Where does the chain of 2.62–63 start? *With contemplating (looking at, dwelling on) the object.*
3. Which verse says to curb desire "in the very beginning"? *BG 3.41.*
4. What does 2.59 say about only restricting? *The taste remains; a higher taste ends it.*
5. A habit feels great tonight and bad tomorrow: nectar-first or poison-first? *Poison later, so the bad kind (18.38).*
6. When is outside help needed? *When a habit is an addiction: professional help and this method go together.*

**M.2 Activity (about 10 minutes): "Cue, friction, replacement"**
1. Each person writes on a card, privately: my habit · its cue · what I will do to the cue · what I will do instead (3 min).
2. In pairs, each reads only the last two lines aloud, or just "my cue is a place/time/object" (3 min).
3. Volunteers share one cue they will move (2 min); thank every volunteer.
4. Close with BG 3.41: the beginning is where we win (2 min). Nobody has to say what the habit is.

**M.3 Reflection**
- **Reflection (private):** Pick one habit. Where is its cue? When does it feel good: at the start or at the end (18.37–38)?
- **Application:** Write one line: "This week I will move/remove ___ and put ___ in its place." *(Nobody needs to share what the habit is.)*

# Part 2: For students

## S1. Summary

Everybody has a habit they have decided to drop and could not. The Bhagavad-gita (an ancient Indian text of a conversation on a battlefield) takes this seriously: its hero, Arjuna, asks why a person does wrong "even unwillingly, as if engaged by force" (BG 3.36). Krsna's answer is that a force called *kama* (desire) works in a chain: we look at something, we get attached, attachment becomes craving, and by the end we have forgotten our own decision (BG 2.62–63). That is why willpower loses: it fights at the end of the chain, where our judgement is already gone.

The Gita's way is different, in three steps. First, fight at the beginning, by changing what you look at and keep near you (BG 3.41). Second, make the habit harder to do; one unguarded sense is enough to carry a boat away (BG 2.67). Third, replace the taste instead of only banning it, because a ban leaves the taste intact and only a higher taste ends it (BG 2.59).

This takes practice, not a single decision (BG 6.35), and when the mind wanders, you bring it back (BG 6.26). A simple test tells you which habits to keep: good habits feel like poison first and nectar later; bad ones feel like nectar first and poison later (BG 18.37–38). If a habit is an addiction, use this method and also get professional help.

## S2. Key ideas

- "Why do I do what I decided not to do?" is a question in the Gita itself (BG 3.36). You are not weak; there
  is a mechanism.
- The chain: look → attach → crave → act → forget your decision (BG 2.62–63).
- Willpower fights at the wrong end. Change the cue instead (BG 3.41).
- One unguarded sense can carry you away; add friction (BG 2.67).
- Banning a habit leaves the taste. Replace it with something better (BG 2.59).
- Practice, not a single heroic act (BG 6.35). Relapse means "bring it back" (BG 6.26).
- Test: nectar first, poison later = break it; poison first, nectar later = build it (BG 18.37–38).

## S3. Verses to remember

- **BG 2.62:** "While contemplating the objects of the senses, a person develops attachment for them, and from
  such attachment lust develops…" *For me:* the habit starts with what I look at. Change that first.
- **BG 2.59:** "Though the embodied soul may be restricted from sense enjoyment, the taste for sense objects
  remains. But, ceasing such engagements by experiencing a higher taste, he is fixed in consciousness."
  *For me:* don't only remove; replace.
- **BG 6.35:** "…it is undoubtedly very difficult to curb the restless mind, but it is possible by suitable
  practice and by detachment." *For me:* hard, and possible, by practice.

## S4. Words to know

- **Kama:** desire, craving; the force behind a habit (BG 3.37).
- **Cue (trigger):** what starts the loop: the phone on the pillow, the open drawer.
- **Higher taste:** something better that replaces the habit, so the taste for the old thing fades (BG 2.59).

## S5. Questions and answers

1. **Isn't a bad habit just weak character?**
   The Gita's hero asks why he is impelled "as if engaged by force" and is given a mechanism, not a scolding
   (BG 3.36–37). Character is what you do once you know the mechanism.
2. **I've tried everything.**
   Most "everything" is willpower at the end of the chain. Have you changed the cue, added friction and put a
   replacement in place? Usually the replacement is missing.
3. **Isn't removing the trigger just avoiding the problem?**
   The Gita says to curb desire "in the very beginning" (BG 3.41). Staying out of the wind is good seamanship,
   not cowardice (BG 2.67).
4. **Can't I just stop, without a replacement?**
   You can stop the action; the taste remains (BG 2.59). Put something better in the slot: a book, a walk,
   ten minutes of mantra meditation, whatever you are willing to try.
5. **I relapsed. Is it all undone?**
   No. The mind wanders; you bring it back (BG 6.26). Count the days you kept.
6. **What if my habit is an addiction?**
   Then this method helps, and professional help is also needed; the two go together. Talk to a doctor or
   counsellor and to someone you trust. Tele-MANAS (free, 24×7): 14416.

## S6. Check your understanding

1. Put these in the Gita's order (BG 2.62–63): craving · contemplating · attachment · loss of memory.
2. True or false: BG 2.59 says that if you restrict yourself long enough, the taste disappears.
3. Which verse says to curb desire "in the very beginning"? (a) BG 2.67 (b) BG 3.41 (c) BG 6.26
4. A habit feels great tonight and bad tomorrow. Which verse describes it, 18.37 or 18.38?
5. In your own words: why does willpower alone usually fail?

**Answers:** 1. contemplating → attachment → craving → loss of memory (BG 2.62–63). 2. False: the taste
remains; only a higher taste ends the engagement (BG 2.59). 3. (b) BG 3.41. 4. BG 18.38 (nectar first, poison
later). 5. Because it acts at the end of the chain, where intelligence is already lost (BG 2.63); change the
cue instead (BG 3.41).
