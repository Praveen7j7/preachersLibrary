# Quest for happiness: knowing the goal of life before defining the way of life

> **VL2 · 180 min · review: sources-checked.** IYF's subtitle states the angle: *What is our goal? Are we achieving it? How can we achieve
> it?* **No IYF notes were available** for this topic (no folder on the site), so the page is built from the Gita and Bhagavatam
> and the VL2 method. The head should check the emphasis.

## B. Why this topic belongs at this level

**Builds on [dys-02](dys-02.md) (DYS 5, Substance and shadow):** that session gave the images (the reflected tree, the limbs and the stomach, the toy kitchen) and the answer that the soul is satisfied by serving the whole. This page does not repeat them or the "phone that gives a week of joy" example; it takes up the Gita's analysis of why sense happiness cannot last (BG 5.22, 18.36-39), argued with experience, and how to define the way of life after the goal.


VL1 offers the Gita as something worth exploring; VL2 asks the listener to reason. The question "what am I actually for?" is the
most reasonable place to start, because every listener already has an answer in practice (their way of life) and few have
examined it. The Gita's analysis of happiness (three kinds, 18.36–39; the chain of desire, 2.62–63; the higher taste, 2.59) is
philosophy argued with experience, which is exactly the VL2 method. Respectful debate: the hedonist's case is given its strength (K).

**IYF's VL2 frame** (IYF's own documents, `research/batch-criteria.md` §2 and §8 point 2): course name "Technical Science
for Spiritual Value", mood *questioning, reasoned*; motive "developing strong faith in practicing spirituality" and "removing all
misconceptions about ISKCON and spirituality"; KRAs "Start doing chanting", "Coming to regular classes", "Meeting with
facilitators". The 2015 VL2 lesson plan runs DYS 1–6 first and then the EBG study-sessions; IYF's own "Happiness Module" decks and the Encyclopedia's IYF-book chapter "The Quest for Happiness" belong to this topic (O). Practice is
expected to *begin* during VL2 (by its end: 2–4 rounds, 15 min of Prabhupada's books and 15–30 min of kirtan hearing a day), so
this page may carry a first practical step without breaking IYF's level (P).

**Not done here:**
- Condemning ambition, pleasure or relationships. The Gita's argument is about *where they are placed*, not that they are wrong.
- Promising that spiritual practice makes life painless. BG 2.14 says the opposite: happiness and distress come and go and are to be tolerated.
- Psychology as proof of theology. One study is cited (O) to illustrate adaptation; it proves nothing about the soul.

## C. Central message

Everyone is seeking happiness that does not end; most of us choose a way of life before asking whether it can deliver that. The Gita
shows why happiness from sense contact cannot last (it has "a beginning and an end", BG 5.22; nectar first, poison later, 18.38) and
describes a happiness that grows (poison first, nectar later, 18.37; "boundless transcendental happiness", 6.20–23). So first know
the goal; then define the way of life (SB 1.2.10).

## D. Learning outcomes

By the end, participants should be able to:
1. State, with examples, the difference between a goal and a way of life, and show that most people choose the second before examining the first.
2. Explain the Gita's three kinds of happiness (BG 18.36–39) and classify three everyday pleasures by them.
3. Give the Gita's reasons why sense happiness does not last (5.22; 18.38; the chain of 2.62–63) and the role of adaptation in experience.
4. Describe the happiness the Gita proposes (6.20–23; 5.21; 2.70) and the means it names (a higher taste, 2.59; inquiry, SB 1.2.10).
5. Draw one reasoned conclusion about their own way of life and choose one test for it.

## E. Scriptural foundation

**Use:** BG 5.22, 18.36–39, 2.59, 6.20–23 and SB 1.2.10 carry the talk.

**BG 5.22**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/5/22/
> "An intelligent person does not take part in the sources of misery, which are due to contact with the material senses. O son of Kuntī, such pleasures have a beginning and an end, and so the wise man does not delight in them."
- **Context:** The chapter on action in Krsna consciousness; the liberated person's inner satisfaction (5.21) is being described.
- **Teaching:** Pleasures from sense contact have a beginning and an end; the wise do not make them their delight.
- **Purport point:** Sense pleasures are temporary "because the body itself is temporary"; the purport quotes SB 5.5.1 (the human form
  is not for labouring after pleasures "available to the stool-eaters") and adds: "The more one is addicted to material pleasures, the
  more he is entrapped by material miseries."
- **Application (preacher's):** The verse's argument is structural, not moralising: whatever begins ends, so it cannot be the *goal*.
- **Use:** in the talk; the key verse.

**BG 18.36, 18.37, 18.38, 18.39**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/18/36/ (and following)
> 18.37: "That which in the beginning may be just like poison but at the end is just like nectar and which awakens one to self-realization is said to be happiness in the mode of goodness."
> 18.38: "That happiness which is derived from contact of the senses with their objects and which appears like nectar at first but poison at the end is said to be of the nature of passion."
> 18.39: "And that happiness which is blind to self-realization, which is delusion from beginning to end and which arises from sleep, laziness and illusion is said to be of the nature of ignorance."
- **Context:** Krsna's analysis of knowledge, action, the doer, understanding, determination and happiness by the three modes.
- **Teaching:** Happiness comes in three kinds, distinguished by their beginning, their end and their relation to self-realisation.
- **Purport points:** 18.36: the conditioned soul "chews the chewed" until good association shows him the repetition. 18.37: the
  regulations of self-realisation are "bitter like poison" at first; one who persists "begins to drink real nectar." 18.38: the
  young couple drawn together by the senses; "at the end… it becomes just like poison." 18.39: for the person in ignorance "there is
  no happiness either in the beginning or at the end."
- **Application (preacher's):** This is the whole topic's diagnostic tool: ask of any pleasure, "what is its beginning, what is its
  end, and does it wake me up or put me to sleep?"
- **Use:** in the talk; the table in N.

**BG 2.62, BG 2.63**: Verified. Contemplation → attachment → lust → anger (2.62); anger → delusion → bewildered memory → lost
intelligence → fall (2.63). Purport to 2.62: the senses "require real engagements"; artificial repression fails; a higher taste succeeds.
**Use:** in the talk; why the chase accelerates.

**BG 2.59**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/2/59/
> "Though the embodied soul may be restricted from sense enjoyment, the taste for sense objects remains. But, ceasing such engagements by experiencing a higher taste, he is fixed in consciousness."
- **Purport point:** Restriction alone is like a diet a patient resents; "one who has tasted the beauty of the Supreme Lord Kṛṣṇa… no
  longer has a taste for dead, material things."
- **Application (preacher's):** The Gita's proposal is not suppression but replacement by something better. This answers the
  hedonist's fair objection (K.1).
- **Use:** in the talk.

**BG 6.20-23**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/6/20-23/
> "…In that joyous state, one is situated in boundless transcendental happiness, realized through transcendental senses… Being situated in such a position, one is never shaken, even in the midst of greatest difficulty. This indeed is actual freedom from all miseries arising from material contact."
- **Teaching:** There is a happiness realised through the self, not the senses; established in it, one is not shaken by difficulty.
- **Purport point:** Transcendental pleasure "realized through transcendental senses" is accepted even by Patanjali's system; the
  purport ties it to Lord Caitanya's "clearance of the impure mirror of the mind."
- **Use:** in the talk; the positive description of the goal.

**BG 5.21, BG 2.70**: Verified. 5.21: the self-realised person "enjoys unlimited happiness, for he concentrates on the Supreme."
2.70: peace belongs to one who, like the ocean filled by rivers, is not disturbed by the inflow of desires, "and not the man who
strives to satisfy such desires." Purport to 2.70: "As long as one has the material body, the demands of the body for sense
gratification will continue. The devotee, however, is not disturbed by such desires, because of his fullness." **Use:** in the talk.

**BG 2.14**: Verified. Happiness and distress come and go like seasons; "one must learn to tolerate them." **Use:** guard against
promising painlessness.

**BG 16.13-15**: Verified. The demoniac mind: "I am the lord of everything. I am the enjoyer. I am perfect, powerful and happy…"
"deluded by ignorance." **Use:** the portrait of a way of life built on the unexamined goal; read it without mocking anyone.

**SB 1.2.10**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/sb/1/2/10/
> "Life's desires should never be directed toward sense gratification. One should desire only a healthy life, or self-preservation, since a human being is meant for inquiry about the Absolute Truth. Nothing else should be the goal of one's works."
- **Purport point:** "In every sphere of life, therefore, the ultimate end must be seeking after the Absolute Truth, and that sort of
  engagement will make one happy because he will be less engaged in varieties of sense gratification."
- **Application (preacher's):** The verse states the order the title asks for: the goal (inquiry into the Absolute Truth) first; the
  way of life (maintenance of the body) in its service.
- **Use:** in the talk.

**SB 5.5.1, SB 7.6.1, SB 1.2.6**: Verified. 5.5.1: the human form is not for struggling after what "dogs and hogs" also get; austerity
purifies and leads to "eternal, blissful life"; purport: the commuter who works all day for the evening's pleasures. 7.6.1: Prahlada:
use the human form "from the very beginning of life" for devotional service; "even a slight amount of sincere devotional service can
give one complete perfection." 1.2.6: the supreme dharma is unmotivated, uninterrupted devotional service, which alone "completely
satisf[ies] the self"; purport: "temporary cessation of misery is falsely called happiness." **Use:** 1.2.6 in the talk (the goal
defined); the others for questions.

**SB 11.2.42**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/sb/11/2/42/ · *BBT translation, completed by Srila Prabhupada's disciples.*
> "Devotion, direct experience of the Supreme Lord, and detachment from other things — these three occur simultaneously… in the same way that pleasure, nourishment and relief from hunger come simultaneously and increasingly, with each bite, for a person engaged in eating."
- **Purport point:** The eater "increasingly becomes uninterested in the food itself" as he is satisfied, but attachment to Krsna
  "increases at every moment"; this is why the Lord's qualities are not material.
- **Application (preacher's):** The one description in scripture of a happiness that grows with each helping instead of fading.
  Use it as the tradition's claim about the goal; the listener can only test it by practice.
- **Use:** in the talk, near the end.

## F. Philosophical explanation

1. **Goal vs way of life.** A goal is what one finally wants; a way of life is the arrangement adopted to get it. Nearly everyone
   adopts the way of life first (degree, job, city, partner) and infers the goal from it afterwards, if at all. The title's claim is
   that this is backwards (SB 1.2.10). Ask the room: "If your plan works perfectly, what will you have?" Keep asking "and then?" until
   the answer is some form of "I'll be happy and it won't end." That is the goal everyone shares.
2. **Why the usual ways cannot reach it.** Three Gita arguments, each checkable in experience.
   - *Structure:* pleasures from sense contact have "a beginning and an end" (5.22); whatever begins ends; a goal that ends is not a goal.
   - *Trajectory:* sense happiness is nectar first and poison later (18.38); its opposite, the happiness of goodness, is poison first
     and nectar later (18.37). Everyone can name examples of both.
   - *Escalation:* contemplation breeds attachment, attachment lust, lust anger, and the chain runs to the loss of intelligence
     (2.62–63). The chase does not stay still; it accelerates. Psychology's "adaptation" finding (O) illustrates the same pattern
     without proving anything about the soul.
3. **The three kinds of happiness (18.36–39).** Ignorance: delusion from beginning to end (sleep, laziness, intoxication).
   Passion: nectar then poison (sense contact). Goodness: poison then nectar, awakening to self-realisation (discipline, study,
   service). Beyond the modes: "boundless transcendental happiness, realized through transcendental senses" (6.20–23), the
   happiness of the self concentrated on the Supreme (5.21). The diagnostic: beginning, end, awake or asleep.
4. **The hedonist's case, fairly.** "Pleasure is the only happiness we know; the rest is wishful thinking; enjoy while you can." Its
   strength: it is honest about experience and refuses promises. The Gita's reply is not "pleasure is sin" but "pleasure cannot be the
   goal, because of 1–3; and there is a higher taste you have not yet tasted" (2.59). That is a claim with a test attached (P).
5. **The Gita's proposal.** Not suppression: "the taste for sense objects remains" under restriction (2.59). Replacement: a higher taste
   that makes the lower fall away by itself. Its positive description: a fullness that is not disturbed by the inflow of desires, as the
   ocean is not disturbed by rivers (2.70); and, in the Bhagavatam, a happiness that grows with each helping (SB 11.2.42). The means
   named: inquiry (SB 1.2.10), austerity that purifies (SB 5.5.1), devotional service begun early (SB 7.6.1), unmotivated and
   uninterrupted (SB 1.2.6). At VL2, name the means and point to VL3 for the practice.
6. **Redefining the way of life.** Once the goal is known, the way of life is not abandoned but *ordered*: the body maintained for the
   inquiry (SB 1.2.10); work, study and relationships kept, and placed. BG 2.14 keeps it honest: the seasons of happiness and distress
   continue; the change is in what one is *for*.
7. **Misunderstandings to prevent.**
   - "The Gita is against enjoyment." It is against mistaking the shadow for the goal (DYS 5); it describes the greatest enjoyment (6.20–23).
   - "Goodness happiness means being miserable now for a reward later." 18.37 says the discipline *becomes* nectar; the purport: "he
     begins to drink real nectar, and he enjoys life."
   - "Spiritual people are never unhappy." BG 2.14.
   - "Psychology has proved the Gita." No; one study illustrates adaptation (O). Keep empirical and theological claims apart.
8. **Pitch for VL2.** Terms defined (modes; "higher taste"). The argument runs from the listener's own experience to the verses, not
   the other way round. One reasoned conclusion to test.

## H. Opening and engagement

**H.1 How to start (first 5 minutes)**
1. Welcome people by name; recall last session in one line: "what we chase is a reflection".
2. Ask H.2 question 1; follow the chain "and then?" up to three steps.
3. Write on the board the word the room ends on, usually "happy" or "peace".
4. Say: "Tonight we ask why that happiness slips away, and what kind would not."
5. Ask people to keep one example from their own week in mind; they will not be asked to share it.

**Adapting for your audience:** the "and then?" chain works for a farmer (harvest, price, a pucca house?), a student (exam, job, salary) or an executive (promotion, board seat); use the one nearest your group.

**H.2 Opening questions**
- Suppose everything you are working for comes true. What will you have? And then what?
- Think of a goal you reached. How did you feel a month later?
- What is something you enjoy that gets better the longer you do it?

**More on opening and engagement**


- **Opening question:** "Suppose everything you are working for comes true. What will you have? And then what?" Follow the chain
  until it stops at "lasting happiness".
- **Relatable scenario (invented):** A graduate lands the job she planned for since school. At the celebration she catches herself
  already planning the next move, and feels the strange flatness she had felt after the entrance exam, and after the degree.
- **Transition:** "The Gita has a precise account of that flatness, and of what would not go flat."
- **Why it matters to this audience:** the choice of way of life is being made now, in these years, usually without examining the goal.

## I. Core teaching points

1. **Everyone's goal is lasting happiness; most choose a way of life first.** SB 1.2.10. *Example:* the "and then?" chain. *Transition:* can the usual ways reach it?
2. **Sense happiness has a beginning and an end.** BG 5.22; 18.38. *Example:* the celebration that goes flat. *Transition:* and it does not stay still.
3. **The chase escalates.** BG 2.62–63; adaptation (O). *Example:* the next target after the one just met (an athlete who breaks a record and is asked about the next). *Transition:* so is there another kind?
4. **Three kinds, and one beyond.** BG 18.36–39; 6.20–23. *Example:* learning an instrument (poison then nectar). *Transition:* the hedonist objects.
5. **Not suppression, a higher taste.** BG 2.59; 2.70; SB 11.2.42. *Transition:* so define the way of life after the goal (SB 1.2.10; 1.2.6).

## J. Stories and illustrations

- **Yamunacarya's confession** (quoted in the purport to BG 5.21): since tasting service to Krsna, "whenever I think of sex pleasure
  I spit at the thought." Use with care and only to illustrate 2.59 (the higher taste replaces the lower); do not use it to shame.
- **The commuter** (SB 5.5.1 purport): the man packed into the local train for an hour each way to earn the evening's eating, drinking
  and sleeping. Prabhupada's own image of a way of life chosen without a goal.
- **The ocean and the rivers** (BG 2.70). Fullness that is not disturbed by inflow.
- **The meal** (SB 11.2.42): pleasure, nourishment and relief from hunger with every bite; the one image of happiness that increases.
- **Contemporary images (the preacher's, not evidence):** the celebration that goes flat; the instrument that is painful to practise and
  a joy to play.

## K. Anticipated questions and objections

1. **"Pleasure is the only happiness there is. The rest is wishful thinking."**
   - Give it its due: it is honest about experience. Then: the Gita does not deny the pleasure; it denies that something with a
     beginning and an end can be the *goal* (5.22), and it claims a higher taste exists (2.59). The claim has a test: try the practice
     for a month and compare. Wishful thinking does not offer tests.
2. **"Isn't this just delayed gratification: suffer now, heaven later?"**
   - 18.37 says the nectar begins when the discipline matures, in this life; the purport: "he begins to drink real nectar, and he enjoys
     life." And 6.20–23 describes the happiness as present, not posthumous.
3. **"My relationships make me happy. Are you calling them poison?"**
   - 18.38 describes happiness that *depends on sense contact* as nectar-then-poison; relationships held as the goal do end and do
     hurt; relationships held within a larger goal are not condemned anywhere in the Gita. The question is placement, not abolition.
4. **"Ambition drives progress. Should I stop striving?"**
   - SB 1.2.10 says maintain life and direct desire to the goal; it does not say stop working. BG 2.70 contrasts the one who is full with
     the one who "strives to satisfy such desires": the difference is the inner state, not the activity.
5. **"Spiritual people I know are not obviously happier."**
   - Fair. BG 2.14: the seasons continue for everyone. The Gita's claim is about what one is anchored to (6.20–23: "never shaken") and
     about a taste that grows (SB 11.2.42), not about a permanent smile. Judge the claim by the practice, not by any one person.
6. **"Doesn't psychology say happiness is just brain chemistry and set-points?"**
   - Psychology describes adaptation well (O); it does not settle what the self is or what it is for. Use the finding as an
     illustration of 18.38, never as proof of theology.

## L. Case studies

*Invented cases for discussion. Not real incidents.*

**Case 1: The spreadsheet.** Kunal has planned his life in a spreadsheet to age forty: salary bands, a flat, a car, marriage at
twenty-nine. Asked what the spreadsheet is for, he is silent, then says "to be comfortable", then "to be happy".
- **Principles:** goal vs way of life (SB 1.2.10); BG 5.22; BG 16.13–15 read gently.
- **Questions:** Which cells are ways and which is the goal? Does any cell deliver something that does not end? What one row would
  change if the goal came first?
- **Takeaway:** A plan without an examined goal is a way of life waiting for a reason.

**Case 2: The instrument.** Fatima took up the violin as an adult. For four months every practice was frustration; now an hour passes
unnoticed and she is happier after it than before.
- **Principles:** BG 18.37 (poison then nectar); 2.59 (the higher taste).
- **Questions:** Which kind of happiness is this by the Gita's table? What did it cost at the start? What in her life has the opposite
  curve (18.38)? What would the Gita's "higher taste" look like if it followed the same pattern?
- **Takeaway:** The happiness that grows begins with discipline; the Gita claims the highest instance of this pattern.

## M. Discussion and reflection

**M.1 Closing check questions** (easy first)
1. What is the difference between a goal and a way of life? *Expected:* the goal is what I finally want; the way of life is the arrangement I adopt to get it.
2. Why does the wise person not make sense pleasure the goal (BG 5.22)? *Expected:* it has a beginning and an end.
3. Name the three kinds of happiness (BG 18.36-39). *Expected:* ignorance, passion (nectar then poison), goodness (poison then nectar).
4. What does BG 2.59 propose instead of suppression? *Expected:* a higher taste.
5. Does the Gita promise that a spiritual person never feels distress (BG 2.14)? *Expected:* No; happiness and distress come and go like seasons.
6. What does SB 1.2.10 say life is for? *Expected:* inquiry into the Absolute Truth, with life maintained. Praise honest answers.

**M.2 Activities (engagement block, about 90 min; choose two)**
- **A. Inventory sort (35 min).** Everyone writes five recent pleasures on slips without names (5 min); groups sort them by beginning, end and whether they leave you awake or asleep (15 min); a spokesperson reads one pile (10 min); the preacher links to N (5 min).
- **B. Two-column debate (30 min).** Groups prepare the strongest case for "pleasure is the goal" and for "a higher taste exists" (10 min), present 3 minutes each, then restate the other side before replying (15 min); the preacher resolves with 2.59 (5 min).
- **C. Goal-and-calendar audit (25 min).** Individually write one line stating your goal and mark in last week's calendar what served it (10 min); pairs ask one question each, "what would change?" (10 min); optional sharing (5 min). Nothing private is asked.

**M.3 Reflection (private, optional).** Which of my daily pleasures are nectar-then-poison, and which poison-then-nectar? Which did I choose, and which chose me? One line stating my actual goal; one change in my way of life that follows from it.

# Part 2: For students

## S1. Summary

Ask "and then what?" of any plan long enough and you reach the same answer: "I want to be happy, and I want it to last." That is everyone's goal. Most of us, though, choose a way of life (degree, job, city, partner) before examining the goal. The Bhagavad-gita (an ancient Sanskrit scripture) examines it, and DYS 5 has already offered the picture of reflection and substance.

Pleasure from sense contact has "a beginning and an end" (BG 5.22) and, when it is of the kind called passion, runs from nectar to poison (BG 18.38); the chase also escalates, from thinking about an object to attachment, lust, anger and loss of judgement (BG 2.62-63). There is another kind: poison first and nectar later, which awakens us (BG 18.37), and beyond that a "boundless" happiness through the self that is "never shaken" (BG 6.20-23).

The Gita's method is not suppression but a higher taste (BG 2.59): like a meal that nourishes with every bite, it grows (SB 11.2.42). This does not promise a painless life: happiness and distress come and go like seasons (BG 2.14). So know the goal first; then define the way of life (SB 1.2.10), keeping life and work going while changing what you are for.

## S2. Key ideas

- Everyone's real goal is happiness that does not end; most choose a way of life before examining it.
- Whatever has a beginning has an end, so it cannot be the goal (BG 5.22).
- Three kinds of happiness: ignorance (delusion throughout), passion (nectar then poison), goodness (poison then nectar); and one beyond the modes (BG 18.36–39; 6.20–23).
- The chase escalates: contemplation → attachment → lust → anger (BG 2.62–63).
- The Gita proposes a higher taste, not suppression (BG 2.59).
- Goal first, then way of life (SB 1.2.10).

## S3. Verses to remember

- **BG 5.22:** Pleasures from sense contact "have a beginning and an end, and so the wise man does not delight in them." *For me:* what ends cannot be my goal.
- **BG 18.37:** "That which in the beginning may be just like poison but at the end is just like nectar… is said to be happiness in the mode of goodness." *For me:* the best things start hard.
- **BG 2.59:** "…ceasing such engagements by experiencing a higher taste, he is fixed in consciousness." *For me:* replace, don't just resist.

## S4. Words to know

- **Goal vs way of life:** what I finally want vs the arrangement I adopt to get it.
- **Modes of nature (gunas):** goodness, passion, ignorance; each has its own kind of happiness (BG 18.36–39).
- **Higher taste:** a happiness that makes lower pleasures fall away by themselves (BG 2.59).
- **Inquiry into the Absolute Truth:** the purpose of human life according to SB 1.2.10.

## S5. Questions and answers

1. **Isn't pleasure the only happiness there is?** It is real, but it begins and ends (BG 5.22). The Gita claims a higher taste exists (2.59) and gives you a way to test it.
2. **Is this "suffer now, heaven later"?** No. BG 18.37 says the discipline itself turns to nectar, in this life.
3. **Are my relationships poison?** No. The Gita speaks of happiness that depends on sense contact. Relationships held within a larger goal are not condemned.
4. **Should I stop being ambitious?** No. Maintain life and work (SB 1.2.10); the change is in what you are for, not in whether you act.
5. **Spiritual people don't look happier.** Happiness and distress come and go for everyone (BG 2.14). The claim is about what you are anchored to, and a taste that grows (SB 11.2.42). Test the practice, not a person.

## S6. Check your understanding

1. According to BG 5.22, why does the wise person not make sense pleasure the goal?
2. Match: nectar-then-poison / poison-then-nectar / delusion throughout → passion / goodness / ignorance (BG 18.37–39).
3. What does BG 2.59 propose instead of forced restriction?
4. In BG 2.62–63, what follows from contemplating the objects of the senses?
5. According to SB 1.2.10, what is a human being meant for?
6. True or false: the Gita promises that a spiritual person will never feel distress.

**Answers:** 1. Because such pleasures "have a beginning and an end" (BG 5.22). 2. Passion = nectar then poison (18.38); goodness = poison then nectar (18.37);
ignorance = delusion throughout (18.39). 3. A higher taste (BG 2.59). 4. Attachment, then lust, then anger, then delusion, loss of memory and intelligence,
and a fall (BG 2.62–63). 5. Inquiry about the Absolute Truth (SB 1.2.10). 6. False: happiness and distress come and go like seasons (BG 2.14).
