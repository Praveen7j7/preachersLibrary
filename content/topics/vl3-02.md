# 4 Regulative Principles of Freedom

> **VL3 · 180 min · review: senior-review · sensitive: true** (the fourth principle concerns sexuality; the IYF notes compare scriptures of
> other faiths, which this page does not do). The title is the Gita's own phrase: "regulative principles of freedom" (BG 2.64). IYF's
> material (`research/iyf-materials/vl3-02/`) is a long handout *Four Regulative Principles* and a deck *Meat Eating*; the handout's

> are expected to be following all four by the end (batch-criteria §3).

## B. Why this topic belongs at this level

VL2 introduces the principles as do's and don'ts of a practice people are trying out (batch-criteria §8, row 2). At VL3 the
participants are *living* them, and the questions change: "Why no eggs?", "My family cooks meat: what do I do?", "Is tea
intoxication?", "What exactly is illicit?", "I slipped: have I lost everything?" The page therefore teaches the principles from
their roots (the four legs of religion in SB 1.17) rather than from their fences, and it gives each principle a positive name: what
it frees. Freedom is the right frame at VL3 for two reasons the texts give: the Gita's own phrase (2.64), and SB 11.5.11, which says the
scriptures that *permit* sex, meat and wine in regulated forms do so "for the ultimate purpose of renunciation", that is, to lead
people out, not to keep them in.

Builds on VL3-01: higher taste (BG 2.59) and the six urges are taught there; here each is used for one point only (the principles hold by taste, not force). Do not re-teach VL3-01's routine audit.

## C. Central message

The four principles are not four fences but four freedoms (BG 2.64): no meat-eating frees mercy, no intoxication frees austerity, no
illicit sex frees cleanliness, no gambling frees truthfulness (the pairing as usually taught, arranged from SB 1.17.24–25 and the
1.17.25 and 1.17.38 purports; F.2), and these four are the legs on which a
devotional life stands. They hold not by force but by a higher taste (BG 2.59), and the practice that gives that taste is the one you
have begun.

## D. Learning outcomes

By the end, participants should be able to:
1. Name the four principles and pair each with the quality it protects (austerity, cleanliness, mercy, truthfulness: SB 1.17.24–25;
   the pairing as usually taught, F.2).
2. Explain the Gita's chain from contemplation to fall (BG 2.62–63) and why the principles are placed *before* the fall.
3. State why rules alone do not hold and what does (BG 2.59; 2.64–65), and name the positive practice behind each principle
   (prasadam for the first: BG 9.26, 3.13).
4. Explain what "regulated" means in SB 11.5.11 (the scriptures' permissions lead out, not in) and apply it to "sex life not contrary to
   religious principles" (BG 7.11).
5. Answer the common objections (K) calmly: eggs, tea and coffee, family meals, "I slipped", "isn’t vegetarianism enough?"

## E. Scriptural foundation

**Use:** BG 2.64 (title), SB 1.17.24–25 and 1.17.38 (the four legs), BG 2.62–63 and 2.59 (mechanism and cure), BG 9.26 and 3.13
(prasadam), SB 11.5.11 (regulated permissions) carry the talk. BG 16.21–24, 17.8–10, 7.11, 5.22, 18.38, SB 11.5.14 and 1.17.39 answer
questions.

**BG 2.64**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/2/64/
> "But a person free from all attachment and aversion and able to control his senses through regulative principles of freedom can obtain the complete mercy of the Lord."
- **Purport point:** External sense control by an artificial process risks a fall unless the senses are engaged in the Lord's
  service; the Krsna conscious person "is concerned only with the satisfaction of Kṛṣṇa", and this consciousness "is the causeless
  mercy of the Lord".
- **Teaching:** The Gita calls regulation "principles of freedom" and says the one who controls the senses through them obtains the
  Lord's complete mercy.
- **Application (preacher's):** The topic's title is this verse. Regulation is the shape of a free life, not its price.
- **Use:** in the talk; the frame.

**BG 2.65**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/2/65/
> "For one thus satisfied [in Kṛṣṇa consciousness], the threefold miseries of material existence exist no longer; in such satisfied consciousness, one’s intelligence is soon well established."
- **Use:** in the talk, with 2.64: the result is satisfaction and steadiness.

**BG 2.59**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/2/59/
> "Though the embodied soul may be restricted from sense enjoyment, the taste for sense objects remains. But, ceasing such engagements by experiencing a higher taste, he is fixed in consciousness."
- **Purport point:** Restriction is like a patient's diet: obeyed, not relished; such restrictions "are only good until one actually
  has a taste for Kṛṣṇa consciousness".
- **Application (preacher's):** Why VL3 teaches the principles together with chanting, hearing and prasadam, never alone.
- **Use:** in the talk (recap from VL3-01).

**BG 2.62–63**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/2/62/ and /63/
> 2.62: "While contemplating the objects of the senses, a person develops attachment for them, and from such attachment lust develops, and from lust anger arises."
> 2.63: "From anger, complete delusion arises, and from delusion bewilderment of memory. When memory is bewildered, intelligence is lost, and when intelligence is lost one falls down again into the material pool."
- **Teaching:** The fall begins with contemplation, long before the act.
- **Application (preacher's):** The principles sit at the top of the chain: they decide what is on the plate, the screen and the
  table, so that contemplation has less to feed on. This is why "I’ll just not act on it" rarely works.
- **Use:** in the talk; the mechanism.

**SB 1.17.24**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/sb/1/17/24/
> "In the age of Satya [truthfulness] your four legs were established by the four principles of austerity, cleanliness, mercy and truthfulness. But it appears that three of your legs are broken due to rampant irreligion in the form of pride, lust for women, and intoxication."
- **Context:** Maharaja Pariksit speaks to the bull, Dharma personified, whose legs have been broken by the personality of Kali.
- **Purport point:** Surrender to the Lord "is possible by persons of austerity, cleanliness, mercy and truthfulness"; as these were
  "curtailed by proportionate development of pride, attachment for women and intoxication, the path of salvation… retreated far, far
  away". The purport also lists the Kali-yuga habits: "drinking wine, smoking, drinking tea and chewing tobacco". (IYF's handout opens
  with this passage.)
- **Use:** in the talk; the root text.

**SB 1.17.25**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/sb/1/17/25/
> "You are now standing on one leg only, which is your truthfulness, and you are somehow or other hobbling along. But quarrel personified [Kali], flourishing by deceit, is also trying to destroy that leg."
- **Purport point:** "The principles of religion do not stand on some dogmas or man-made formulas, but they stand on four primary
  regulative observances, namely austerity, cleanliness, mercy and truthfulness." Austerity is to accept voluntarily what is
  uncomfortable for the body but good for spiritual realisation (fasting is the example); cleanliness of mind "is effected by
  glorifying the Supreme Lord"; "the resultant action of human civilization in the Age of Kali is dissatisfaction". The purport then
  pairs the destroyers with the legs: "By pride, either artificial or real, the resultant action of austerity is spoiled; by too much
  affection for female association, cleanliness is spoiled; by too much addiction to intoxication, mercy is spoiled; and by too much lying
  propaganda, truthfulness is spoiled." (IYF's handout quotes this sentence; its bracketed mapping onto the four prohibitions is IYF's
  gloss, see F.2.)
- **Application (preacher's):** The purport's definitions give each principle its positive content: austerity (not intoxication)
  is voluntary acceptance of the uncomfortable; cleanliness (not illicit sex) is a clean mind, made by chanting; and so on.
- **Use:** in the talk.

**SB 1.17.38**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/sb/1/17/38/
> "Sūta Gosvāmī said: Mahārāja Parīkṣit, thus being petitioned by the personality of Kali, gave him permission to reside in places where gambling, drinking, prostitution and animal slaughter were performed."
- **Purport point:** "The basic principles of irreligiosity, such as pride, prostitution, intoxication and falsehood, counteract the
  four principles of religion, namely austerity, cleanliness, mercy and truthfulness." Kali lives in four places: gambling, drinking,
  prostitution, animal slaughter. The purport adds Jiva Gosvami's point that the Vedas give regulated forms (drinking in a
  sacrifice, sex in marriage, meat in sacrifice) for those in ignorance, to lead them gradually, and that "what is recommended for
  those in the mode of ignorance may be poison for those in the mode of goodness".
- **Application (preacher's):** The four places of Kali are the four principles in their negative form.
- **Use:** in the talk; the list itself.

**SB 1.17.39**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/sb/1/17/39/
> "The personality of Kali asked for something more, and because of his begging, the King gave him permission to live where there is gold because wherever there is gold there is also falsity, intoxication, lust, envy and enmity."
- **Use:** for K.9 (money and the four; the fifth place).

**BG 16.21**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/16/21/
> "There are three gates leading to this hell – lust, anger and greed. Every sane man should give these up, for they lead to the degradation of the soul."
- **Purport point:** "The beginning of demoniac life is described herein"; a sane man gives up these three enemies "which can kill
  the self".
- **Application (preacher's):** Teach as diagnosis (what the four principles starve: lust, greed and the anger that follows), not as
  threat. The VL3 profile forbids fear preaching.
- **Use:** in the talk, briefly.

**BG 3.37**: Verified. Lust "born of contact with the material mode of passion", later "transformed into wrath", "the all-devouring
sinful enemy of this world". **Use:** background to 2.62–63.

**BG 18.38**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/18/38/
> "That happiness which is derived from contact of the senses with their objects and which appears like nectar at first but poison at the end is said to be of the nature of passion."
- **Use:** in the talk: the Gita's own one-line description of the four pleasures.

**BG 5.22**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/5/22/
> "An intelligent person does not take part in the sources of misery, which are due to contact with the material senses. O son of Kuntī, such pleasures have a beginning and an end, and so the wise man does not delight in them."
- **Purport point:** Quotes SB 5.5.1: sense pleasure is "available to the stool-eaters [hogs]"; "the more one is addicted to material
  pleasures, the more he is entrapped by material miseries".
- **Use:** background and K.

**BG 17.8–10**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/17/8/ (and following)
> 17.8: "Foods dear to those in the mode of goodness increase the duration of life, purify one’s existence and give strength, health, happiness and satisfaction. Such foods are juicy, fatty, wholesome, and pleasing to the heart."
> 17.10: "Food prepared more than three hours before being eaten, food that is tasteless, decomposed and putrid, and food consisting of remnants and untouchable things is dear to those in the mode of darkness."
- **Teaching:** Foods are classed by the modes they are dear to; goodness foods "purify one’s existence". The verses describe
  qualities, not a menu.
- **Application (preacher's):** The first principle's positive side is a goodness diet, not merely "no meat". Say what is *eaten*.
- **Use:** in the talk.

**BG 9.26**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/9/26/
> "If one offers Me with love and devotion a leaf, a flower, a fruit or water, I will accept it."
- **Purport point:** The process "is very easy and can be attempted even by the poorest of the poor"; Krsna "wants only loving
  service and nothing more"; *bhakti* is mentioned twice to stress that devotion, not status, makes the offering acceptable.
- **Application (preacher's):** The first principle, done fully, is prasadam: the question changes from "what do I avoid?" to "what
  do I offer?" (The 6.17 purport, VL3-01, says eating is regulated "only when one is practiced to take and accept prasādam".)
- **Use:** in the talk.

**BG 3.13**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/3/13/
> "The devotees of the Lord are released from all kinds of sins because they eat food which is offered first for sacrifice. Others, who prepare food for personal sense enjoyment, verily eat only sin."
- **Purport point:** Devotees "cannot accept anything without first offering it to the Supreme Person"; those who cook for themselves
  "are not only thieves but also the eaters of all kinds of sins"; people must be taught "the easy process of saṅkīrtana-yajña".
- **Use:** in the talk, with 9.26.

**BG 7.11**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/7/11/
> "I am the strength of the strong, devoid of passion and desire. I am sex life which is not contrary to religious principles, O lord of the Bhāratas [Arjuna]."
- **Purport point:** Sex life according to religious principles "should be for the propagation of children, not otherwise", and the
  parents' responsibility is "to make their offspring Kṛṣṇa conscious".
- **Teaching:** Krsna identifies Himself with sex life "not contrary to religious principles".
- **Application (preacher's):** This is the ground of "no *illicit* sex": the purport says such sex life is for children; *marriage*
  as its setting comes from SB 11.5.11 ("sex through sacred marriage") and the 1.17.38 purport ("association with women outside
  marriage" as Kali's place).
- **Application (preacher's):** State the standard plainly once, in the Gita's words, with no shame language; the depth belongs to
  one-to-one guidance (VL3-08) and later batches.
- **Use:** in the talk, one sentence; and K.6.

**SB 11.5.11**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/sb/11/5/11/ · *BBT translation, completed by Srila Prabhupada's disciples.*
> "In this material world the conditioned soul is always inclined to sex, meat-eating and intoxication. Therefore religious scriptures never actually encourage such activities. Although the scriptural injunctions provide for sex through sacred marriage, for meat-eating through sacrificial offerings and for intoxication through the acceptance of ritual cups of wine, such ceremonies are meant for the ultimate purpose of renunciation."
- **Purport point:** The regulated permissions let the conditioned soul "indirectly" worship the Lord "by accepting the austerity of
  regulated sense gratification"; "through purification the living entity gradually develops a higher taste".
- **Application (preacher's):** Answers "the scriptures allow it, so why not?": the allowance is a ladder, and the principles are its
  higher rungs. Also the texts' own ground for "regulated" marriage in the fourth principle.
- **Use:** in the talk and K.5.

**SB 11.5.14**: Verified · *BBT translation, completed by Srila Prabhupada's disciples.* Those who "commit violence against innocent
animals who are fully trusting in them" will "be eaten by the same creatures they have killed". IYF's handout quotes it. **Use:**
K.2, carefully: as the Bhagavatam's statement of karmic consequence, not as a threat to the listener's family.

**BG 16.23–24**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/16/23/ and /24/
> 16.23: "He who discards scriptural injunctions and acts according to his own whims attains neither perfection, nor happiness, nor the supreme destination."
> 16.24: "One should therefore understand what is duty and what is not duty by the regulations of the scriptures. Knowing such rules and regulations, one should act so that he may gradually be elevated."
- **Use:** K.1 ("who decides what is illicit?") and the closing: "gradually be elevated" is the mood of the whole topic.

## F. Philosophical explanation

1. **Legs, not fences.** SB 1.17.24–25 names the four legs of religion: austerity, cleanliness, mercy, truthfulness. The 1.17.38
   purport names what breaks them and where Kali lives: gambling, drinking, prostitution, animal slaughter. The four principles are the
   four places we refuse to give Kali a room. Taught this way, each "no" has a face: *what it protects*.
2. **The pairing.** Prabhupada's purports pair the destroyers with the legs: pride spoils austerity, attachment for women spoils
   cleanliness, intoxication spoils mercy, lying spoils truthfulness (1.17.25 purport), and "pride, prostitution, intoxication and
   falsehood counteract… austerity, cleanliness, mercy and truthfulness" (1.17.38 purport). The one-to-one mapping usually taught in
   IYF (meat ↔ mercy, intoxication ↔ austerity, illicit sex ↔ cleanliness, gambling ↔ truthfulness) is a teaching arrangement of these
   statements; present it as such ("the way it is usually taught"), and note the purports do not pair them in exactly one line.
   *Application (preacher's):* the arrangement is sound and memorable; it is the N grid.
3. **The mechanism.** BG 2.62–63: contemplation, attachment, lust, anger, delusion, loss of memory and intelligence, fall. The
   principles act at the first link by changing what is in front of the senses. That is why they are *regulative*: they govern the
   environment, not only the act. BG 18.38 gives the one-line diagnosis of the four pleasures: nectar first, poison after.
4. **Why they hold.** Not by force. BG 2.59: restriction leaves the taste; a higher taste removes it. 2.64–65: control "through
   regulative principles of freedom" obtains the Lord's mercy and yields satisfaction. So the first principle is really prasadam
   (9.26; 3.13), the second is a clean mind made by chanting (1.17.25 purport), the third is austerity accepted for a purpose, the
   fourth is an honest life. A VL3 participant who chants and hears daily is already building the taste that makes the four natural.
5. **The scriptures' permissions.** SB 11.5.11: marriage, sacrificial meat, ritual wine are regulated forms "for the ultimate purpose of
   renunciation". The 1.17.38 purport (Jiva Gosvami) says the same: recommendations for one class may be poison for another. This
   answers both the sceptic ("your own books allow it") and the legalist ("so I can do it in the allowed way"): the allowance exists
   to move people *out*. For VL3, "regulated" in the fourth principle therefore means the Gita's own definition (7.11).
6. **Positive content, principle by principle.**
   - *No meat, fish or eggs → mercy.* Eat in goodness (17.8–10); offer what you eat (9.26); 3.13 calls unoffered cooking "sin" and
     offered eating a release from it. Eggs and fish are included in IYF's standard as the standard; the texts here speak of
     animal slaughter and of unoffered food; say so honestly (K.3).
   - *No intoxication → austerity.* Austerity is voluntary acceptance of the uncomfortable for realisation (1.17.25 purport). A clear
     mind is the instrument of japa and hearing; intoxication rents it out.
   - *No illicit sex → cleanliness.* The Gita's standard in one sentence (7.11); cleanliness of mind by glorifying the Lord (1.17.25
     purport). No depth here; one-to-one for personal questions.
   - *No gambling → truthfulness.* Gambling includes the wish to gain without work and the speculation that follows it; Kali also
     lives "where there is gold" (1.17.39). Honest dealings in money are the positive side.
7. **Freedom as the frame.** Each principle frees a capacity the practice needs: a merciful heart can chant for others; a clear head
   can hear; a clean mind can remember Krsna; an honest tongue can speak truth. "Freedom" is not a marketing word; it is 2.64's.
8. **Misunderstandings to prevent.**
   - "Vegetarianism is the goal." No; prasadam is the practice and love of Krsna the goal. IYF's handout says it well: not
     vegetarians, "Krishna-tarians". Pride in diet is itself a breach of austerity (1.17.25 purport: pride spoils austerity).
   - "Breaking one principle once means I am out." BG 16.24: "gradually be elevated". A slip is a slip; tell your facilitator and
     continue (VL3-08). Never shame language.
   - "Family members who eat meat are sinners I should avoid." The texts condemn the act, not the person; VL3-13 teaches dealings
     with family. Cook, offer and share prasadam; do not preach at the dinner table.
   - "The principles are the whole of spiritual life." They are the legs; chanting and hearing are the life (SPS/DPS build on this).
   - "Health statistics prove the principles." They don't, and the ones in IYF's deck are unsourced (O). The case is scriptural and
     experiential.
9. **Pitch for VL3.** Practical, encouraging. Four terms defined (austerity, cleanliness, mercy, truthfulness; *prasadam*). Each
   participant leaves knowing which principle is settled, which is still hard, and one step for it.

## H. Opening and engagement

**H.1 How to start (first 5 minutes)**
1. Greet, give your name, and say that nothing today is a test of anyone's life; questions are welcome.
2. Write "Four noes" on the board.
3. Ask H.2 question 1 and collect answers until the four principles appear.
4. Say: "Today we turn four noes into four yeses, from the texts."
5. Give the transition line and begin the frame.

**H.2 Opening questions** (about the group's own lives)
1. When people learn you follow a lifestyle with limits, which "no" do they ask about first?
2. Think of a time a rule you were given made sense only later. What changed your mind?
3. In your home, workplace or village, what is the hardest meal, party or gathering to keep your standards in?

- **Opening question:** "When people learn you’re in this programme, which 'no' do they ask about first?" Collect answers on the
  board until the four appear. "Today we turn these four noes into four yeses, from the texts."
- **Relatable scenario (invented):** Kavya, VL3, has been vegetarian a year and chants four rounds. Her cousin says, "Your own
  scriptures allow meat in sacrifice, and wine too. So it's just your group's rule." She did not know what to say. (SB 11.5.11 is the
  answer; promise it.)
- **Transition:** "The Gita never calls these rules. It calls them 'regulative principles of freedom'. Let’s see what they free."
- **Why it matters to this audience:** they are living the four in homes and hostels that do not; they need reasons they can say in
  one breath, and a way to handle their own slips without despair.

## I. Core teaching points

1. **Freedom, not fences.** BG 2.64–65. *Example:* a trellis frees the vine to climb. *Transition:* what do they protect?
2. **Four legs.** SB 1.17.24–25; 1.17.38 purport. *Example:* a table with three broken legs is not "a bit unsteady". *Transition:* how
   does a leg break?
3. **The chain.** BG 2.62–63; 18.38. *Example:* the fall starts at the screen or the plate, not at the act. *Transition:* so why not just
   stronger rules?
4. **Taste, not force.** BG 2.59. *Example:* the patient's diet versus the person who found better food. *Transition:* then each
   principle has a positive side.
5. **Prasadam is the first principle done fully.** BG 9.26; 3.13; 17.8. *Example:* the question becomes "what do I offer?"

6. **The permissions are a ladder out.** SB 11.5.11; 1.17.38 purport (Jiva Gosvami); the Gita's standard (7.11). *Example:* Kavya's
   cousin. *Transition:* what about when I slip?

## K. Anticipated questions and objections

> **Before you teach:** decide that you will answer the "illicit sex" questions in one calm sentence from BG 7.11 and offer one-to-one
> time for anything personal. Decide also that you will not quote statistics you cannot source.

1. **"Who decides what is 'illicit' or 'intoxicating'? Isn’t this just ISKCON’s rule?"**
   - BG 16.23–24: the standard comes from scripture, not whim. The Gita gives the standard for sex (7.11); the Bhagavatam names Kali's
     four places (1.17.38). ISKCON's wording of the four is a summary of these.
2. **"Plants are alive too. Isn’t eating them violence?"**
   - IYF's handout answers this well from Prabhupada's teaching: every living being lives on another; a human should commit only the
     violence necessary, and offered food releases the eater from the reaction (BG 3.13). SB 11.5.14 states the consequence of killing
     trusting animals. Say it as the text's statement, not as a verdict on anyone's family.
3. **"Why eggs? Why fish? They are not 'meat'."**
   - Be honest: the verses here speak of animal slaughter (1.17.38) and of goodness food (17.8–10) and offered food (9.26); the
     tradition classes fish as animals and eggs as not offerable to Krsna, and IYF's standard includes both. State the standard and its
     basis plainly; do not invent a verse for eggs.
4. **"Is tea or coffee intoxication?"**
   - The principle names intoxication; the tradition's practice avoids tea and coffee as stimulants, and the 1.17.24 purport lists
     "drinking tea" among Kali-yuga habits (the IYF handout quotes it). Say what the standard is and let the facilitator guide the
     person's pace. Do not make it the whole talk.
5. **"Your scriptures allow meat in sacrifice and wine in ritual. So it’s optional."**
   - SB 11.5.11: those permissions are "meant for the ultimate purpose of renunciation"; the 1.17.38 purport says what is recommended
     for one class may be poison for another. The allowance is a ladder out, not a licence.
6. **"What exactly counts as illicit sex?"**
   - BG 7.11 and purport: sex according to religious principles is "for the propagation of children"; SB 11.5.11 places it in
     "sacred marriage". That is the standard. Anything more
     personal is for one-to-one guidance (VL3-08), not the group.
7. **"I broke a principle last week. Have I lost everything?"**
   - No. BG 16.24 says "gradually be elevated"; VL2-02 taught from BG 2.40 and 6.41–45 that nothing sincere is lost. Tell your
     facilitator, restart today. Shame is not a principle.
8. **"My family cooks meat. Do I refuse their food? Leave the table?"**
   - Keep the standard, keep the relationship. Eat what you can, offer what you cook, never lecture at the table; VL3-13 is the full
     topic. *Application (preacher's)*: a cheerful, reliable son or daughter is the best argument a family will hear.
9. **"Is earning money or investing 'gambling'?"**
   - Honest work is not gambling. The principle targets gain without work and the deceit that follows; SB 1.17.39 warns that Kali also
     lives "where there is gold", so money needs honesty (truthfulness) around it. Speculative betting is out; a salary is not.
10. **"Is being vegetarian enough?"**
    - No. Vegetarianism is a diet; prasadam is a relationship (BG 9.26). And pride in one's diet spoils austerity (1.17.25 purport).

# Part 2: For students

## S1. Summary

The Bhagavad-gita calls regulation "regulative principles of freedom" (BG 2.64). The four principles (no meat, fish or eggs; no intoxication; no illicit sex; no gambling) are not four fences but four freedoms. Srimad-Bhagavatam says religion stands on four legs, austerity, cleanliness, mercy and truthfulness (SB 1.17.24–25), and that Kali, the spirit of this age, lives in four places: gambling, drinking, prostitution and animal slaughter (SB 1.17.38). So each "no" protects a "yes": no meat frees mercy, no intoxication frees austerity, no illicit sex frees cleanliness, no gambling frees truthfulness (as usually taught). Scripture, not personal whim, says what is illicit or intoxicating (BG 16.23–24); for sex the Gita gives the standard of sex for children, within marriage (BG 7.11).

A fall begins with contemplation, long before the act (BG 2.62–63), so the principles work by changing what is in front of us. They hold not by force but by a higher taste (BG 2.59): the first principle done fully is prasadam, food offered to Krsna (BG 9.26), and eating offered food releases the eater from the reactions (BG 3.13). Vegetarian food alone is only a diet; prasadam is a relationship, and pride in one's diet spoils austerity (SB 1.17.25 purport).

Scriptural permissions for meat, wine or sex in regulated forms are "meant for the ultimate purpose of renunciation" (SB 11.5.11): a ladder out, not a licence. A slip is not the end: "gradually be elevated" (BG 16.24); tell your facilitator and restart today. With family who do not follow, keep the standard and the relationship: eat what you can and never lecture at meals.

## S2. Key ideas

- "Regulative principles of freedom": the Gita's own name for them (BG 2.64).
- Four legs of religion: austerity, cleanliness, mercy, truthfulness (SB 1.17.24–25).
- Kali's four places: gambling, drinking, prostitution, animal slaughter (SB 1.17.38).
- The fall starts with contemplation (BG 2.62); the principles act at the first link.
- Higher taste, not force, makes them hold (BG 2.59); prasadam is the first principle's positive side (BG 9.26).
- Permissions in scripture lead out, not in (SB 11.5.11).
- Slips: report, restart, "gradually be elevated" (BG 16.24).

## S3. Verses to remember

- **BG 2.64:** control of the senses "through regulative principles of freedom" obtains "the complete mercy of the Lord". *For me:* the principles free me; they are not a price.
- **SB 1.17.24:** religion's "four legs were established by the four principles of austerity, cleanliness, mercy and truthfulness". *For me:* each "no" protects one leg.
- **BG 2.62:** "While contemplating the objects of the senses, a person develops attachment for them…" *For me:* guard the first link, not only the last.
- **BG 9.26:** "If one offers Me with love and devotion a leaf, a flower, a fruit or water, I will accept it." *For me:* the question is "what do I offer?"

## S4. Words to know

- **Austerity (tapa):** accepting voluntarily what is uncomfortable for the body but good for spiritual life (SB 1.17.25 purport).
- **Cleanliness (śauca):** of body and of mind; the mind is cleaned by glorifying the Lord.
- **Mercy (dayā):** kindness and compassion to all living beings.
- **Truthfulness (satya):** honesty in word and dealing.
- **Prasadam:** food offered to Krsna with love before eating (BG 9.26).
- **Kali:** the personality of this age of quarrel, who lives where the four principles are broken (SB 1.17.38).

## S5. Questions and answers

1. **Who decides what is "illicit" or "intoxicating"?** Scripture, not whim (BG 16.23–24). The Gita gives the standard for sex (BG 7.11); the Bhagavatam names Kali's four places (SB 1.17.38).
2. **Plants are alive too. Isn't eating them violence?** Every being lives on another; a human should do only the violence necessary, and offered food releases the eater from the reaction (BG 3.13).
3. **The scriptures allow meat in sacrifice and ritual wine, so isn't it optional?** Those permissions are "meant for the ultimate purpose of renunciation" (SB 11.5.11): a ladder out, not a licence.
4. **What counts as illicit sex?** Sex according to religious principles is for children (BG 7.11 purport), within marriage (SB 11.5.11). Personal questions go to your facilitator one-to-one.
5. **I broke a principle. Have I lost everything?** No. "Gradually be elevated" (BG 16.24). Tell your facilitator and restart today.
6. **My family eats meat. Do I leave the table?** Keep the standard and the relationship: eat what you can, cook and offer when you can, never lecture at meals (VL3-13 is the full topic).
7. **Is being vegetarian enough?** No. Vegetarianism is a diet; prasadam is a relationship (BG 9.26), and pride in diet spoils austerity (SB 1.17.25 purport).
