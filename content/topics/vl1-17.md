# Overcoming anger

> **VL1 · 45–60 min · review: sources checked, awaiting senior review.** Scripture light but exact. IYF holds eleven files on this topic; the
> page follows their shared structure (problem → cause → symptoms → solution → application) and their best
> story (Ambarisa and Durvasa, SB 9.4). Their quotation lists and the SB 4.8 genealogy of anger are left out.
> Not a sensitive topic, but read the note on violence at home in K.

## A. Topic overview

| | |
|---|---|
| Batch / level | VL1 (Vedic Level 1), a stand-alone talk |
| Suggested duration | 45–60 min (G gives a 30-minute cut) |
| Preparation time | About 90 min, the first time |
| Difficulty | Introductory |
| Prerequisites | None (VL1-16 "Mind your mind" gives the chariot model this talk assumes) |
| Audience | Students and young professionals; everyone has anger they regret (family, roommates, traffic, online). Some may live with anger directed at them |
| Teaching objective | Show where anger comes from (frustrated desire), what it costs (loss of judgement), and a method that is neither venting nor suppression: catch it early, redirect it, and remove its root by a higher taste |

## B. Why this topic belongs at this level

Builds on vl1-16: the chariot (mind as reins) and "just try harder fails" are taken as known; here only the chain from desire to anger, and the practice of catching it, are taught. Do not redraw the chariot.

Anger is the emotion this audience most often regrets and least understands. The Gita gives a precise causal
chain (desire → frustration → anger → loss of judgement) that matches their experience and suggests where to
intervene. The talk is practical and non-judgemental: anger is a signal, not a sin to be ashamed of, and even
great sages lost their temper (SB 9.4).

How the subject grows at other levels:

| Level | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **VL1** (here) | The chain; the cost; early catching; redirecting; the Ambarisa story; three everyday tools |
| VL2 | The modes of nature: anger and the mode of passion/ignorance (BG 3.37 purport) |
| VL3 | A small daily practice that lowers the baseline: chanting, a pause routine, sleep and food |
| SPS | Tolerating "the actions of anger" as one of the six urges (NOI 1); anger in devotional dealings; forgiveness |
| DPS | Handling anger in teams: feedback, conflict, and repairing a relationship |
| BSS | Anger and the brahmacari's inner life (BIKC chapters) |

**Not done here:**
- Anger as a mode-of-nature analysis (VL2).
- The cosmology of anger's origin (SB 3–4; on IYF's slides; left out).
- Treating all anger as wrong: the scripture itself speaks of anger rightly applied (NOI 1 purport).

## C. Central message

Anger is frustrated desire wearing a mask; it feels like power and costs us our judgement. We overcome it not
by venting or by bottling but by catching it early, redirecting it to something worth being fierce about, and
slowly removing its root.

## D. Learning outcomes

By the end, participants should be able to:
1. Trace the Gita's chain from contemplation to anger to loss of judgement (BG 2.62–63) in one of their own
   incidents.
2. Explain why "anger is frustrated desire" (BG 3.37, 16.21 purport) and name their own usual trigger-desire.
3. Distinguish three responses (venting, bottling, managing) and say why the Gita's path is a fourth: redirect
   and remove the root.
4. Retell the Ambarisa–Durvasa story accurately and draw its lesson about pride and anger.
5. Choose one tool to use at the next flare-up.

## E. Scriptural foundation

**Use:** BG 2.62–63, 3.37 and SB 9.4 carry the talk. The rest prepare you for questions.

**BG 2.62–63**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/2/62/ · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/2/63/
> 2.62: "…from such attachment lust develops, and from lust anger arises."
> 2.63: "From anger, complete delusion arises, and from delusion bewilderment of memory. When memory is bewildered, intelligence is lost, and when intelligence is lost one falls down again into the material pool."
- **Context:** Chapter 2, Krsna describes how a person loses steadiness.
- **Teaching:** Anger comes from desire and leads to delusion, forgetfulness and loss of intelligence.
- **Purport point (2.63):** In Krsna consciousness "everything becomes spiritualized, and there is no danger of
  a downfall"; artificial renunciation is called *phalgu*, "less important". (The 2.62 purport adds that
  artificial repression fails, "for the slightest thought of sense pleasure will agitate him".)
- **Application (preacher's):** Everyone recognises the sequence: "I don't know what came over me" is
  "bewilderment of memory". The chain tells us anger is late in the sequence; intervene earlier.
- **Use:** in the talk (the spine).

**BG 3.37**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/3/37/
> "It is lust only, Arjuna, which is born of contact with the material mode of passion and later transformed into wrath, and which is the all-devouring sinful enemy of this world."
- **Context:** Arjuna has asked (3.36) what forces a person to act wrongly against his will.
- **Teaching:** Desire (lust, *kama*) is the root; anger is its transformation.
- **Purport point:** "when lust is unsatisfied, it turns into wrath; wrath is transformed into illusion". The
  purport adds that anger can be spiritualised: Hanuman burned Lanka in anger and is a great devotee, and
  Krsna encouraged Arjuna to use his anger against the enemy for the Lord's satisfaction.
- **Application (preacher's):** Behind every anger is a thwarted "I want". Find the want, and you find the
  handle.
- **Use:** in the talk.

**BG 16.21**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/16/21/
> "There are three gates leading to this hell – lust, anger and greed. Every sane man should give these up, for they lead to the degradation of the soul."
- **Teaching:** Anger is one of three gates to a degraded condition.
- **Purport point:** "One tries to satisfy his lust, and when he cannot, anger and greed arise."
- **Application (preacher's):** At VL1 soften "hell" to "a hellish state of mind", which is also what the
  angry person experiences; do not preach damnation.
- **Use:** in the talk, one line.

**BG 5.23**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/5/23/
> "Before giving up this present body, if one is able to tolerate the urges of the material senses and check the force of desire and anger, he is well situated and is happy in this world."
- **Teaching:** Checking desire and anger makes one happy *in this world*, not only hereafter.
- **Purport point:** "Material desires, when unsatiated, generate anger, and thus the mind, eyes and chest
  become agitated." "one must practice to control them".
- **Application (preacher's):** The body gives early warning (chest, eyes). Practice means noticing those
  signs before the words come out.
- **Use:** in the talk.

**BG 2.56**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/2/56/
> "One who is not disturbed in mind even amidst the threefold miseries or elated when there is happiness, and who is free from attachment, fear and anger, is called a sage of steady mind."
- **Purport point:** "Success or no success, a Kṛṣṇa conscious person is always steady in his determination."
  "he is not at all angry even when his attempts are unsuccessful."
- **Application (preacher's):** The steady person is not someone who never wants anything; it is someone
  whose peace does not depend on getting it.
- **Use:** in the talk (the goal).

**BG 16.1–3**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/16/1-3/
> "…freedom from anger… these transcendental qualities, O son of Bharata, belong to godly men…"
- **Purport point:** "Akrodha means to check anger. Even if there is provocation one should be tolerant, for
  once one becomes angry his whole body becomes polluted."
- **Use:** in the talk, one line (the quality is *checking* anger, not never feeling it).

**NOI 1**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/noi/1/
> "A sober person who can tolerate the urge to speak, the mind's demands, the actions of anger and the urges of the tongue, belly and genitals is qualified to make disciples all over the world."
- **Purport point:** "Krodha, anger, cannot be stopped, but it can be applied rightly." "It was in anger that
  Hanumān set fire to Laṅkā, but he is worshiped as the greatest devotee of Lord Rāmacandra." Arjuna "was not
  willing to fight, but Kṛṣṇa incited his anger".
- **Application (preacher's):** This is the page's licence for "redirect": anger at injustice, at one's own
  laziness, at cruelty, can be fuel. The test is whom it serves.
- **Use:** in the talk.

**SB 9.4.18–20**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/sb/9/4/18-20/
> "Mahārāja Ambarīṣa always engaged his mind in meditating upon the lotus feet of Kṛṣṇa, his words in describing the glories of the Lord, his hands in cleansing the Lord's temple…"
- **Teaching:** Ambarisa's senses and mind were fully engaged in service.
- **Application (preacher's):** This is why he did not flare when provoked: an engaged mind is hard to hijack.
- **Use:** in the talk (sets up the story).

**SB 9.4.39–40**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/sb/9/4/39-40/
- **Teaching:** Faced with a dilemma (offend a guest or break his vow's timing), Ambarisa consults the brahmanas
  and drinks only water, which "may be accepted as eating and also as not eating".
- **Application (preacher's):** Ambarisa's care shows he gave no real offence; Durvasa's anger was about his
  own pride.
- **Use:** in the talk (story).

**SB 9.4.63**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/sb/9/4/63/
> "I am completely under the control of My devotees. Indeed, I am not at all independent…"
- **Context:** The Lord replies to Durvasa, who has fled to Vaikuntha for protection from the Sudarsana disc.
- **Teaching:** The Lord protects His devotee and will not override him even to rescue an offender.
- **Use:** in the talk (story climax).

**SB 9.4.68**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/sb/9/4/68/
> "The pure devotee is always within the core of My heart, and I am always in the heart of the pure devotee…"
- **Use:** in the talk, one line, to explain why Durvasa is sent back to Ambarisa.

## F. Philosophical explanation

1. **The principle.** Anger is desire (*kama*) frustrated (BG 3.37; 16.21 purport). It arises late in a
   chain that begins with what the mind dwells on (2.62) and it ends in loss of memory and judgement (2.63).
   It cannot be simply stopped, but it can be applied rightly (NOI 1 purport), and its root is removed by a
   higher engagement (2.63 purport; SB 9.4.18–20).
2. **Why it matters.** Anger is the emotion whose consequences are most visible and least reversible: words
   said, relationships cut, careers damaged. The Gita's chain lets a person intervene before the words.
3. **Connections.** Builds on the chariot (VL1-16): anger is the horses bolting after a thwarted destination.
   Leads to fear (VL1-18): "free from attachment, fear and anger" (BG 2.56) are one package. At higher levels,
   anger is one of the six urges (NOI 1) and the modes (VL2).
4. **Consequences.** Someone who knows that anger is frustrated desire stops asking "why am I so angry?" and
   asks "what did I want?". They catch the body's early warnings (5.23 purport), use the pause, and over time
   find fewer things worth bolting for.
5. **Misunderstandings to prevent.**
   - **"Spiritual people never get angry."** Durvasa was a great sage and lost his temper; Hanuman and Arjuna
     used anger in service (NOI 1 purport). *Akrodha* is checking anger, not numbness (16.1–3 purport).
   - **"Anger is energy; let it out."** Venting is the chain running to its end (2.63). The text asks us to
     check the force (5.23), then redirect it.
   - **"Bottle it; that's tolerance."** Bottled anger is artificial renunciation, undone by a small
     disturbance (2.63 purport). Tolerance in the Gita means the urge is felt, checked and redirected.
   - **"Anger at injustice is wrong too."** Not if it serves what is right and stays under the intelligence
     (NOI 1 purport). The test is whom it serves and whether the driver still holds the reins.
6. **Pitch for VL1.** Say "desire" rather than "lust" when explaining BG 3.37 to this audience; *kama* means
   any strong selfish wanting. Use *krodha* once, defined. No preaching about hell; the Gita's "gates" describe
   a state anyone who has raged recognises.

## H. Opening and engagement

**H.1 How to start (first 5 minutes)**
1. Greet, say your name, and say this is a no-shame talk: everyone gets angry, so we study how it works.
2. Ask the "think privately" question below; allow ten seconds of silence.
3. Ask H.2 question 1 as a show of hands, not a story.
4. Say: "Nobody has to share a memory today."
5. Give the transition and begin with what anger costs.

**H.2 Opening questions** (about the room's own life)
1. Where do you feel anger first in your body (jaw, chest, hands, face)?
2. What small thing reliably irritates you (a late bus, a slow connection, a loud neighbour)?
3. Think of someone who stays calm under pressure. What do they do differently?

- **Opening question:** "Think, privately, of the last time anger made you say or do something you regret.
  Don't share it. Just notice: at that moment, did you feel weak or powerful?" Pause. "Most people say
  powerful. That is the trap."
- **Relatable scenario (invented):** Sameer's roommate uses his laptop charger without asking, again. Sameer
  explodes, says something about the roommate's family, and the room is silent for a week. What did Sameer
  want? What did he get?
- **Transition:** "The Gita describes this exact sequence, step by step, in two verses."
- **Why it matters to this audience:** One angry message can end a friendship or a job; one angry sentence to
  a parent can take years to repair.

## I. Core teaching points

1. **Anger costs judgement.** From anger, delusion; from delusion, loss of memory and intelligence (BG 2.63).
   *Example:* "I don't know what came over me." *Transition:* so where does it come from?
2. **Anger is frustrated desire.** "When lust is unsatisfied, it turns into wrath" (BG 3.37 purport; 16.21
   purport). *Example:* Sameer wanted respect and control of his things. *Transition:* so the handle is the
   want, not the roommate.
3. **The body warns early.** Desires unsatisfied agitate "the mind, eyes and chest" (BG 5.23 purport). Checking
   the force at that stage is the practice. *Example:* the hot face, the clenched jaw: that is the moment.
   *Transition:* and then?
4. **Neither vent nor bottle: redirect.** "Anger cannot be stopped, but it can be applied rightly" (NOI 1
   purport). Hanuman, Arjuna. *Example:* anger at your own procrastination can be fuel. *Transition:* but the
   deepest fix is upstream.
5. **Remove the root by engagement.** Ambarisa's mind and senses were fully engaged (SB 9.4.18–20), so a
   provocation did not hijack him; the steady person is "not at all angry even when his attempts are
   unsuccessful" (2.56 purport). *Transition:* to the story and the tools.

## J. Stories and illustrations

- **Ambarisa and Durvasa** (SB 9.4; verified verses 18–20, 39–40, 63, 68; read the rest of chapter 4 and the
  start of chapter 5 before telling it). King Ambarisa, whose every sense was engaged in service, completes a
  year-long vow. The sage Durvasa arrives as his guest, goes to bathe, and delays. The time to break the fast
  is running out; the king consults the brahmanas and drinks only water (39–40). Durvasa, perceiving this,
  grows angry, creates a demon to kill the king; the Lord's disc destroys the demon and chases the sage across
  the universe. He reaches the Lord, who says He is "completely under the control of My devotees" (63) and
  sends him back to the one he offended (68). Ambarisa prays for the sage's release and then feeds him.
  *Connection:* the sage's anger came from pride and a misread offence; the king's calm came from an engaged
  mind and absence of pride. Tell it from the text; do not add dialogue.
- **Hanuman and Arjuna** (NOI 1 purport; BG 3.37 purport): anger applied in service. *Connection:* redirect.
- **The mongoose and the mother** (on IYF's handout): a traditional folk tale (Panchatantra/Hitopadesa type) of
  a mother who kills the pet mongoose in haste, believing it attacked her child, then finds it had killed a
  snake. *Label it a traditional tale, not scripture.* *Connection:* anger acts before the facts (2.63).
- **Left out:** IYF's slide of quotations ("anger is one letter short of danger", etc.) and the proverb "if you
  are patient in one moment of anger…" (a traditional saying, fine informally, not a source); the SB 3–4
  genealogy of anger (Adharma's descendants, Rudra's residences), which is advanced and distracting at VL1.

## K. Anticipated questions and objections

> **Before you teach:** someone in the room may live with another person's anger (a parent, partner or
> roommate who is violent or abusive). This talk is about *our own* anger. If a question or disclosure points
> to violence at home, do not advise "tolerance"; speak to the person afterwards, take it seriously, and point
> them to help. *(Pastoral guidance, not a scriptural claim.)*

1. **"Isn't anger sometimes right? Injustice should make us angry."**
   - Yes. "Anger cannot be stopped, but it can be applied rightly" (NOI 1 purport); Hanuman and Arjuna are
     the examples. The test: does the intelligence still hold the reins, and whom does the anger serve?
2. **"Psychologists say suppressing anger is unhealthy. Isn't 'tolerate' suppression?"**
   - The Gita agrees that bottling fails: artificial restraint is undone by a small disturbance (BG 2.63
     purport). "Check the force" (5.23) means pause before acting, then redirect or address the want. That is
     closer to what good counselling teaches than to suppression.
3. **"Durvasa was a great sage and still lost it. What hope do I have?"**
   - That is the comfort of the story: anger is not a sign you are hopeless; it is a sign a desire (there,
     for respect) was frustrated. The king shows the alternative, and he was a householder with a kingdom.
4. **"I get angry at small things. Why?"**
   - Small triggers usually hide a bigger want (respect, control, fairness). BG 3.37: find the desire. Also
     the body: hunger, tiredness and overload lower the threshold (BG 6.17's regulation of eating and sleep
     helps; see VL1-15).
5. **"Can chanting really help with anger?"**
   - Offer it as a test, not a claim: an engaged mind is harder to hijack (SB 9.4.18–20), and the pause itself
     breaks the chain at 2.62–63. Ten quiet minutes before replying to a provoking message is a fair experiment.
6. **"My anger scares me; I've hurt people."**
   - *Pastoral:* acknowledge the courage of saying so; talk afterwards; say plainly that counselling or anger
     management therapy is fully compatible with spiritual life and that taking it is itself a spiritual act of
     responsibility.

## L. Case studies

*Invented cases for discussion. Not real incidents.*

**Case 1: The charger.** Sameer's roommate borrows his charger without asking for the third time. Sameer
explodes, insults the roommate's family, and the room goes cold for a week.
- **Dilemma:** What did Sameer want, and what did he get?
- **Principles:** BG 3.37 (the want behind the anger); 2.63 (loss of judgement); 5.23 purport (the body's
  early warning).
- **Questions:** Name Sameer's desire. At what point could he have checked the force? What would "redirect"
  have looked like (a clear request, a boundary)?
- **Takeaway:** The handle is the want; the moment is the body's warning.

**Case 2: The group project.** Priyanka is furious that a teammate did nothing and still got credit. She
drafts a scorching message to the whole group.
- **Principles:** NOI 1 purport (anger applied rightly vs wrongly); 2.56 purport (steady whether or not
  attempts succeed); BG 16.1–3 purport (*akrodha*: check, even when provoked).
- **Questions:** Is her anger "right"? Does the message serve justice or ego? What would a redirected version
  be (speak to the teacher, set a rule for next time)? What if she waits a day?
- **Takeaway:** Right anger still needs the driver holding the reins.

## M. Discussion and reflection

**M.1 Closing check questions** (easy first; praise every try)
1. Is anger a sign of weakness or power? *It feels like power but costs judgement.*
2. What comes before anger in BG 2.62? *Desire (frustrated desire); contemplating sense objects before that.*
3. What comes after anger in BG 2.63? *Delusion, loss of memory, loss of intelligence.*
4. Why did the sage Durvasa become angry? *Pride; the king's calm showed him up.*
5. Name one tool. *Notice the body sign, pause ten seconds, redirect the energy, remove the root.*

**M.2 Activity (about 8 minutes): "The ten-second pause"**
1. 1 min: everyone names a body sign of anger quietly to themselves.
2. 3 min: in pairs, pick one trigger and write a one-line pause plan ("when I feel X, I breathe out slowly and walk away for ten seconds").
3. 3 min: practise it once; the listener plays the person who annoyed you, kindly.
4. 1 min: volunteers share one plan. Thank each; no one shares a story.

**M.3 Reflection**
- **Reflection:** What is my usual anger style: lock it up, turn it loose, or manage it? What want is usually
  behind my anger?
- **Application (private):** What is one early body-sign of my anger, and what will I do in the ten seconds
  after I notice it? *(Nobody needs to share.)*

## O. Research and supplementary resources

None required. IYF's three "anger styles" (lock it up / turn it loose / manage it) are a common

# Part 2: For students

## S1. Summary

Everyone has said something in anger they wish they could take back. The Bhagavad-gita explains the sequence step by step: we dwell on something, we want it, the want is frustrated, anger arises, and "from anger, complete delusion arises", then loss of memory and judgement (BG 2.62-63; 3.37). Anger is frustrated desire wearing a mask: it feels like power and costs us our intelligence. Its costs are to judgement, words, health and relationships.

We handle anger in three common ways: lock it up, turn it loose, or manage it. Venting runs the chain to its end, and bottling breaks at the first push. The Gita points to a better way: notice the early body signs (BG 5.23), pause, stay steady whether things go well or badly (BG 2.56), redirect the energy to something worth being fierce about, and slowly remove the root.

King Ambarisa shows this. His mind was fully engaged in service (SB 9.4.18-20), so when a great sage, Durvasa, lost his temper at him, the king stayed calm. The story shows that pride fuels anger and an engaged mind resists it. Start with one tool and keep it.

## S2. Key ideas

- Anger is late in a chain that starts with what the mind dwells on (BG 2.62).
- Anger is frustrated desire: find the want and you find the handle (BG 3.37).
- Anger costs judgement: "I don't know what came over me" is BG 2.63.
- The body warns early (chest, eyes, jaw); checking the force there is the practice (BG 5.23).
- Anger "cannot be stopped, but it can be applied rightly" (NOI 1 purport). The test is whom it serves.
- A mind engaged in something higher is hard to hijack (SB 9.4.18–20).

## S3. Verses to remember

- **BG 2.63:** "From anger, complete delusion arises, and from delusion bewilderment of memory. When memory is
  bewildered, intelligence is lost…" *For me:* anger is where my judgement leaves.
- **BG 5.23:** One who can "check the force of desire and anger… is well situated and is happy in this world."
  *For me:* the payoff is now, not later.
- **BG 2.56:** The steady person is "free from attachment, fear and anger". *For me:* peace that doesn't
  depend on getting my way.

## S4. Words to know

- **Kama (desire):** strong selfish wanting; the root of anger (BG 3.37).
- **Krodha (anger):** the energy that arises when the want is blocked.
- **Akrodha:** checking anger, even when provoked (BG 16.1–3 purport). Not numbness.

## S5. Questions and answers

1. **Isn't anger sometimes right?**
   Yes. The tradition says anger "can be applied rightly" (NOI 1 purport): Hanuman and Arjuna are the examples.
   The test is whether your intelligence still holds the reins and whom the anger serves.
2. **Isn't "tolerating anger" just suppression?**
   No. The Gita's teachers warn that artificial repression fails at the slightest push (BG 2.62 purport, said
   of sense desire, the root of anger). "Check the force" (BG 5.23) means pause, then
   redirect or address the want openly.
3. **A great sage like Durvasa lost his temper. What hope do I have?**
   That is the comfort: anger is not proof you are hopeless; it is a frustrated want (there, for respect). The
   king shows the alternative, and he ran a kingdom.
4. **Why do I get angry at small things?**
   Small triggers hide big wants: respect, control, fairness. Tiredness and hunger lower the threshold too.
5. **I've hurt people with my anger and it scares me.**
   Saying so takes courage. Talk to someone you trust, and know that counselling is fully compatible with
   spiritual life.

## S6. Check your understanding

1. According to BG 2.62, anger arises from: (a) other people's behaviour, (b) lust/desire, (c) bad luck.
2. True or false: BG 2.63 says anger sharpens the intelligence.
3. What does the NOI 1 purport say about anger: that it can be stopped, or applied rightly?
4. In SB 9.4, why did King Ambarisa drink only water before Durvasa returned?
5. In your own words: what is the difference between checking anger and bottling it?

**Answers:** 1. (b) (BG 2.62). 2. False: from anger comes delusion, loss of memory and loss of intelligence
(BG 2.63). 3. "Anger cannot be stopped, but it can be applied rightly" (NOI 1 purport). 4. The time to break
his vow was running out; after consulting the brahmanas he drank water, which counts as eating and not eating
(SB 9.4.39–40). 5. Checking means pausing at the body's warning and then redirecting or speaking clearly;
bottling means pretending it isn't there, which fails at the next push.
