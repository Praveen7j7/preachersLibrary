> "Mind your mind", VL1-17 "Overcoming anger"). This page takes the second word of its title: **emotion**. Its
> spine is the song of the brahmana from Avanti (SB 11.23), who discovered under abuse that the cause of his

## B. Why this topic belongs at this level

Everyone in the room was hijacked by an emotion this week. The experience needs no selling. What they lack is a
model that explains it and a method that is not "calm down". The Avanti brahmana's song gives both in a form a
VL1 audience accepts at once: a man who lost everything, was mocked and mistreated, and sang that the people
were not the cause. The Gita's chapters 2 and 3 then supply the mechanism.

Builds on vl1-03, vl1-16, vl1-17 and vl1-26: the chariot model (vl1-16), suppression versus higher taste (vl1-03) and the habit loop (vl1-26) are not repeated. The BG 2.62–63 chain, argued in vl1-26 for habits, is used here only for emotions, in one pass.

## C. Central message

Events do not make our emotions; the mind's reading of events does. A thought dwelt on becomes an attachment,
a desire, an anger, a loss of judgement. Control is not suppressing the feeling; it is steadying the mind with
intelligence and feeding it daily so that the storm never fully forms.

## D. Learning outcomes

By the end, participants should be able to:
1. Retell the Avanti brahmana's discovery in one sentence (SB 11.23.42) and say why it is not victim-blaming.
2. Describe the chain in BG 2.62–63 and locate where an emotion becomes a storm.
3. Explain the ladder of senses, mind, intelligence and self (BG 3.42) and what "steady the mind by intelligence"
   means (BG 3.43).
4. Distinguish control from suppression using BG 2.64, 2.70 and 5.20.
5. Try one technique: notice, name, bring back (BG 6.26).

**SB 11.23.42**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/sb/11/23/42/
> "These people are not the cause of my happiness and distress. Neither are the demigods, my own body, the planets, my past work, or time. Rather, it is the mind alone that causes happiness and distress and perpetuates the rotation of material life."
- **Context:** Mocked and mistreated as a beggar (the chapter describes it), the brahmana sings.
- **Teaching:** The mind, not people, body, fate, karma or time, is the cause of happiness and distress.

- **Application (preacher's):** Why "calm down" never works: the storming mind is deaf to advice. Intelligence has

- **Teaching:** The Supersoul within is a witness; the soul has embraced the mind, a mirror of the world, and is

## F. Philosophical explanation

1. **The principle.** The mind sits between the senses and the intelligence (BG 3.42). It receives impressions,
   dwells on them, and the dwelling becomes attachment, desire, anger and loss of judgement (BG 2.62–63). The
   events themselves are not the cause; the mind's reading is (SB 11.23.42). Hence every practice in the tradition
   aims at the mind (SB 11.23.45), and the method is to steady it by deliberate intelligence (BG 3.43) and bring it
   back whenever it wanders (BG 6.26).
2. **Why it matters.** This audience lives at the mercy of notifications and comments. The teaching that the
   cause is the mind's reading is the first good news they have had about emotions: there is a lever, and they hold
   it. The brahmana held it in the worst conditions.
3. **Connections.** To the mind as friend or enemy (VL1-16), to anger as one case (VL1-17), to the hierarchy
   (VL1-22), and later to chanting as the practice that fixes the mind (VL3).
4. **Consequences.** A person who locates the cause in the mind stops sorting people into friends and enemies
   (SB 11.23.48), argues less, and gains time between feeling and action. Over months, the ocean image (BG 2.70)
   becomes their experience: rivers still arrive, the level holds.
5. **Misunderstandings to prevent.**
   - **"Control means suppression."** BG 2.64 asks freedom from *aversion* too; BG 2.70 says desires continue.
     Suppression is aversion in disguise.
   - **"Control means feeling nothing."** BG 5.20 describes steadiness, not numbness; the brahmana still felt his
     ruin (SB 11.23.28–29).
   - **"The mind is the cause, so suffering is my fault."** The brahmana's verse is a tool. Pain
     caused by others is real; the verse is about what one does with the mind's reading of it. Never use it against
     a victim.
   - **"Emotional problems are spiritual weakness."** Persistent conditions are medical (K).
6. **Pitch for VL1.** Words: "reading", "chain", "ladder", "bring it back". *Sthira-buddhi* may be said once with
   BG 5.20. The brahmana's story is told as a story; the room is asked to test the chain against one memory, not to
   believe it.

## H. Opening and engagement

**H.1 How to start (first 5 minutes)**
1. Greet, give your name, and say today's talk is about a feeling everyone has, not about being weak.
2. Ask H.2 question 1 with a show of hands.
3. Thank the room; do not ask anyone to describe what happened.
4. Promise a story of a man who lost everything and what he said afterwards.
5. Give the transition line below and tell the story.

**H.2 Opening questions** (about the room's own life)
1. Has one message, comment or look ever spoiled your day?

3. Who do you know that stays calm under pressure? What do they seem to do differently?

- **Opening question:** "Hands up if one message, one comment or one look ruined a whole day for you this month."
  Pause. "Who sent the message: them, or your mind?"
- **Relatable scenario (invented; adapt to any setting: a message at work, a remark at the market, a neighbour's comment):** Sameer posts a photo; a friend comments "nice try". Sameer reads it eleven
  times. By evening he has drafted three replies, skipped dinner and decided the friendship is over. The comment
  took two seconds; the storm took six hours. Who made the storm?
- **Transition:** "A man in the Bhagavatam lost his wealth, his family's respect and his dignity, was mocked in

- **Why it matters to this audience:** Their moods, relationships and study hours are being set by other people's
  two-second actions. The talk gives the lever back.

## I. Core teaching points

1. **The cause is the mind's reading, not the event.** The brahmana crosses out people, body, planets, karma and
   time, and names the mind (SB 11.23.42). *Example:* Sameer's eleven readings. *Transition:* how does a reading
   become a storm?
2. **Emotions have a build-up.** Contemplation → attachment → desire → anger → forgetting → loss of judgement
   (BG 2.62–63). *Example:* re-reading, imagining, drafting, sending. *Transition:* where do we stand to stop it?
3. **Stand one step up.** Senses, mind, intelligence, self (BG 3.42); steady the mind by *deliberate* intelligence
   (BG 3.43). The storming mind is deaf to advice (SB 11.23.43), so intelligence acts before or after, not during.
   *Example:* the rule made in advance: "I don't reply within an hour." *Transition:* but doesn't control mean
   bottling up?
4. **Control is freedom, not suppression and not numbness.** Free from attachment *and* aversion (BG 2.64); neither
   rejoicing nor lamenting, yet "self-intelligent" (BG 5.20); the ocean that the rivers do not raise (BG 2.70).
   *Example:* the senior who hears the same comment and shrugs, not because he feels nothing, but because the level
   holds. *Transition:* how is this built?
5. **Bring it back, every time.** The method is return (BG 6.26), sustained by daily practice (BG 18.33); all the
   tradition's practices aim at this (SB 11.23.45). *Example:* a minute of attention to breath or sound, noticing
   the wander, returning. *Transition:* what did the brahmana finally do?
6. **Fix the mind on something that holds it.** His conclusion (SB 11.23.57); the mind as friend (BG 6.6).
   *Transition:* to the takeaway.

- **The ocean in the rainy season** (BG 2.70 purport). More water arrives; the level holds. *Connection:* the goal
  is steadiness, not emptiness.
- **Contemporary (invented):** Sameer's six hours (H).

## K. Anticipated questions and objections

> **Before you teach (pastoral guidance):** this talk is about everyday emotion. Persistent anxiety, panic attacks, long low mood,
> trauma or thoughts of self-harm are not "an uncontrolled mind" and are not solved by a technique. If a question or
> a face suggests this, acknowledge the person, don't analyse them in public, offer to talk afterwards, and say
> plainly that professional help is compatible with spiritual life. **Tele-MANAS, 14416, is a free 24-hour national
> mental-health helpline.**

1. **"If the mind is the cause, are you saying my pain is my fault?"**
   - No. The brahmana's verse (SB 11.23.42) locates the *lever*, not the blame. Others really did wrong him; the
     chapter says so. What he found was that his happiness and distress passed through his mind, and that was the
     one place he could work. Never quote the verse at someone who has been hurt.
2. **"Isn't controlling emotions unhealthy? Shouldn't I express them?"**
   - The Gita does not ask for suppression. BG 2.64 asks freedom from attachment *and* aversion; BG 2.70 says the
     rivers keep flowing. The research in O distinguishes suppressing a feeling from re-reading the situation; the
     Gita's method is the second (BG 3.43, "deliberate intelligence"). Expressing wisely and bottling up are both
     different from this.
3. **"I can't think clearly when I'm angry. How is intelligence supposed to help?"**
   - The text agrees: the storming mind is "insensitive to intelligent advice" (SB 11.23.43 purport). So
     intelligence works *before* (rules made in advance; cutting the first link, BG 2.62) and *after* (bringing the
     mind back, BG 6.26), and daily practice lengthens the gap between feeling and act (BG 18.33).
4. **"Is the goal to feel nothing?"**
   - No. BG 5.20 describes steadiness with intelligence intact; BG 2.70's ocean is full, not empty. The brahmana
     felt his ruin; he changed its reading (SB 11.23.28).
5. **"What is the practice, concretely?"**
   - BG 6.26: notice the wander, bring it back. All the tradition's practices aim at the mind (SB 11.23.45); the
     brahmana's own was fixing the mind in service of Krsna (SB 11.23.57). At VL1, the ten-minute mantra meditation
     at the end of the programme is offered as one way practitioners do this.
6. **"Why do I feel worse after hours online, even when nothing bad happened?"**
   - BG 17.16 purport: "The more we think of sense enjoyment, the more the mind becomes dissatisfied." The second
     study in O found that a wandering mind is associated with lower happiness in the moment. Feed the mind
     differently and watch.

## L. Case studies

**Case 1: The group chat.** Ananya's hostel group chat has split over a dinner plan. She was not mentioned, but she
has read the thread forty times, decided who her enemies are, and has not studied for two days.
- **Dilemma:** What happened, and where could it have been stopped?
- **Principles:** SB 11.23.42 (the mind as cause); BG 2.62–63 (the chain); SB 11.23.48 (sorting people into friends
  and enemies).
- **Questions:**
  - Walk Ananya's two days through the chain. Where did contemplation become attachment?
  - Which of the brahmana's crossed-out causes is she blaming?
  - What rule made in advance (BG 3.43) would have cut the first link?
- **Suggested analysis:** The event was nothing; the forty readings were everything. A rule ("I read the group
  once a day") is deliberate intelligence; a friend who studies with her is a change of what the mind is fed.
- **Takeaway:** The storm is made of re-readings.

**Case 2: The praise.** Vikram's professor praises him publicly. He is elated for a day, then anxious for a week
that he will not live up to it, and his work suffers.
- **Principles:** BG 5.20 (neither rejoice nor lament); BG 2.70 (the ocean); BG 2.62 (contemplation, this time of
  praise).
- **Questions:**
  - Why does the Gita list rejoicing beside lamenting?
  - What did Vikram contemplate, and what attachment grew from it?
  - What would the ocean look like in his case?
- **Takeaway:** The high and the crash are one system; steadiness covers both.

## M. Discussion and reflection

**M.1 Closing check questions** (easy first; praise every try)
1. In the story, who does the brahmana say is not the cause of his distress? *Other people (and the other causes he lists in SB 11.23.42); it is the mind.*
2. What is the first link in BG 2.62? *Contemplation.*
3. Does BG 2.64 mean to hate what disturbs us? *No: free from attachment and aversion.*
4. What does the ocean in BG 2.70 do when rivers flow in? *Stays steady.*
5. Why decide in advance (BG 3.43; SB 11.23.43)? *The storming mind does not listen; a rule made earlier does.*

**M.2 Activity (about 8 minutes): "Name it, step up"**
1. Read Sameer's story aloud; pairs find the chain steps in it (3 min).
2. Each writes one rule for next time ("when I feel the heat, I take three breaths and name it") (2 min).
3. Volunteers share a rule, not a story; thank each (2 min).
4. 1 min: link to BG 6.26: bring the mind back, every time.

**M.3 Reflection**
- **Reflection:** Think of your last storm. Where in the chain could you have stepped one rung up?
- **Application (private, optional):** Pick one rule to make in advance for the next provocation. *(Nobody needs to
  share.)*

# Part 2: For students

## S1. Summary

A two-second comment can ruin six hours. Who made the storm? The Bhagavatam tells of a brahmana (a learned priest) from Avanti who lost his wealth, was abandoned by his family and mocked in the street, and who then sang that the people around him were not the cause of his happiness and distress; it was the mind alone (SB 11.23.42). Events matter, but the mind's reading of them decides what we feel.

The Gita explains how a feeling grows: we dwell on something, grow attached, want it, get angry when blocked, forget ourselves and lose judgement (BG 2.62–63). The mind sits between the senses and the intelligence (BG 3.42), and the way to steady it is the "deliberate" use of intelligence (BG 3.43): making rules in advance, because a storming mind does not listen to advice at that moment (SB 11.23.43), and bringing the mind back gently every time it wanders (BG 6.26).

Control does not mean suppression or hatred of what disturbs us: the steady person is free from attachment and aversion (BG 2.64). It does not mean feeling nothing (BG 5.20). It means being like an ocean that is always being filled by rivers but stays still (BG 2.70). If a feeling is too heavy or lasts for weeks, talking to a qualified person is wise and goes together with spiritual life.

## S2. Key ideas

- The event is not the cause of my emotion; my mind's reading of it is (SB 11.23.42). That is a lever, not a blame.
- Emotions have a build-up: contemplation → attachment → desire → anger → forgetting (BG 2.62–63). Catch it early.
- Stand one step up: intelligence can observe the mind (BG 3.42–43). The storming mind is deaf to advice, so decide in advance.
- Control is freedom from attachment *and* aversion (BG 2.64), not suppression, not numbness (BG 5.20).
- The goal is the full ocean, not an empty one (BG 2.70). Rivers keep coming.
- The method is return: notice, name, bring it back (BG 6.26), sustained by daily practice (BG 18.33).

## S3. Verses to remember

- **SB 11.23.42:** "These people are not the cause of my happiness and distress… it is the mind alone…" *For me:*
  the lever is in my hand.
- **BG 2.62:** "While contemplating the objects of the senses, a person develops attachment for them…" *For me:*
  the storm is made of re-readings.
- **BG 6.26:** "From wherever the mind wanders… one must certainly withdraw it and bring it back…" *For me:* I
  don't have to stop the wandering; I have to return one more time.

## S4. Words to know

- **Mind (manas):** the part of us that reads, dwells and feels; sits between the senses and the intelligence (BG 3.42).
- **Steady intelligence (sthira-buddhi):** the steadiness of one who neither rejoices nor laments, with intelligence
  intact (BG 5.20 purport).
- **Bring it back:** the Gita's method for a wandering mind (BG 6.26).

## S5. Questions and answers

1. **If the mind is the cause, is my pain my fault?**
   No. The brahmana was really wronged. He found that his distress passed through his mind and that this was the one
   place he could work. The verse is a tool for you, never a weapon against someone who is hurt.
2. **Isn't controlling emotions unhealthy?**
   Suppression is. The Gita asks for freedom from attachment and aversion (BG 2.64), and says desires keep flowing
   (BG 2.70). The method is to read the situation differently in advance (BG 3.43), not to bottle it up.
3. **I can't think when I'm angry. How does intelligence help?**
   It can't, during the storm (SB 11.23.43 purport). So it works before (rules made in advance; cut the first link,
   BG 2.62) and after (bring it back, BG 6.26). Daily practice widens the gap (BG 18.33).
4. **Is the goal to feel nothing?**
   No. The steady person is "self-intelligent" and alive (BG 5.20); the ocean is full (BG 2.70). If low mood or
   anxiety lasts for weeks, talk to someone qualified; in India, Tele-MANAS (14416) is free, all hours; elsewhere, a local crisis line or doctor. That is compatible
   with spiritual life.

## S6. Check your understanding

1. In SB 11.23.42 the brahmana names the cause of happiness and distress as: (a) other people, (b) past karma,
   (c) the mind alone.
2. True or false: BG 2.64 says control means developing a strong aversion to the things that disturb you.
3. Put the chain of BG 2.62–63 in order: anger, contemplation, attachment, loss of intelligence, desire.
4. What does the ocean in BG 2.70 do when the rivers flow in?
5. In your own words: why does the Gita say to steady the mind by "deliberate" intelligence (BG 3.43)?

**Answers:** 1. (c) (SB 11.23.42). 2. False: "free from all attachment and aversion" (BG 2.64). 3. Contemplation →
attachment → desire → anger → (delusion, bewildered memory) → loss of intelligence (BG 2.62–63). 4. It is "ever
being filled but is always still" (BG 2.70): it stays steady. 5. Because the storming mind is deaf to advice
(SB 11.23.43 purport); the decision has to be made before, as a rule, and practised daily (BG 18.33).
