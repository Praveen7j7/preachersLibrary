## B. Why this topic belongs at this level

Builds on [DYS-2](dys-01-s2.md): once we accept that hearing from a reliable source is a way of knowing, we ask *what is worth hearing about*. This session's own angle is **relevance**: the problems that remain after technology, and the one question only humans ask. It leaves the proof of the soul to [DYS-4](dys-01-s4.md). VL2-06 (Vedic culture in practice) later covers how ISKCON re-establishes Vedic life; this page does not.

**Terms for readers new to IYF:** DYS = "Discover Your Self", the six-session opening course of IYF (ISKCON Youth Forum Delhi); VL1–VL3 = its successive course levels; SPS, DPS, BSS = its advanced study batches, BSS being for brahmacaris (celibate student-monks).

**Not done here:**
- The book's "Universal Government" lines pairing illness with misdeeds: cruel to anyone ill or bereaved.
- The book's statistics (youth illness, suicide, tsunami, Pompeii): see O. Never use suicide figures casually.
- Contempt for anyone's life. The point is the waste of a rare capacity, not that people are animals.

## C. Central message

Animals and humans share eating, sleeping, mating and defending; what is ours is the ability to ask "Why am I suffering, and what is life for?" Technology relieves symptoms of birth, old age, disease and death but cannot remove them; the Vedic literature speaks to the root, and that is why it stays relevant.

## D. Learning outcomes

1. Name the four activities humans share with animals and the one question that is ours (SB 1.2.10).
2. Name the four problems and the threefold miseries (BG 8.15 purport) with one example each.
3. Explain with the boil story why a stopgap is not a cure.
4. Retell the story of the king and the "fool".
5. Say in a sentence what the Vedic literature is for (who I am, how I relate to God, what the goal is).

## E. Scriptural foundation

**SB 1.2.10** — Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/sb/1/2/10/
> "…a human being is meant for inquiry about the Absolute Truth. Nothing else should be the goal of one's works."
- Context: Suta Gosvami answering the sages: life's desires should not be for sense gratification.
- Teaching: The human form is for inquiry into the Absolute Truth; the body is maintained for that.
- Purport point: Seekers of the Absolute Truth are less drawn to sense gratification because the search absorbs them.
- Application (preacher's): The human difference is a difference in *question*, not worth.
- Use: in the talk.

**SB 5.5.1** — Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/sb/5/5/1/
> "…one who has been awarded this human form should not work hard day and night simply for sense gratification, which is available even for dogs and hogs…"
- Context: Rsabhadeva instructing His sons.
- Teaching: Human life is not for struggling after pleasures animals also have, but for lasting happiness.
- Purport point: Prabhupada describes the commuter who works all day for the evening's eating, drinking and sleeping.
- Application (preacher's): This is the scriptural form of the book's "eating, sleeping, mating, defending" comparison. Teach it without contempt.
- Use: in the talk.

**BG 8.15** — Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/8/15/
> "…never return to this temporary world, which is full of miseries…"
- Purport point: names the miseries: "birth, old age, disease and death", the book's four real problems.
- Use: in the talk, to name the four problems with a source.

**BG 7.3** — Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/7/3/ — Purport: mankind is absorbed in "eating, sleeping, defending and mating". Use: background.

## F. Philosophical explanation

1. **What is human life for?** Eating, sleeping, mating, defending are common to animals; the human capacity is to ask about suffering and the Absolute (SB 1.2.10; SB 5.5.1; BG 7.3 purport). The *Hitopadesa*/*Mahabharata* verse the book quotes (*ahara-nidra-bhaya-maithunam…*) is **Requires source review**: the book attributes it to the Mahabharata; it is usually cited from the Hitopadesa.
2. **Four problems, three miseries.** Birth, old age, disease, death (BG 8.15 purport). The threefold miseries, a classification Prabhupada uses throughout his purports: from one's own body and mind, from other beings, from nature.
3. **Symptom vs root.** Technology and medicine relieve symptoms, wonderfully; the book's boil story (impure blood keeps producing boils) says the root is deeper. This is a point about the *question*, not a criticism of medicine.
4. **Why the Vedas are relevant.** The three questions the literature answers (Sanskrit *sambandha*: who am I and how do I relate to God; abhidheya: the practice; prayojana: the goal), defined simply. The problems are unchanged, so an answer that addresses them is not dated. Say "the tradition holds".
5. **The cause of suffering.** The tradition says we suffer partly from forgetting our real position (CC Madhya 20.117, R.1); at DYS level say only "suffering has a cause worth asking about".
6. **Misunderstandings.** "Spiritual people reject comfort": SB 1.2.10 says maintain the body. "Suffering is a punishment": never say it to a person who suffers. "Old means outdated": age is no argument either way; ask what the text says.
7. **Pitch.** Warm, non-judging; everyone has worked hard for a goal that did not last.

## H. Opening and engagement

**H.1 How to start (first 5 minutes)**
1. Greet; link back: "last time we saw we have limits and can learn from a reliable source."
2. Ask H.2 question 1; collect on the board.
3. Circle everything animals also do; note what is left.
4. Say: "Today's question is not whether the old books are old; it is whether the problems are."
5. Put the session question up: what is life for?

**Adapting for your audience:** the promotion case suits office life. For a village or working audience, use the farmer who toils for the harvest, sells it, and begins again; for a student, an exam rank that brings relief for a week. Keep it respectful: nobody's work is despised.

**H.2 Opening questions**
- What can you do that no animal can? (Most answers are technology; steer to the question only we ask.)
- What did you work hardest for, and how long did the feeling last?
- If you could remove one problem from life, which would it be?

## I. Core teaching points

1. **The human question** (SB 1.2.10; 5.5.1). *Transition:* what stops us from asking?
2. **Four problems no technology removes** (BG 8.15). *Transition:* is relief a cure?
3. **Symptom vs root** (boil story). *Transition:* where is the root addressed?
4. **The literature's three subjects.** *Transition:* the first answer: who am I? (DYS-4).

## J. Stories and illustrations

- **The king and the "fool"** (book ch. 3; a teaching story, source not given). Tell it in your own words; lesson: the greatest folly is never to ask where one is going.
- **The boil and the impure blood** (book ch. 3; a teaching story). Symptom vs root.
- **Siddhartha sees old age, sickness and death** (traditional account; Encyclopedia transcript 0013). The four problems are *seen*, not read. Make no claim about Buddhist doctrine.
- **The blindfolded pinch** (Encyclopedia transcript 0013): with consent, gently tap a blindfolded volunteer's arm; he never says "the pain came by itself". A light classroom illustration, not an argument.

## K. Anticipated questions and objections

1. **"Aren't the Vedas outdated?"** Dates do not decide truth either way; the question is whether the problems they address are outdated. They are not.
2. **"Doesn't science keep improving life?"** Yes, gratefully. Medicine delays and eases; the four remain.
3. **"Animals also think and feel."** Agreed. The difference is the *question*, not worth (SB 1.2.10).
4. **"Is suffering a punishment?"** Do not say so. Say suffering has causes worth asking about; compassion first.
5. **"If life is for inquiry, should I drop my career?"** No; SB 1.2.10 says maintain the body as needed for the inquiry.

## L. Case studies

*Invented case for discussion. Not a real incident.*

**The promotion.** Arjun gets the promotion he worked towards for five years. Two weeks later he feels as he did before, and slightly afraid of the next target.
- **Dilemma:** the goal was reached; the feeling did not change.
- **Principles:** the four activities done better are still the four (SB 5.5.1); symptom vs root.
- **Questions:** What problem did the promotion solve? What did it not touch? What question has Arjun never asked?
- **Analysis:** Neither the promotion nor Arjun is a failure; one capacity is unused.
- **Takeaway:** Human life has a capacity a career cannot use up (SB 1.2.10).

## M. Discussion and reflection

**M.1 Closing check questions**
1. Name two activities humans share with animals. *Expected:* eating, sleeping, mating, defending.
2. What is the human question? *Expected:* Why do I suffer, what is life for?
3. Name the four problems. *Expected:* birth, old age, disease, death.
4. What is the difference between a symptom and a root? *Expected:* the boil: relieving the boil vs the impure blood.
5. Is the aim to despise ordinary life? *Expected:* No; the aim is to use the rare capacity.

**M.2 Activities (engagement block, ~90 min; choose two)**
- **A. Role-play: the king and the "fool" (30 min).** Three roles (king, "fool", narrator). Rehearse 10 min, play 5 min, then discuss: where would I ask the real question? Praise every performance.
- **B. Symptom or root? (25 min).** Groups sort ten everyday problems on cards (invented, e.g. "tired every afternoon", "feel lonely in a crowd") into "relieved by a stopgap" and "needs a root answer"; share one.
- **C. Pair talk and share (30 min).** "A goal I reached that did not last as long as I hoped." Each listens 3 min, restates in a sentence; volunteers share only what they wish.

**M.3 Reflection (private, optional).** Write your own honest answer to "what is my life for?" in one line.

# Part 2: For students

## S1. Summary
Look at what humans and animals do: eat, sleep, mate and defend. Animals do these very well, sometimes better than we do. What only a human can do is ask "Why am I suffering? Who am I? What is life for?" The Bhagavatam says human life is meant for that inquiry (SB 1.2.10), and not only for the pleasures that "dogs and hogs" also have (SB 5.5.1). This is not contempt for anyone; it is a reminder of how rare our capacity is.

Everyone, rich or poor, meets four problems: birth, old age, disease and death (BG 8.15). Technology and medicine help us enormously, but they ease symptoms. In the story of the boil, a boil keeps coming back because the blood is impure; treating the boil is not treating the blood. In the story of the king and the "fool", the so-called fool saw that the king, busy with everything, had never asked where he was going.

The Vedic literature stays relevant because the problems have not changed. It answers three things: who I am and how I relate to God, how to live that relationship, and what the goal is. Nobody is asked to drop their career; the body is to be looked after so the question can be asked.

## S2. Key ideas
- Humans share eating, sleeping, mating, defending with animals.
- The human difference is the question: who am I, why do I suffer?
- Four problems remain: birth, old age, disease, death.
- Relief of symptoms is not removal of the root.
- Old books are relevant if their subject, our problems, is still with us.

## S3. Verses to remember
- **SB 1.2.10:** "…a human being is meant for inquiry about the Absolute Truth." *For me:* my questions are the purpose of my life, not a distraction.
- **SB 5.5.1:** do not work hard "simply for sense gratification". *For me:* use my rare capacity.
- **BG 8.15:** the world is "full of miseries". *For me:* name the problem honestly.

## S4. Words to know
- **Absolute Truth:** the final reality behind everything.
- **Threefold miseries:** from my own body and mind, from other beings, from nature.
- **Sambandha, abhidheya, prayojana:** who I am and how I relate to God; the practice; the goal.

## S5. Questions and answers
1. **Aren't the Vedas outdated?** Dates do not decide truth. The problems they address are still here.
2. **Doesn't science solve these?** It eases a great deal; the four remain.
3. **Do animals think too?** Yes; the difference is the question we can ask.
4. **Is suffering a punishment?** We do not say so. Suffering has causes worth asking about.
5. **Must I give up my career?** No. Look after the body so the inquiry can happen.

## S6. Check your understanding
1. Name two activities humans share with animals.
2. What is the human question?
3. Name the four problems.
4. In the boil story, what is the root?
5. Does SB 1.2.10 ask us to neglect the body?

**Answers:** 1. Eating, sleeping, mating, defending. 2. Who am I and why do I suffer (SB 1.2.10). 3. Birth, old age, disease, death (BG 8.15). 4. The impure blood. 5. No; it is maintained for the inquiry.
