# World cup of life

> **VL1 · 45–60 min · review: sources checked, awaiting senior review.** Scripture light but exact. IYF holds one deck (HG Sundar Gopal Das, "The
> World Cup of Our Life", about 70 slides): a tournament image ("happiness ratio, league to final"), the
> anonymous "paradox of our time" poem, a long run of crisis statistics, a "root causes" slide (lust, greed,
> anger, envy, illusion), and "technology + spirituality = championship". This page keeps the tournament image
> and the root-causes slide, points the synthesis to VL1-39, and leaves out the statistics and the "great
> scientists on God" quotations (several are unsourced or misattributed). Not sensitive. Mood: light, playful
> about cricket, serious about the question underneath.

## B. Why this topic belongs at this level

The tournament is the audience's native metaphor: rankings, cut-offs, knockouts. IYF's deck opens with a simple
arithmetic (fourteen teams happy at the league stage, one at the end) that everyone feels in their own life. The
Gita describes exactly this kind of happiness, "nectar at first but poison at the end" (BG 18.38), and then
describes another kind, and a way to play that keeps a person steady through both results (BG 2.14, 2.70). At
VL1 this is an attractive question, not a doctrine.

Builds on vl1-21 (the two kinds of happiness, BG 18.37–38 and 5.22, taught in depth there), vl1-05 (BG 2.70 and 16.21) and
vl1-41 (the player's rules, BG 2.47–48): keep this page to its own angle, the tournament arithmetic (one cup, many losers, the cup
expires) and the cup that does not expire; give those verses one line each and link the earlier pages.

- The "scientists who believed in God" quotations (Einstein, Newton, Kelvin, Compton, Anfinsen and others):

## C. Central message

Life as the world runs it is a tournament: one cup, many losers, and even the winner's cup expires. The Gita
calls that kind of happiness "nectar at first but poison at the end", and describes another kind, hard at the
start and sweet at the end, a gain after which "there is no greater gain". The real World Cup of life is that
win, more than one person can hold it, and the way to it is to play every match steadily, winning or losing.

## D. Learning outcomes

By the end, participants should be able to:
1. Explain the deck's "happiness ratio" (fourteen teams at the league stage, one at the end) and say what it
   shows about outcome-based happiness.
2. Distinguish the two kinds of happiness in BG 18.37–38 and give one example of each from their week.
3. State why the Gita calls sense pleasure a source of misery (BG 5.22) without calling pleasure itself bad.
4. Describe the "win that doesn't expire" in the Gita's own words (BG 6.20–23, 9.2) and why more than one can
   hold it.
5. Name one way to play the next match steadily (BG 2.14, 2.70) and one root cause to watch (BG 16.21).

## E. Scriptural foundation

**Use:** BG 18.37–38 and 5.22 carry the "tournament" half; BG 6.20–23 and 9.2 the "real cup"; BG 2.14 and 2.70
the "how to play". BG 16.21 and 2.66 are for the deck's root-causes slide and for questions.

**BG 18.38**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/18/38/
> "That happiness which is derived from contact of the senses with their objects and which appears like nectar at first but poison at the end is said to be of the nature of passion."
- **Context:** Chapter 18 classifies happiness by the three modes.
- **Teaching:** Happiness from sense contact is nectar first, poison at the end: the mode of passion.
- **Purport point:** The purport's example is a young couple whose attraction "may be very pleasing to the
  senses, but at the end … becomes just like poison"; such happiness "should be avoided by all means".
- **Application (preacher's):** This is the trophy. The verse does not say the nectar is fake; it says it
  turns. At VL1 use the tournament, not the purport's example of a relationship.
- **Use:** in the talk (the tournament's cup).

**BG 18.37**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/18/37/
> "That which in the beginning may be just like poison but at the end is just like nectar and which awakens one to self-realization is said to be happiness in the mode of goodness."
- **Teaching:** Happiness in goodness is poison-like at first, nectar at the end, and awakens self-realisation.
- **Purport point:** The rules to control mind and senses "are very difficult, bitter like poison, but if one is
  successful … he begins to drink real nectar, and he enjoys life."
- **Application (preacher's):** The other curve. Every athlete knows it: training is the poison, the form is the
  nectar. The Gita says the same shape applies to the inner life.
- **Use:** in the talk.

**BG 5.22**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/5/22/
> "An intelligent person does not take part in the sources of misery, which are due to contact with the material senses. O son of Kuntī, such pleasures have a beginning and an end, and so the wise man does not delight in them."
- **Teaching:** Sense pleasures have a beginning and an end, and so are sources of misery; the wise do not delight
  in them.
- **Purport point:** "A liberated soul is not interested in anything which is temporary." The purport quotes
  SB 5.5.1 on not labouring hard for pleasures available to animals, and ends: "The more one is addicted to
  material pleasures, the more he is entrapped by material miseries."
- **Application (preacher's):** The cup expires: that is the whole argument. Not "pleasure is sin" but "pleasure
  with an end built in becomes misery". Keep the tone observational.
- **Use:** in the talk.

**BG 6.20-23**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/6/20-23/
> "…upon gaining this he thinks there is no greater gain. Being situated in such a position, one is never shaken, even in the midst of greatest difficulty. This indeed is actual freedom from all miseries arising from material contact."
- **Context:** Chapter 6 describes the perfected state of yoga.
- **Teaching:** A gain after which one thinks there is no greater gain; in it one is not shaken by the greatest
  difficulty; this is freedom from material misery.
- **Purport point:** The purport says this transcendental pleasure "is real life" and "is easily achieved by
  execution of devotional service, or bhakti-yoga."
- **Application (preacher's):** The real cup: a win that does not expire and is not shaken by the next match.
  At VL1 present the verse as a description to be curious about, not a claim to be accepted.
- **Use:** in the talk (the real cup).

**BG 9.2**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/9/2/
> "This knowledge is the king of education, the most secret of all secrets. It is the purest knowledge, and because it gives direct perception of the self by realization, it is the perfection of religion. It is everlasting, and it is joyfully performed."
- **Teaching:** This knowledge is the king of education, gives direct perception of the self, is everlasting and
  joyfully performed.
- **Purport point:** "there is, unfortunately, no university or educational institution where the science of
  the spirit soul is instructed. Yet the soul is the most important part of the body".
- **Application (preacher's):** Two words for the audience: "everlasting" (unlike the cup) and "joyfully
  performed" (unlike the poison-first stereotype of spiritual life). The 18.37 curve has a cheerful version.
- **Use:** in the talk, one line.

**BG 2.14**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/2/14/
> "O son of Kuntī, the nonpermanent appearance of happiness and distress, and their disappearance in due course, are like the appearance and disappearance of winter and summer seasons. They arise from sense perception, O scion of Bharata, and one must learn to tolerate them without being disturbed."
- **Purport point:** "one has to learn to tolerate nonpermanent appearances and disappearances of happiness and
  distress" while discharging one's duty.
- **Application (preacher's):** How to play every match: results are seasons. Tolerance here is a skill, not
  passivity.
- **Use:** in the talk (how to play).

**BG 2.70**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/2/70/
> "A person who is not disturbed by the incessant flow of desires – that enter like rivers into the ocean, which is ever being filled but is always still – can alone achieve peace, and not the man who strives to satisfy such desires."
- **Purport point:** "Desires may come to him like the waters of the rivers that flow into the ocean, but he is
  steady in his activities"; "The fruitive workers, the salvationists, and also the yogis who are after mystic
  powers are all unhappy because of unfulfilled desires."
- **Application (preacher's):** The ocean is the player who is already full; the rivers are the next cup, the
  next rank. Peace belongs to the full, not to the chasers.
- **Use:** in the talk (how to play).

**BG 16.21**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/16/21/
> "There are three gates leading to this hell – lust, anger and greed. Every sane man should give these up, for they lead to the degradation of the soul."
- **Purport point:** "One tries to satisfy his lust, and when he cannot, anger and greed arise."
- **Application (preacher's):** The deck's "root causes" slide (lust, greed, anger, envy, illusion) in its
  scriptural form: three gates. At VL1 soften "hell" to "a hellish state", as VL1-17 does. Use for the question
  "why does the world's tournament go wrong?".
- **Use:** in the talk, one point.

**BG 2.66**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/2/66/
> "…neither transcendental intelligence nor a steady mind, without which there is no possibility of peace. And how can there be any happiness without peace?"
- **Purport point:** "Disturbance is due to want of an ultimate goal".
- **Application (preacher's):** Why a society (or a person) with no final goal cannot be at peace however many
  cups it wins.
- **Use:** background; one line if asked.

## F. Philosophical explanation

1. **The principle.** Happiness that comes from contact with its object has an end built in and so becomes
   misery (BG 5.22); it is nectar first and poison later (18.38). The tournament makes this visible: one cup,
   many losers, and the cup itself expires. The Gita describes a second kind, hard first and sweet later
   (18.37), culminating in a gain after which "there is no greater gain" and which no difficulty shakes
   (6.20–23), "everlasting" and "joyfully performed" (9.2). The way to it is to play every match steadily:
   results as seasons (2.14), the self as an ocean the rivers do not disturb (2.70), and the three gates (lust,
   anger, greed) watched (16.21).
2. **Why it matters.** A generation is being trained to want a cup that only one can hold and nobody can keep.
   Naming the arithmetic frees people; naming the alternative gives them somewhere to go.
3. **Connections.** VL1-21 (pleasure hunt), VL1-41 (the player's rules), VL1-39 (the deck's synthesis),
   VL1-17/20 (anger and greed, two of the gates), VL2-03 (the argument in full).
4. **Consequences.** Someone who accepts this stops confusing the cup with the game; enjoys sport and success
   without staking their worth on them; expects the 18.37 curve from any inner practice and does not quit at the
   poison stage; and checks the three gates when their own tournament turns ugly.
5. **Misunderstandings to prevent.**
   - **"The Gita is against enjoyment."** It classifies enjoyment (18.37–38) and recommends the kind that lasts.
     9.2: "joyfully performed".
   - **"So don't compete."** Arjuna competed. Play fully (VL1-41); just know which cup is which.
   - **"Spiritual happiness is for after death."** 6.20–23 describes a yogi "never shaken, even in the midst of
     greatest difficulty", a state of this life; the purport to 9.2 says one "can perceive the results directly".
   - **"Technology caused the crisis."** The deck's statistics suggest this; the Gita's diagnosis is the three
     gates (16.21), not the tools. VL1-39 handles the tools.
6. **Pitch for VL1.** Enjoy the cricket. No Sanskrit except *sattva*/*rajas* if the room asks what "goodness" and
   "passion" mean (one line: two of the three qualities of nature). Never tell the room their enjoyment is
   "poison"; let the verse say it, and let them test it.

## H. Opening and engagement

**H.1 How to start (first 5 minutes)**
1. Greet, give your name, and say the talk uses the idea of a tournament to ask what a lasting win is.
2. Ask the opening question silently; nobody shares.
3. Ask H.2 question 2 and take two or three answers; thank each.
4. If someone names a sport, use it; if not, use any local contest (a school final, a village match, a business pitch).
5. Use the transition line below and begin "The arithmetic".

**H.2 Opening questions** (about the room's own life)
1. What is your personal "final": the one result you think would settle things? (Do not share.)
2. How long did the happiness of the last big win last?
3. Have you ever been happy for someone who beat you? What made that possible?

- **Opening question:** "What is your personal World Cup right now: the one result that, if it came, you think
  would settle things? Don't share it. Now: how long did the last such result keep you happy?" Pause. "That
  half-life is the subject."
- **Relatable scenario (invented):** Dev gets the placement everyone wanted. For three days he is the happiest
  person on campus. On day four he learns what the batchmate at the other company is earning. The cup is still
  on his desk; the nectar has gone somewhere.
- **Transition:** "The Gita has a two-verse description of exactly this curve, and of a second curve that runs
  the other way."
- **Why it matters to this audience:** They will play many tournaments. Knowing which cup expires and which does
  not is the difference between a life of seasons and a life of defeats.

## I. Core teaching points

1. **The arithmetic of the cup.** Fourteen happy, then eight, four, one; and the one has it for a season (IYF's
   slide). *Example:* Dev's three days. *Transition:* the Gita names this curve.
2. **Nectar first, poison later.** Happiness from "contact of the senses with their objects" is "like nectar at
   first but poison at the end" (BG 18.38); it has "a beginning and an end" (BG 5.22). *Example:* the cup on
   the desk, day four. *Transition:* is there another curve?
3. **Poison first, nectar later.** "In the beginning … like poison but at the end … like nectar" (BG 18.37).
   *Example:* every training season; also every inner practice. *Transition:* why does the world's tournament
   turn nasty?
4. **Three gates.** Lust, anger and greed (BG 16.21): the deck's root causes, in the Gita's words. *Example:* the
   fan who breaks a TV; the student who sabotages a friend's notes. *Transition:* and the real cup?
5. **A gain with no greater gain.** "Upon gaining this he thinks there is no greater gain … never shaken"
   (BG 6.20–23); "everlasting … joyfully performed" (BG 9.2). *Example:* nobody has to lose for this one to be
   won. *Transition:* how do we play on the way?
6. **Seasons and oceans.** Results come and go "like … winter and summer seasons" (BG 2.14); the full person is
   "like rivers into the ocean" (BG 2.70). *Example:* the same player after a win and a loss. *Transition:* one
   experiment.

## J. Stories and illustrations

- **Dev's cup** (invented, above). *Connection:* BG 18.38, 5.22.
- **The ocean and the rivers** (BG 2.70, the verse's own image). *Connection:* fullness, not chasing, gives peace.
- **The ball never wins** (Chaitanya Charan, *The Cricket of Life*, VOICE essay; supporting, cite as his): the
  ball is "sometimes kissed, sometimes beaten"; teams win and lose; the ball never wins. *Connection:* when we
  identify with the team (body, company, country) we are the ball. An essay, not scripture; use briefly.
- **IYF's "paradox of our time"** ("taller buildings, shorter tempers…"): an anonymous piece circulated online
  since the late 1990s, often misattributed to George Carlin or to a Seattle pastor. *If used, label it
  anonymous and do not attribute it.* *Connection:* the cups multiplied, the peace did not (2.66).
- **Left out:** the deck's crime, suicide, corruption, consumption and disaster statistics (unsourced); the
  Union of Concerned Scientists' 1992 "Warning to Humanity" (real, but not this talk's business); the "great
  scientists" quotations (VL2-08, with sources).

## K. Anticipated questions and objections

1. **"You're telling me not to enjoy cricket / success?"**
   - No. The Gita classifies happiness (18.37–38) rather than banning it, and calls the lasting kind "joyfully
     performed" (9.2). Enjoy the match; don't stake your worth on the cup.
2. **"Isn't 'poison at the end' an exaggeration? I enjoyed my result."**
   - For three days (Dev). The verse describes a curve, not a lie: the nectar is real and it turns (18.38). Test
     it against your own last big result.
3. **"If only one can win, isn't competition itself the problem?"**
   - Competition is fine; Arjuna competed (VL1-41). The problem is a cup that only one can hold and nobody can
     keep, and the three gates that open when we chase it (16.21). The Gita's cup has no such limit.
4. **"'No greater gain' (6.20–23): that sounds like a promise nobody can check."**
   - Fair. The Gita presents it as a state reached "by practice of yoga" (6.20–23), and the 18.37 curve says the
     start is hard. The honest offer at VL1 is an experiment, not a guarantee: try one small practice for a
     month and see which curve it follows.
5. **"The deck blames technology for all the crises."**
   - The Gita blames the three gates, not the tools (16.21). VL1-39 takes technology properly; here, keep the
     tools out of the dock.
6. **"How do I stop one bad result from ruining my month?"**
   - 2.14: a result is a season, to be tolerated "without being disturbed"; 2.70: be the ocean, not the river.
     Practically: name the result, name what is still yours (the work, VL1-41), and do the next day's duty.

## L. Case studies

*Invented cases for discussion. Not real incidents.*

**Case 1: Day four.** Dev wins the placement everyone wanted and is euphoric for three days. On day four he
learns what a batchmate is earning and the cup stops shining.
- **Dilemma:** Was the happiness real, and what did it depend on?
- **Principles:** BG 18.38 (nectar first, poison later); BG 5.22 (an end built in); BG 2.70 (the chaser vs the
  ocean).
- **Questions:** Plot Dev's happiness over the week. What turned it: the result, or the comparison? What would an
  "ocean" response to the batchmate's salary look like?
- **Takeaway:** A cup that depends on others losing cannot be kept.

**Case 2: The poison stage.** Meera started a daily ten-minute practice (reading, sitting quietly) a week ago.
It is boring and she sees no result. She is about to quit.
- **Principles:** BG 18.37 (poison first, nectar later); BG 9.2 (joyfully performed).
- **Questions:** Which curve is Meera on, and where? What does every athlete know about week one? What would a
  fair trial length be before judging?
- **Takeaway:** The real cup's curve starts low; quitting at week one is judging the final by the first over.

## M. Discussion and reflection

**M.1 Closing check questions** (easy first; praise every try)
1. In the talk's arithmetic, how many winners are there at the end? *One cup; many others go home.*
2. How does happiness "like nectar at first" end (BG 18.38)? *As poison.*
3. How does the other kind begin and end (BG 18.37)? *Like poison at first, like nectar at the end.*
4. Can more than one person hold the lasting win (BG 6.20–23, 9.2)? *Yes; nobody has to lose.*
5. What are the three gates in BG 16.21? *Lust, anger, greed.*

**M.2 Activity (about 8 minutes): "Two curves"**
1. 2 min: each draws two curves on paper: one happiness that starts high and falls, one that starts hard and rises.
2. 3 min: in pairs, each labels one example of each from their own week (for example, a late-night feed against a regular practice, a skill or sport).
3. 2 min: volunteers share one example; sharing is optional. Thank each.
4. 1 min: link to the experiment in P.

**M.3 Reflection**
- **Comprehension:** What are the two kinds of happiness in BG 18.37–38 and how does each begin and end? Why does
  BG 5.22 call sense pleasure a source of misery? What are the three gates in 16.21?
- **Reflection:** Which of my current goals is a cup only one can hold? Which of my pleasures has turned from
  nectar to poison in the last year?
- **Application (private):** One practice to run for a month on the 18.37 curve. *(Nobody needs to share.)*

## P. Practical takeaway

- **Main lesson:** The world's cup: one winner, and it expires. The Gita's cup: hard first, sweet later, "no
  greater gain", and more than one can hold it. Play every match as a season, full as the ocean.
- **One action (VL1):** Two things for a month. (1) After your next result, good or bad, write down on day one
  and day four how it feels; see the curve for yourself. (2) Start one ten-minute daily practice (a verse, a
  quiet sit, a mantra) and do not judge it before thirty days; you are testing the other curve.
- **Reflection question:** Which of my goals could everyone in this room reach without anyone losing?
- **Follow-up:** VL1-41 "Modern Kurukshetra"; VL1-39 "Spirituality and technology"; VL1-21 "Pleasure hunt".
  Reading: *Bhagavad-gita As It Is* 18.36–39, 5.22, 6.20–23 and 2.70 with purports.

# Part 2: For students

## S1. Summary

Life as the world runs it is a tournament: fourteen teams are happy at the league stage, one at the end, and even
that one holds the cup for a season. The Bhagavad-gita describes this kind of happiness exactly: it comes from
"contact of the senses with their objects" and is "like nectar at first but poison at the end" (BG 18.38),
because such pleasures "have a beginning and an end" (BG 5.22). It also describes another kind, "like poison" at the start and
"like nectar" at the end (BG 18.37), every athlete's training curve, which leads to a gain after which "there is
no greater gain" and which no difficulty shakes (BG 6.20–23), "everlasting" and "joyfully performed" (BG 9.2).
That is the real World Cup of life, and nobody has to lose for you to win it. On the way, play every match
steadily: results are seasons (BG 2.14), the full person is an ocean the rivers do not disturb (BG 2.70), and
three gates, lust, anger and greed, are what turn any tournament ugly (BG 16.21).

You can use this wherever you live: a village contest, a school final or a business deal all have one winner and many who go home. The talk does not say
"do not compete". It asks which of your happinesses last, and invites one experiment: pick one habit that is hard now and may grow sweeter, and keep it for a month. This
is an invitation to test, not a belief you must accept first.

## S2. Key ideas

- One cup, many losers, and the cup expires: 14 → 8 → 4 → 1.
- Nectar first, poison later: happiness from sense contact (BG 18.38); it has an end built in (BG 5.22).
- Poison first, nectar later: the training curve of the inner life (BG 18.37).
- The real cup: "no greater gain … never shaken" (BG 6.20–23); "everlasting … joyfully performed" (BG 9.2).
- How to play: results are seasons (BG 2.14); be the ocean (BG 2.70); watch the three gates (BG 16.21).

## S3. Verses to remember

- **BG 18.38:** Happiness from the senses "appears like nectar at first but poison at the end". *For me:* enjoy
  the result; don't stake my worth on it.
- **BG 18.37:** "That which in the beginning may be just like poison but at the end is just like nectar…" *For
  me:* don't quit a good practice at week one.
- **BG 2.70:** The person "not disturbed by the incessant flow of desires – that enter like rivers into the
  ocean … can alone achieve peace". *For me:* be the ocean, not the river.

## S4. Words to know

- **Goodness (sattva) and passion (rajas):** two of the three qualities of material nature that BG 18.37–38
  use to classify happiness. One line each; VL2 explains the three.

## S5. Questions and answers

1. **Are you telling me not to enjoy cricket or success?**
   No. The Gita classifies happiness (BG 18.37–38) and calls the lasting kind "joyfully performed" (BG 9.2).
   Enjoy the match; don't stake your worth on the cup.
2. **"Poison at the end" sounds exaggerated.**
   Test it: how long did your last big result keep you happy? The nectar is real, and it turns (BG 18.38).
3. **Is competition itself the problem?**
   No; Arjuna competed. The problem is a cup only one can hold and nobody can keep, and the three gates that open
   when we chase it (BG 16.21).
4. **Can anyone check "no greater gain" (BG 6.20–23)?**
   It is described as a state reached by practice, with a hard start (BG 18.37). Try one small practice for a
   month and see which curve it follows.
5. **How do I stop one bad result ruining my month?**
   Treat it as a season (BG 2.14); be the ocean (BG 2.70); name what is still yours, the work, and do tomorrow's
   duty.

## S6. Check your understanding

1. According to BG 18.38, happiness from the senses is like what at first, and like what at the end?
2. What does BG 5.22 say such pleasures have, which makes them sources of misery?
3. True or false: BG 18.37 says happiness in goodness is pleasant from the very start.
4. Name the three gates in BG 16.21.
5. In your own words: why can more than one person hold the Gita's cup, when only one team can hold the World Cup?

**Answers:** 1. Nectar at first, poison at the end (BG 18.38). 2. "A beginning and an end" (BG 5.22). 3. False:
"in the beginning … just like poison but at the end … just like nectar" (BG 18.37). 4. Lust, anger and greed
(BG 16.21). 5. Because it is a state of the self, a gain after which one thinks "there is no greater gain" (BG 6.20–23),
"everlasting" (BG 9.2); it is not a trophy that must be taken from someone else.
