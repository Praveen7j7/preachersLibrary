# Eight practical tips for positive thinking

> **VL1 · 45–60 min · review: sources checked, awaiting senior review.** Sensitive: "positive thinking" talks can wound someone who is
> depressed or anxious. Read the note in K before teaching. Scripture light but exact: the eight tips are
> IYF's; each is tied to one verse that actually supports it.

## A. Topic overview

| | |
|---|---|
| Batch / level | VL1 (Vedic Level 1), a stand-alone talk |
| Suggested duration | 45–60 min |
| Preparation time | About 90 min, the first time |
| Difficulty | Introductory |
| Prerequisites | None (pairs well with VL1-10 "BG and Art of Work") |
| Audience | Students and young professionals; the original IYF talk was given to a Rotary club and to students. Some listeners may be under real stress or low mood |
| Teaching objective | Give eight usable habits of mind, each rooted in a Gita teaching, so that the listener leaves with one to try and with the sense that this tradition understands the mind |

## B. Why this topic belongs at this level

"Positive thinking" is a phrase this audience already trusts and already half-distrusts: they have seen it
sold as a slogan. IYF's talk takes the phrase and gives it a floor. The slides (VOICE, "Eight practical
tips for positive thinking", 2007) open with a contrast borrowed from management writing: a **character
ethic** (simplicity, integrity, non-envy, patience, humility, trust as the foundation of success) versus a
**personality ethic** (techniques and attitudes without the character underneath). Their point:
"Positive thinking would be meaningless without the right map." The eight tips are the map.

At VL1 the aim is **attraction through usefulness and honesty**: the tips must work on Monday, and the talk
must admit what positive thinking cannot do (K).

**Builds on vl1-09 (mind as friend or enemy, BG 6.5–6) and vl1-10 (effort without attachment to results, BG 2.47–48):** recap each in one minute and link to its page; do not re-teach the arguments or reuse their examples. This page's own angle is eight small habits of thought.

The eight tips, as IYF lists them:
1. Give up unreasonable expectations.
2. Success is not in the trophy won but in the race run.
3. Give up unhealthy comparison.
4. Count the blessings of God upon you.
5. Don't be overly bothered about what the world thinks of you.
6. Tolerate the *svabhava* (nature) of others.
7. Give up the blame game; see the sunny side of people.
8. Desires should be neither expressed nor suppressed, but sublimated.

How the subject grows at other levels:

| Level | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **VL1** (here) | Eight habits, each tied to one verse; the mind as friend or enemy (BG 6.5–6); satisfaction as an austerity of the mind (BG 17.16) |
| VL2 | The mind and the self; why untrained thinking returns |
| VL3 | The mind's wandering and how practice steadies it (BG 6.26, 6.34–35; see VL1-12) |
| SPS | Tip 8 in full: higher taste (BG 2.59), Ambarisa's engagement of every sense (SB 9.4.18–20) as a devotional method |
| DPS | Leading others without blame or comparison |
| BSS | The six urges (NOI 1) and sublimation in student-monk life |

**Not done here:**
- Presenting positive thinking as a cure for depression or anxiety (see K).
- The reincarnation and soul section that occupies the middle of IYF's slides. One sentence from it is kept
  under tip 5 (the body is temporary; the self is not).
- Pressing anyone to chant. The slides end with mantra meditation; here it is mentioned under tip 8 as
  what practitioners do.

## C. Central message

The mind can be your best friend or your worst enemy (BG 6.6), and the difference is trainable. Eight small
habits, each one found in the Gita, move the mind from complaint and comparison toward satisfaction, and
satisfaction is the Gita's definition of a disciplined mind (BG 17.16).

## D. Learning outcomes

By the end, participants should be able to:
1. Name at least five of the eight tips and the one verse behind each.
2. Explain BG 6.5–6 in plain words: the same mind can lift me or sink me, depending on who is in charge.
3. Distinguish three ways of handling a desire (express, suppress, sublimate) and say why the Gita
   prefers the third (BG 2.59; BG 2.61).
4. Say honestly what positive thinking is *not* (a treatment for illness, or a reason to accept
   injustice).
5. Choose one tip to practise for a week.

## E. Scriptural foundation

**Use:** the eight tips each need one verse; BG 6.5–6 and BG 17.16 frame the whole talk. The rest prepare
you for questions.

**BG 6.5**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/6/5/
> "One must deliver himself with the help of his mind, and not degrade himself. The mind is the friend of the conditioned soul, and his enemy as well."
- **Context:** Chapter 6, on controlling the mind; the first verses set the goal of yoga practice.
- **Teaching:** The mind is the instrument of both rescue and ruin.
- **Purport point:** "the mind should be trained so that it will not be attracted by the glitter of
  material nature". Prabhupada quotes the Amrta-bindu Upanisad: the mind is "the cause of bondage" and
  "the cause of liberation".
- **Application (preacher's):** "Positive thinking" is the Gita's "deliver yourself with the help of your
  mind", in ordinary words.
- **Use:** in the talk (frame).

**BG 6.6**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/6/6/
> "For him who has conquered the mind, the mind is the best of friends; but for one who has failed to do so, his mind will remain the greatest enemy."
- **Purport point:** An unconquered mind means one "has to serve the dictations of lust, anger, avarice,
  illusion, etc."
- **Application (preacher's):** Ask the room: who is in charge in your head at 11 p.m. before a deadline?
- **Use:** in the talk (frame).

**BG 17.16**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/17/16/
> "And satisfaction, simplicity, gravity, self-control and purification of one's existence are the austerities of the mind."
- **Teaching:** The Gita names *satisfaction* as a discipline of the mind, something practised, not a mood
  that arrives.
- **Purport point:** "Satisfaction of the mind can be obtained only by taking the mind away from thoughts of
  sense enjoyment. The more we think of sense enjoyment, the more the mind becomes dissatisfied." Also:
  the mind "should be so trained that it can be always thinking of doing good for others."
- **Application (preacher's):** Tips 1, 3 and 4 (expectations, comparison, gratitude) are three roads to
  this one austerity.
- **Use:** in the talk (frame; tips 1, 3, 4).

**BG 2.47**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/2/47/
> "You have a right to perform your prescribed duty, but you are not entitled to the fruits of action…"
- **Teaching:** Effort is mine; the result is not.
- **Application (preacher's):** Tip 2, "success is in the race run, not the trophy won", is this verse in a
  slogan. If the group has heard VL1-10, one sentence of recall is enough.
- **Use:** in the talk (tip 2).

**BG 2.48**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/2/48/
> "Perform your duty equipoised, O Arjuna, abandoning all attachment to success or failure. Such equanimity is called yoga."
- **Application (preacher's):** Tip 2's "right definition of success": steadiness, not the scoreboard.
- **Use:** in the talk (tip 2).

**BG 4.22**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/4/22/
> "He who is satisfied with gain which comes of its own accord, who is free from duality and does not envy, who is steady in both success and failure, is never entangled, although performing actions."
- **Teaching:** Satisfaction, freedom from envy and steadiness named together.
- **Purport point:** such a person "labors honestly as far as is in his power, and is satisfied with
  whatever is obtained by his own honest labor."
- **Application (preacher's):** Tips 1 (unreasonable expectations) and 4 (count your blessings) in one
  verse. Note "labors honestly": satisfaction is not laziness.
- **Use:** in the talk (tips 1, 4).

**BG 12.13-14**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/12/13-14/
> "One who is not envious but is a kind friend to all living entities… who is equal in both happiness and distress, who is tolerant, always satisfied, self-controlled…"
- **Teaching:** The qualities Krsna calls dear to Him begin with non-envy and include tolerance and
  satisfaction.
- **Application (preacher's):** Tip 3 (unhealthy comparison is envy in a suit) and tip 6 (tolerance).
- **Use:** in the talk (tip 3).

**BG 2.14**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/2/14/
> "…the nonpermanent appearance of happiness and distress, and their disappearance in due course, are like the appearance and disappearance of winter and summer seasons… one must learn to tolerate them without being disturbed."
- **Application (preacher's):** Tip 5: praise and blame are weather. IYF's slides tag this tip *tāṁs
  titikṣasva*, the verse's own words, "learn to tolerate them".
- **Use:** in the talk (tip 5).

**BG 3.33**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/3/33/
> "Even a man of knowledge acts according to his own nature, for everyone follows the nature he has acquired from the three modes. What can repression accomplish?"
- **Teaching:** Everyone, even a person of knowledge, acts from the nature acquired from the modes; repression
  accomplishes little.
- **Purport point:** The purport applies the verse to oneself: one should not "suddenly give up his
  prescribed duties and become a so-called yogī", but work from one's position under guidance.
- **Application (preacher's):** Tip 6, "tolerate the svabhava of others", is our *extension* of the verse
  from one's own nature to other people's. Say so. The verse supports the realism (people act from their
  nature); the tolerance is the application.
- **Use:** in the talk (tip 6).

**BG 2.59**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/2/59/
> "Though the embodied soul may be restricted from sense enjoyment, the taste for sense objects remains. But, ceasing such engagements by experiencing a higher taste, he is fixed in consciousness."
- **Teaching:** Suppression leaves the taste intact; only a higher taste replaces it.
- **Purport point:** Restriction by rules alone "is something like restricting a diseased person from
  certain types of eatables. The patient, however, neither likes such restrictions nor loses his taste for
  eatables." IYF's slides expand this into a story about a diabetic landlord and mangoes; that story is
  IYF's illustration of the purport, not the purport itself.
- **Application (preacher's):** Tip 8: not expression, not suppression, but a better engagement.
- **Use:** in the talk (tip 8).

**BG 2.61**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/2/61/
> "One who restrains his senses, keeping them under full control, and fixes his consciousness upon Me, is known as a man of steady intelligence."
- **Purport point:** Prabhupada's example is Maharaja Ambarisa, who "silently tolerated all the sage's
  injustices and thereby emerged victorious" over the angry Durvasa Muni, because every sense was engaged
  (he then quotes SB 9.4.18–20).
- **Application (preacher's):** The two-part formula IYF teaches: withdraw, then *engage*.
- **Use:** in the talk (tip 8), as the Ambarisa example.

**SB 9.4.18-20**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/sb/9/4/18-20/
> "Mahārāja Ambarīṣa always engaged his mind in meditating upon the lotus feet of Kṛṣṇa, his words in describing the glories of the Lord, his hands in cleansing the Lord's temple, and his ears in hearing the words spoken by Kṛṣṇa or about Kṛṣṇa…"
- **Teaching:** A list of every sense, each given a positive engagement.
- **Application (preacher's):** Sublimation is concrete: not "think pure thoughts" but "give each sense a
  job". At VL1, use it as the picture of a fully engaged person; the devotional details can be read out
  as the text says them.
- **Use:** in the talk (tip 8).

**For questions (K):**
- **BG 2.62–63** (Verified): "While contemplating the objects of the senses, a person develops attachment
  for them, and from such attachment lust develops, and from lust anger arises. From anger, complete
  delusion arises, and from delusion bewilderment of memory…" The chain that tip 8 interrupts at its
  first link. Purport to 2.62: "The senses require real engagements".
- **NOI 1** (Verified): "A sober person who can tolerate the urge to speak, the mind's demands, the actions
  of anger and the urges of the tongue, belly and genitals is qualified to make disciples all over the
  world." IYF's slide "Six urges to be regulated" rests on this verse. At VL1 name the six briefly and
  move on; the genitals are mentioned in the text and need no comment.

## F. Philosophical explanation

1. **The principle.** The mind is an instrument, and it is currently steering (BG 6.5–6). Most negative
   thinking has a small number of engines: expectations the world never agreed to meet, comparison, blame,
   fear of opinion, and ungoverned desire. Each of the eight tips disables one engine. The target state is
   not excitement but *satisfaction*, which the Gita classes as an austerity of the mind (BG 17.16): a
   discipline, practised, and therefore learnable.
2. **Why it matters.** This audience lives in a comparison machine (feeds, ranks, placements). The Gita's
   diagnosis is older than the machine: dissatisfaction grows the more the mind dwells on enjoyment
   (BG 17.16 purport); envy and comparison are the same disease (BG 12.13–14; BG 4.22).
3. **Connections.** Tip 2 is VL1-10 in one line. Tip 8 opens on to the whole subject of sense control at
   VL3 and SPS. Tips 5 and 6 depend on the teaching that I am not this body and others act from their
   acquired nature (BG 3.33), which VL2 argues properly.
4. **Consequences.** A person who drops one engine of negativity usually notices it within days: less
   replaying of conversations, fewer imaginary arguments, more energy for work. That is experience, not
   proof.
5. **Misunderstandings to prevent.**
   - **Positive thinking as denial.** BG 2.14 asks us to *tolerate* distress, which means feeling it and
     continuing; it does not ask us to pretend.
   - **Positive thinking as treatment.** Clinical depression and anxiety are illnesses. The tips are
     habits for a healthy mind, and they are compatible with, never a substitute for, professional care
     (K).
   - **Tolerating others' nature as tolerating harm.** Tip 6 is about colleagues' quirks and relatives'
     habits, not about abuse. Say this out loud.
   - **Sublimation as suppression with a spiritual label.** BG 2.59 says suppression leaves the taste
     intact; the answer is a *better engagement*, not a stronger "no".
6. **Pitch for VL1.** Use "the mind as friend or enemy", "expectations", "comparison", "satisfaction".
   *Svabhava* can be said once and translated as "acquired nature". No appeal to authority: "Here is what an
   ancient text says the mind does. Check it against your own week."

## H. Opening and engagement

**H.1 How to start (first 5 minutes)**
1. Greet, say your name, and say today is eight small, testable habits, not a pep talk.
2. Write "Who is driving?" on the board.
3. Ask H.2 question 1 by show of hands; take two answers and thank each by name.
4. Say that anyone who feels low for long should also talk to a doctor or counsellor; this talk supports that care, it does not replace it.
5. Give the transition line below.

**H.2 Opening questions** (about the room's own life)
1. Think of the night before something important. Who was driving in your head, you or your mind?
2. When did a comparison last spoil a good day for you (a friend's result, a colleague's bonus, a neighbour's new car, someone's post online)?

- **Opening question:** "At 11 p.m. the night before something important, who is driving in your head:
  you, or your mind?" Pause. "The Gita says the mind can be your best friend or your worst enemy. Tonight:
  eight ways to change seats."
- **Relatable scenario (invented):** Aisha gets 92% and spends the evening upset because a classmate got
  94%. Her brother fails a paper and spends the evening listing everyone who is to blame. Same evening,
  two people, no peace in either room. What do they have in common?
- **Transition:** "Both minds are doing exactly what untrained minds do. The good news is that 'untrained'
  is a temporary condition."
- **Why it matters to this audience:** Results, rankings, social feeds, family comparisons. Most of their
  unhappiness this month was manufactured by their own mind, not by events.

## I. Core teaching points

Each tip: the principle, its verse, one example, the transition. IYF's own examples are used where they
are illustrations rather than claims.

1. **Give up unreasonable expectations.** BG 4.22. *Plain:* many demands we make on the world were never
   agreed to: that people always like us, that we never fail, that others read our needs unspoken (IYF's
   list). The gap between expectation and reality is where stress lives. *Example:* the friend who did not
   reply within the hour. *Transition:* the biggest unreasonable expectation is about success itself.
2. **Success is in the race run, not the trophy won.** BG 2.47–48. *Plain:* effort is mine, the result is
   not; steadiness is the Gita's word for success. *Example (IYF's):* lakhs sit an entrance exam, a few
   hundred get in; if the rest are "failures", we have manufactured an epidemic of misery. *Transition:*
   the same mind that rates my result rates it against yours.
3. **Give up unhealthy comparison.** BG 12.13–14; BG 4.22 ("does not envy"). *Plain:* comparison upward
   breeds envy, comparison downward breeds pride; both poison. *Example (IYF's):* sprinters practise against
   their own stopwatch. *Transition:* the cure for comparison is to look at what I already have.
4. **Count your blessings.** BG 17.16 (satisfaction as an austerity). *Plain:* gratitude is a practice, not a
   feeling. *Example:* write three specific things each night for a week. *Transition:* but what about what
   others think of me?
5. **Don't be overly bothered by what the world thinks.** BG 2.14. *Plain:* praise and blame come and go
   like seasons; "learn to tolerate them". One sentence of the deeper reason: the body others judge is
   temporary; the self is not (VL2 argues it). *Example (traditional fable, not scripture):* the father,
   the son and the donkey, criticised whichever of them rode. *Transition:* and what about what others
   *are* like?
6. **Tolerate the nature of others.** BG 3.33. *Plain:* people act from acquired nature; repression
   accomplishes little. If you have chosen to live or work with someone, accept the nature and stop
   relitigating it. *Our extension of the verse; say so.* **Boundary:** this is about habits and
   temperaments, never about accepting harm. *Transition:* the opposite of tolerance is blame.
7. **Give up the blame game; see the sunny side.** BG 17.16 purport (a mind "always thinking of doing good
   for others"). *Plain:* blaming weather, government, boss and spouse keeps the mind in what IYF's slides
   call "BMW mode: blaming, mourning, wailing". Look for the gold in people, not the dirt. *Example:* Srila
   Prabhupada's early followers in 1960s New York came with every habit he opposed; he saw what they could
   become. (Not in IYF's notes. **Requires source review:** verify the details in *Srila Prabhupada-lilamrta*
   before telling it.)
   *Transition:* the deepest engine of negativity is ungoverned desire.
8. **Desires: neither expressed nor suppressed, but sublimated.** BG 2.59; BG 2.61; SB 9.4.18–20.
   *Plain:* expressing every desire leads to hankering and the chain of BG 2.62–63; suppressing leaves the
   taste intact (BG 2.59 purport, the diseased patient); the third way is to give each sense a better
   engagement, as Ambarisa did. Mention that practitioners use chanting as the most portable of these
   engagements. *Transition:* to what positive thinking is not (K), then the takeaway.

## J. Stories and illustrations

- **Maharaja Ambarisa and Durvasa Muni** (BG 2.61 purport; SB 9.4.18–20). Keep to what the purport
  says: the sage became angry out of pride and could not check his senses; the king, a devotee, tolerated
  the injustice and "emerged victorious" because every sense was engaged in service. The fuller narrative
  is SB 9.4–5; read it before adding any detail. *Connection:* tip 8, sublimation as engagement.
- **The diseased patient and the forbidden food** (BG 2.59 purport). Prabhupada's own analogy: restriction
  alone does not remove the taste. IYF's slides tell it as a diabetic landlord who breaks into his own
  mango store; present that as IYF's expansion. *Connection:* why suppression fails.
- **The father, the son and the donkey** (traditional fable, not scripture; IYF uses it for tip 5). Whatever
  they do, someone criticises. *Connection:* the futility of living for opinion.
- **Duryodhana's envy of the Pandavas** (IYF's example for tip 3). The Mahabharata narrative is well
  known, but no BBT passage is cited for it on this page: **Requires source review** before telling it as
  scripture. The point can be made with BG 12.13–14 alone.
- **Contemporary (invented):** Aisha and her brother (H).
- **From the Preaching Encyclopedia, IYF Books ch. 6 "Developing Positive Thinking"** (HG Radheshyam Das,
  VOICE; added 28 Oct 2026), the written form of this talk, which confirms that IYF's character-ethic framing
  came through VOICE (O). Usable, attributed as teaching analogies: the **ship and the water** (water all around
  cannot sink a ship; only the water that gets in can), the **two shoe salesmen** sent to a barefoot country (one
  sees no market, the other sees nothing but market), the **wrong map** (effort and attitude cannot get you to
  Pune with a map of Bombay; first the map, then diligence, then attitude), and the **ostrich** for false

  after Duryodhana's abuse and taking it as an opportunity for pilgrimage; the Bhagavatam narrates this at the
  start of Canto 3 (chapter 1). Read that chapter before telling it, and tell only what it says. The chapter does
  not supply the Duryodhana-envy story above, which stays **Requires source review**.
  **Not taken:** its claims about hormones and "thought vibrations", the "Dr Spock / and we said OK" piece, and
  the Edison "900 ways" anecdote, all unsourced.
- **Not used:** the slides' quotation attributed to Andrew Carnegie ("digging for gold") and the "squirrel
  and the mountain" story attributed to the Rig Veda could not be verified; do not present them as
  sourced.

## K. Anticipated questions and objections

> **Before you teach:** someone in the room may be clinically depressed or anxious, or grieving. "Think
> positive" said to such a person is a wound. Say plainly, once, that these tips are habits for a healthy
> mind and that illness of the mind deserves professional care exactly as illness of the body does, and that
> such care is fully compatible with spiritual life. If someone discloses distress, acknowledge them, do not
> analyse it in public, and talk afterwards. *(Pastoral guidance, not a scriptural claim.)*

1. **"Isn't positive thinking just self-deception? Problems are real."**
   - Yes, problems are real, and the Gita does not ask us to deny them: BG 2.14 says *tolerate* distress,
     which presupposes feeling it. The tips are not about believing nice things; they are about removing
     manufactured suffering (expectations, comparison, blame) so that real problems get real attention.
2. **"Tolerating others' nature sounds like putting up with bad behaviour."**
   - Tip 6 is about temperaments and habits, not about harm. Nobody is asked to tolerate abuse, cheating
     or cruelty; BG 2.47 asks us to *act* in our duty, which includes protecting ourselves and others.
     Reading BG 3.33 as realism about what nagging can achieve is our application; it is not a licence for others.
3. **"If I give up comparison, how will I improve?"**
   - Compare with your own yesterday (IYF's stopwatch). BG 4.22 praises honest labour "as far as is in his
     power"; what it removes is envy, not effort.
4. **"Isn't 'count your blessings' a way to keep poor people quiet?"**
   - Gratitude is for *my* mind, not a policy for others. BG 17.16's purport pairs satisfaction with "doing
     good for others"; a satisfied mind has more energy for service and justice, not less.
5. **"Sublimation sounds like suppression with a nicer name."**
   - BG 2.59 says suppression leaves the taste intact and names the alternative: a higher taste. Ambarisa
     (SB 9.4.18–20) did not sit on his hands; every sense had a job. The difference is engagement versus
     restriction.
6. **"I've tried positive thinking and I still feel low all the time."**
   - *Pastoral guidance:* persistent low mood is not a failure of attitude; it can be an illness, and
     illness deserves care. Encourage them to speak to a doctor or counsellor, and offer to talk after the
     session. Do not quote verses at this question.

## L. Case studies

*Invented cases for discussion. Not real incidents.*

**Case 1: The group chat.** Meera's college group posts internship offers all week. She has none yet. She
mutes the chat, then unmutes it, then lies awake calculating who is ahead.
- **Dilemma:** What is actually hurting her: the lack of an offer, or the comparison?
- **Principles:** BG 12.13–14 (non-envy); BG 4.22 (satisfied, steady, yet labouring honestly); BG 2.47
  (effort vs result).
- **Questions:**
  - Which of the eight tips does Meera need first?
  - What would "compare with your own stopwatch" look like for her this week?
  - Is muting the chat sublimation, suppression or avoidance?
- **Suggested analysis:** Tips 3 and 2. Muting is fine as a boundary but is not the cure; the cure is to
  redirect the hours into applications and to measure the week by effort. Gratitude (tip 4) for what she
  does have is a daily practice, not a one-time pep talk.
- **Takeaway:** The feed does not create the envy; it feeds an engine already running.

**Case 2: The flatmate.** Dev's flatmate is untidy, loud and perpetually late with rent. Dev has lectured
him weekly for six months. Nothing has changed except Dev's blood pressure.
- **Dilemma:** Tolerate, or act?
- **Principles:** BG 3.33 (people act from acquired nature; "what can repression accomplish?"); BG 2.47
  (act in your duty); tip 7 (blame).
- **Questions:**
  - Which parts of this are temperament (tip 6) and which are obligations (rent) that need action?
  - What has six months of lecturing accomplished? What might work instead?
  - Where is Dev's own mind in this: friend or enemy (BG 6.6)?
- **Suggested analysis:** Separate the two. Untidiness and noise are nature: accept, negotiate a minimum,
  stop relitigating. Rent is an obligation: a clear deadline and a consequence, calmly. The lecturing was
  blame, and it fed Dev's negativity more than it changed the flatmate.
- **Takeaway:** Tolerance of nature and firmness about obligations are not in conflict.

## M. Discussion and reflection

**M.1 Closing check questions** (easy first; praise every try)
1. When is the mind a friend, by BG 6.6? *For one who has conquered it.*
2. Which of the five engines is "comparison"? *Tip 3; compare with your own yesterday.*
3. What does BG 17.16 call satisfaction? *An austerity (discipline) of the mind.*
4. Does "tolerate others' nature" include tolerating harm? *No; harm calls for action.*
5. What are the three ways of handling a desire in tip 8? *Express, suppress or sublimate; the Gita points to sublimation (BG 2.59).*

**M.2 Activities (choose one; about 10 minutes)**
- **Option A, "Pick two" (10 min).** 1. Hand out the eight tips. 2. 2 min: each privately marks the two they need most. 3. 5 min: groups of three, same tip, share one way to try it this week (sharing optional). 4. 3 min: link to BG 6.6; thank everyone.
- **Option B, "Reframe" (8 min).** 1. Give three short cards (a friend gets a promotion; a message goes unanswered; an exam does not go well). 2. 4 min: pairs write an "engine" reply and a "habit" reply. 3. 4 min: two pairs read theirs; praise each.

**M.3 Reflection**
- **Reflection:** Which two tips would change your week the most? Which one engine of negativity
  (expectation, comparison, opinion, blame, desire) runs loudest in you?
- **Application (private, optional):** Choose one tip. Write it on the lock screen of your phone for seven
  days. *(Nobody needs to share.)*

# Part 2: For students

## S1. Summary

The Gita says the mind can be your best friend or your worst enemy (BG 6.6), and that which one it is
depends on training. Most of our unhappiness in a week is manufactured by five engines in the mind:
unreasonable expectations, comparison, fear of others' opinions, blame, and ungoverned desire. Eight small
habits, each found in the Gita, switch those engines off.

The first habits are about expectation and comparison. Give up unreasonable expectations, and measure success by the race run, not the trophy (BG 4.22, 2.47–48). Compare with your own yesterday and count your blessings (BG 12.13–14). Next come opinion and nature: praise and blame pass like seasons (BG 2.14), and each person has a nature we must tolerate, though never harm (BG 3.33). Then drop the blame game and look for the good in people.

The last habit is about desire. It can be indulged, crushed, or given a better engagement. The Gita says that crushing leaves the taste intact, and only a higher taste ends it (BG 2.59). King Ambarisa, a devotee-king, gave every sense a service to the Lord (SB 9.4.18–20).

The target is not excitement but satisfaction, which the Gita calls an "austerity of the mind" (BG 17.16), along with simplicity, gravity, self-control and purity of one's existence: a discipline you can practise, not a mood you wait for. Positive thinking is not pretending problems are not there, and it is not a treatment for illness; it is removing the suffering we add on top.

## S2. Key ideas

- The mind is a friend or an enemy; the difference is who is in charge (BG 6.5–6).
- Satisfaction is a practice, not luck (BG 17.16).
- Effort is mine, the result is not; steadiness is the real success (BG 2.47–48).
- Comparison is envy in a suit; compare with your own yesterday (BG 12.13–14; BG 4.22).
- Desires are neither indulged nor crushed but given a better engagement (BG 2.59; SB 9.4.18–20).

## S3. Verses to remember

- **BG 6.6:** "For him who has conquered the mind, the mind is the best of friends; but for one who has
  failed to do so, his mind will remain the greatest enemy." *For me:* I can notice who is driving.
- **BG 17.16:** "satisfaction, simplicity, gravity, self-control and purification of one's existence are the
  austerities of the mind." *For me:* satisfaction is something I do, daily.
- **BG 2.59:** restriction alone leaves the taste; "by experiencing a higher taste, he is fixed in
  consciousness." *For me:* replace, don't just resist.

## S4. Words to know

- **Svabhava:** one's acquired nature; the way a person tends to act (BG 3.33). Others have one; so do I.
- **Austerity (of the mind):** a discipline practised to train the mind; the Gita's list includes
  satisfaction (BG 17.16).
- **Sublimation:** redirecting a desire into a better engagement rather than indulging or suppressing it
  (BG 2.59; the example of King Ambarisa, SB 9.4.18–20).

## S5. Questions and answers

1. **Isn't positive thinking self-deception?**
   No. The Gita says *tolerate* distress (BG 2.14), which means feeling it and carrying on, not denying it.
   The eight tips remove suffering we manufacture, so that real problems get our real attention.
2. **Does "tolerate others' nature" mean putting up with bad behaviour?**
   No. It is about temperaments and habits, not harm. Nobody is asked to accept abuse or cheating. The Gita
   also tells us to act in our duty (BG 2.47).
3. **If I stop comparing, how will I improve?**
   Compare with your own yesterday. The Gita praises honest work "as far as is in his power" and removes
   only the envy (BG 4.22).
4. **Is sublimation just suppression with a nicer name?**
   The Gita says suppression leaves the taste intact (BG 2.59). The alternative is a better engagement for
   each sense, as King Ambarisa practised (SB 9.4.18–20).
5. **I have tried and I still feel low all the time.**
   Persistent low mood is not a failure of attitude. It can be an illness, and illness deserves care from a
   doctor or counsellor. That care goes together with spiritual life, never against it.

## S6. Check your understanding

1. In BG 6.6 the mind is "the best of friends" for: (a) a person with a quick mind, (b) a person who has
   conquered the mind, (c) a person with no desires.
2. BG 17.16 lists five austerities of the mind. Name two.
3. True or false: BG 2.59 says that if you restrict the senses firmly enough, the taste for enjoyment
   disappears.
4. Which tip matches each: "compare with your own stopwatch"; "praise and blame are like seasons"; "give
   every sense a job"?
5. In your own words: what is the difference between tolerating a person's nature and tolerating harm?

**Answers:** 1. (b) (BG 6.6). 2. Any two of: satisfaction, simplicity, gravity, self-control, purification
of one's existence (BG 17.16). 3. False: "the taste for sense objects remains"; only a higher taste ends it
(BG 2.59). 4. Tip 3 (BG 12.13–14); tip 5 (BG 2.14); tip 8 (SB 9.4.18–20). 5. Nature means habits and
temperament, accepted because repression accomplishes little (BG 3.33); harm calls for action, which is my
duty (BG 2.47).
