# Making the right choice

> **VL1 · 45–60 min · review: sources checked, awaiting senior review.** IYF's deck (*UMANG – Making the Right Choice*, subtitle "Material
> problems only has spiritual solution") runs six themes: the rarity of human life; material existence as
> suffering; fallible shelters; real great fortune; two worlds; the "U-turn" (admit, try, cry; four R's). It is a
> youth-fest talk pitched a level above VL1 and leans on statistics about suicide and disease. This page keeps
> its spine (a rare chance; what to spend it on; whom to ask; the turn) and re-pitches it for VL1: **the Gita
> treats choice as ours** (BG 18.63), gives a test for choices (18.37–38) and a picture of clear and clouded
> judgement (18.30, 18.32), and honours every reason a person has for walking in (7.16). The deck's statistics,
> species count and "sinful rich" argument are left out (B1).

## B. Why this topic belongs at this level

Choosing is the thing this audience does all day and dreads most. The Gita is, structurally, a book about one
choice: Arjuna must decide, and after seven hundred verses Krsna says, "Deliberate on this fully, and then do
what you wish to do" (18.63). That respect for the chooser is the right note for VL1, where nothing may be
pressed. The Gita also gives practical tools: a two-question test for any option (18.37–38), a description of
good judgement (18.30) and of the fog that inverts it (18.32), and the reminder that one-pointed people are
steadier than many-branched ones (2.41). The deck's grand frame (rarity of human life; two worlds; the U-turn)
is kept as the horizon and told lightly.

**Builds on vl1-32:** [vl1-32](vl1-32.md) uses BG 18.37–38 (nectar-first, poison-later) to break a habit. Here the same verses serve a different point, testing a choice before making it. Give the verses one line, not the habit-loop story, and do not reuse its examples.

**Not done here:**
- Frightening the room with mortality statistics or hellish futures.
- Judging anyone's wealth, misfortune or past choices.
- Pressing a decision tonight. The talk ends where the Gita ends: "do what you wish".

## C. Central message

The Gita ends not with a command but with "Deliberate on this fully, and then do what you wish to do": the
choice is ours. It also gives us a way to choose well: ask when an option feels good, at the start or at the
end; notice whether your judgement is clear or clouded; and, before the hardest choices, do what Arjuna did,
ask someone who can see further than you.

## D. Learning outcomes

By the end, participants should be able to:
1. Explain what BG 18.63 says about human freedom and why the Gita ends there.
2. Apply the timing test (poison-first or nectar-first, 18.37–38) to a real option in front of them.
3. Describe clear judgement (18.30) and clouded judgement (18.32) in their own words, and name one thing that
   clouds theirs.
4. Say why one-pointed people are steadier (2.41) and what Arjuna did before deciding (2.7).
5. State, in plain words, why the texts call a human life a rare chance (SB 11.20.17; CC Madhya 19.151) and
   what that implies for the choices of the next year.

## E. Scriptural foundation

**Use:** BG 18.63 and 18.37–38 carry the talk (freedom and the test). 18.30, 18.32, 2.41 and 2.7 give the
method. 7.16, 7.3, SB 11.20.17 and CC Madhya 19.151 set the horizon. 8.15 is used once. SB 11.20.17 is in
Canto 11: *BBT translation, completed by Srila Prabhupada's disciples*.

**BG 18.63**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/18/63/
> "Thus I have explained to you knowledge still more confidential. Deliberate on this fully, and then do what you wish to do."
- **Context:** Krsna's closing words after the whole teaching, before the final verses.
- **Teaching:** After explaining everything, Krsna leaves the decision to Arjuna.
- **Purport point:** "the words yathecchasi tathā kuru – 'As you like, you may act' – indicate that God does not
  interfere with the little independence of the living entity." "Before surrendering, one is free to deliberate
  on this subject as far as the intelligence goes".
- **Application (preacher's):** This is the whole tone of a VL1 talk on choice. God Himself, having taught for
  seven hundred verses, does not force. Neither does the preacher. "Deliberate fully" is the task; "do what you
  wish" is the freedom.
- **Use:** in the talk (the key verse).

**BG 18.37**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/18/37/
> "That which in the beginning may be just like poison but at the end is just like nectar and which awakens one to self-realization is said to be happiness in the mode of goodness."
- **Purport point:** The rules of self-realisation "are very difficult, bitter like poison, but if one is
  successful in following the regulations and comes to the transcendental position, he begins to drink real
  nectar".
- **Application (preacher's):** Half of the test: the good choice usually tastes bad first. Early mornings,
  honest conversations, the harder course.
- **Use:** in the talk (the test).

**BG 18.38**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/18/38/
> "That happiness which is derived from contact of the senses with their objects and which appears like nectar at first but poison at the end is said to be of the nature of passion."
- **Purport point:** "In the beginning this may be very pleasing to the senses, but at the end, or after some
  time, it becomes just like poison."
- **Application (preacher's):** The other half: the wrong choice usually tastes good first. The two verses
  together are a test anyone can run in ten seconds on any option: *when* does it feel good?
- **Use:** in the talk (the test).

**BG 18.30**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/18/30/
> "O son of Pṛthā, that understanding by which one knows what ought to be done and what ought not to be done, what is to be feared and what is not to be feared, what is binding and what is liberating, is in the mode of goodness."
- **Teaching:** Clear judgement knows what to do and not do, what to fear and not fear, what binds and what frees.
- **Purport point:** "Understanding which discriminates by intelligence is situated in the mode of goodness."
- **Application (preacher's):** Three pairs, which the room can use as questions: Ought I? Should I fear this?
  Will it bind me or free me? Most bad choices fail the third.
- **Use:** in the talk.

**BG 18.32**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/18/32/
> "That understanding which considers irreligion to be religion and religion to be irreligion, under the spell of illusion and darkness, and strives always in the wrong direction, O Pārtha, is in the mode of ignorance."
- **Purport point:** "Intelligence in the mode of ignorance is always working the opposite of the way it should.
  It accepts religions which are not actually religions and rejects actual religion… They think truth to be
  untruth and accept untruth as truth."
- **Application (preacher's):** Clouded judgement does not merely err; it inverts. At VL1, name the ordinary
  fogs: exhaustion, intoxication, anger, the wrong company, three a.m. Nobody is called ignorant; the fog is.
- **Use:** in the talk.

**BG 2.41**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/2/41/
> "Those who are on this path are resolute in purpose, and their aim is one. O beloved child of the Kurus, the intelligence of those who are irresolute is many-branched."
- **Purport point:** The resolute purpose "is based on knowledge"; "as by watering the root of a tree one
  automatically distributes water to the leaves and branches, so by acting in Kṛṣṇa consciousness one can render
  the highest service to everyone".
- **Application (preacher's):** The many-branched intelligence is the audience's experience of choice paralysis.
  The verse's remedy is not more options but one aim; at VL1, ask "what is your one aim?" and let the question
  sit.
- **Use:** in the talk.

**BG 2.7**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/2/7/
> "Now I am confused about my duty and have lost all composure because of miserly weakness. In this condition I am asking You to tell me for certain what is best for me. Now I am Your disciple, and a soul surrendered unto You. Please instruct me."
- **Teaching:** At his crossroads, Arjuna admits confusion and asks someone he trusts.
- **Purport point:** The remedy for perplexity is to "approach a bona fide spiritual master".
- **Application (preacher's):** The one choice that makes other choices easier: whom to ask. Arjuna chose his
  advisor before he chose his action. At VL1: find one person who can see further than you and has nothing to
  gain from your decision.
- **Use:** in the talk.

**BG 7.16**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/7/16/
> "O best among the Bhāratas, four kinds of pious men begin to render devotional service unto Me – the distressed, the desirer of wealth, the inquisitive, and he who is searching for knowledge of the Absolute."
- **Purport point:** "These persons come to the Supreme Lord for devotional service under different conditions."
  When "completely purified by the association of a pure devotee, they also become pure devotees."
- **Application (preacher's):** The deck lists these as the four who take shelter. Use the verse as a welcome:
  whether you came tonight because you are stressed, want success, are curious, or are searching, the Gita
  counts your reason as a good one.
- **Use:** in the talk (the welcome).

**BG 7.3**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/7/3/
> "Out of many thousands among men, one may endeavor for perfection, and of those who have achieved perfection, hardly one knows Me in truth."
- **Application (preacher's):** Said with a smile: the fact that you are in this room on a Sunday evening puts you
  in a small group. Not flattery; an observation about choices already made.
- **Use:** in the talk, one line.

**SB 11.20.17**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/sb/11/20/17/ · *BBT translation, completed by Srila Prabhupada's disciples*
> "The human body, which can award all benefit in life, is automatically obtained by the laws of nature, although it is a very rare achievement. This human body can be compared to a perfectly constructed boat having the spiritual master as the captain and the instructions of the Personality of Godhead as favorable winds impelling it on its course…"
- **Teaching:** A human life is a rare and well-made boat; a guide and good instruction carry it across.
- **Purport point:** "one who cannot understand that the human body is a suitable boat for crossing the ocean of
  material existence will see no need to accept a captain in the form of the spiritual master".
- **Application (preacher's):** The deck's opening verse. Read the boat image and stop before "killer of his own
  soul"; at VL1 the point is privilege, not threat. The captain is the 2.7 point again: choose whom you ask.
- **Use:** in the talk (the horizon).

**CC Madhya 19.151**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/cc/madhya/19/151/
> "…Out of many millions of wandering living entities, one who is very fortunate gets an opportunity to associate with a bona fide spiritual master by the grace of Kṛṣṇa…"
- **Purport point:** "if one desires something, Kṛṣṇa fulfills one's desire. If the living entity by chance or
  fortune comes in contact with the Kṛṣṇa consciousness movement and wishes to associate with that movement,
  Kṛṣṇa… gives him the chance to meet a bona fide spiritual master."
- **Application (preacher's):** The deck's "real great fortune". The purport makes fortune a matter of
  *wishing*: the choice to want guidance is itself the fortunate choice.
- **Use:** in the talk.

**BG 8.15**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/8/15/
> "…this temporary world, which is full of miseries…"
- **Application (preacher's):** One phrase, once, to say that the texts do not pretend the world is painless;
  then move on. Not a theme at VL1.
- **Use:** in the talk, one line.

## F. Philosophical explanation

1. **The principle.** Choice is real and ours: "do what you wish to do" (18.63), because "God does not interfere
   with the little independence of the living entity" (purport). Good choosing has three parts in the Gita:
   a test for options (poison-first or nectar-first, 18.37–38), clear rather than clouded judgement (18.30 vs
   18.32), and the prior choice of whom to ask (2.7; the captain of SB 11.20.17). One aim makes the rest steady
   (2.41). The horizon is that a human life is a rare chance (SB 11.20.17; CC Madhya 19.151; 7.3), and every
   reason for turning toward it is honoured (7.16).
2. **Why it matters.** The audience is drowning in options and advice. The Gita does not add another instruction;
   it gives a way to decide and then respects the decision. That is rarer than it sounds, and it is why the room
   will trust the preacher who models it.
3. **Connections.** What we choose *for* (VL1-01 happiness; VL1-21 the pleasure hunt and sreyas vs preyas);
   career choices (VL1-43); the mind that clouds judgement (VL1-16); bad habits as choices already automated
   (VL1-32). At SPS/DPS the deck's four R's become the stages of surrender, with a mentor.
4. **Consequences.** A listener who takes this in will run the ten-second test on tonight's options, notice when
   their judgement is fogged and postpone big decisions until it clears, name one aim, and find one person to
   ask. None of this requires belief; all of it can be tested.
5. **Misunderstandings to prevent.**
   - **"The Gita tells you what to choose."** It teaches, then says "do what you wish" (18.63). It respects the
     chooser.
   - **"Poison-first means suffering is good."** No: it means good things often cost at the start. The test is
     about *timing*, not about seeking pain.
   - **"Fallible shelters are useless."** The deck overstates. Family, friends and doctors are goods; the texts
     say they are not *final*. Keep them; add a captain.
   - **"If I don't decide for spiritual life tonight I've wasted my rare chance."** SB 11.20.17 is a privilege,
     not a deadline. The purport to BG 3.41 (VL1-32) says "even a late beginner can become a lover of God".
   - **"People who are rich or poor deserve it."** Not taught here, and the deck's version of it is left out
     (B1). See VL1-06.
6. **Pitch for VL1.** Sanskrit: *sattva* (clarity) and *tamas* (fog) at most, defined once. Tone: respectful of
   their freedom, playful about the fog, serious about the captain.

## H. Opening and engagement

**H.1 How to start (first 5 minutes)**
1. Greet, give your name, and say nothing today will be pressed on anyone.
2. Ask everyone to hold one real choice in mind; no one says it aloud.
3. Ask H.2 question 1 by show of hands.
4. Read BG 18.63 once, slowly, as the promise of the talk.
5. Give the transition and begin segment 2.

**H.2 Opening questions** (about the room's own life)
1. Who has a choice this month that they have not yet made? Hands only.
2. When did you last make a choice while tired or angry, and what happened? *(Think; do not share the story.)*
3. Whom do you ask before an important decision, and why that person?

- **Opening:** "Think of one choice in front of you this month. A course, a job, a city, a friendship, how you
  spend your evenings. Don't say it aloud. Hold it." Pause. "The most famous spiritual book in India is about a
  man who had to make one choice, and the book ends with his teacher saying: now decide. We're going to look at
  how he got there, and you'll keep your choice in your hand the whole time."
- **Relatable scenario (invented):** Tanvi has two offers: a higher-paying job in a city where she knows no one,
  and a lower-paying one near her family and friends. Everyone has an opinion. She has made a spreadsheet. She
  still cannot decide, and she has stopped sleeping.
- **Transition:** "Let's give Tanvi three tools from the Gita, and then leave her free."
- **Why it matters to this audience:** They will make more consequential choices in the next five years than in
  the rest of their lives, mostly tired, mostly advised by people with something to gain.

## I. Core teaching points

1. **Every reason for being here is a good one.** The distressed, the ambitious, the curious, the searching
   (7.16). *Example:* name the four with a smile; most people are two of them. *Transition:* and the Gita's
   last word to all four is the same.
2. **The choice is yours.** "Deliberate on this fully, and then do what you wish to do" (18.63); "God does not
   interfere with the little independence of the living entity" (purport). *Example:* seven hundred verses, then
   freedom. *Transition:* so how do we deliberate well?
3. **The ten-second test.** Nectar first, poison later; or poison first, nectar later (18.37–38). *Example:* the
   late night vs the early morning; the easy elective vs the hard one; the honest conversation vs the avoided
   one. *Transition:* but sometimes I can't even see straight.
4. **Clear or clouded.** Clear judgement knows what to do, what to fear, what binds and what frees (18.30);
   clouded judgement inverts them (18.32). Ordinary fogs: exhaustion, intoxication, anger, the wrong company,
   three a.m. One aim steadies the many branches (2.41). *Example:* Tanvi's spreadsheet at midnight.
   *Transition:* what did Arjuna do when he couldn't see?
5. **Choose whom to ask, first.** "I am asking You to tell me for certain what is best for me" (2.7). The boat
   needs a captain (SB 11.20.17); fortune begins with wishing for guidance (CC Madhya 19.151). *Example:* one
   person who sees further and gains nothing from your choice. *Transition:* why does it matter so much?
6. **A rare chance, held lightly.** The human boat (11.20.17); one in thousands (7.3); a world not painless
   (8.15). The deck's U-turn in a line: admit, ask, try. *Transition:* to one choice this week.

- **Not told here:** the suicide statistics, the COVID slides, and the "sinful rich are being set up to fall"

## K. Anticipated questions and objections

> **Before you teach:** someone in the room is at a real and painful crossroads (a failed exam, a family
> crisis, a relationship ending). Do not use their moment as an example. If distress is visible, acknowledge
> it, do not analyse it publicly, and talk afterwards; if there are signs of depression or self-harm, refer
> (Tele-MANAS 14416; see VL1-19 K). *(Pastoral guidance, not a scriptural claim.)*

1. **"If God doesn't interfere with my choice, why do religious people keep telling me what to do?"**
   - The Gita's own ending is "do what you wish to do" (18.63). A good teacher explains and then respects the
     decision; that is the model here. If you have met pressure, that was the person, not the text.
2. **"The poison-first test would make me choose suffering every time."**
   - No. The test is about *timing*, not about seeking pain. A good choice often costs at the start (18.37); a
     bad one often pays at the start (18.38). Plenty of good choices also feel good immediately; the test
     catches the ones that lie.
3. **"How do I know whether my judgement is clear or clouded right now?"**
   - 18.30 gives three questions: ought I? should I fear this? will it bind or free me? If you cannot answer
     them calmly, or if you are exhausted, intoxicated, angry or in the wrong company, the fog of 18.32 is on;
     postpone, sleep, and ask someone (2.7).
4. **"Whom should I ask? Everyone has an agenda."**
   - Arjuna chose someone who could see further and had nothing to gain from his decision. Look for that: a
     mentor, a teacher, an elder who is not invested in the outcome. The texts call the ideal version a captain
     (SB 11.20.17); start with the best available.
5. **"The deck says family, doctors and science are useless shelters. Really?"**
   - The texts do not say useless; they say not *final*. A doctor cures a disease and cannot stop death; a family
     loves you and cannot carry your choices. Keep them; add a captain.
6. **"I'm only here because I'm stressed / want to succeed. Is that a bad reason?"**
   - The Gita lists the distressed and the ambitious among four kinds who turn toward God and calls them pious
     (7.16). Your reason is counted good.
7. **"Isn't 'rare human life' just a way to scare me into religion?"**
   - It is a statement of privilege, like being told you hold a good boat (SB 11.20.17). The talk ends, as the
     Gita does, with your freedom (18.63). Nobody here is being frightened into anything.

## L. Case studies

*Invented cases for discussion. Not real incidents.*

**Case 1: Tanvi's two offers.** Higher pay far from everyone; lower pay near family. Spreadsheet, insomnia,
everyone's opinion.
- **Dilemma:** How does she decide without more data?
- **Principles:** BG 18.37–38 (when does each option feel good?); 18.30 (ought, fear, bind/free); 18.32 (is she
  deciding in a fog?); 2.41 (what is her one aim?); 2.7 (whom to ask).
- **Questions:** Which option is nectar-first for her, and in what way? Which fear in 18.30's second pair is
  driving her? Who could she ask who gains nothing either way?
- **Suggested analysis:** The spreadsheet is many-branched intelligence; the question "what is my one aim?"
  prunes it. She postpones until she has slept, runs the test on both offers, and asks one disinterested elder.
  Then she chooses, and the Gita's last word to her is "do what you wish".
- **Takeaway:** Clear the fog, name the aim, ask well, then choose freely.

**Case 2: Dev's evening.** Dev has an exam in a week. Friends are going out tonight; he could study, go, or go
"just for an hour".
- **Principles:** BG 18.38 (nectar first); 18.37 (poison first); 18.30 (will it bind or free me tomorrow?).
- **Questions:** Which option passes the timing test? Is "just for an hour" a clear or clouded thought? What
  small choice tonight would he thank himself for at the exam?
- **Takeaway:** The ten-second test works on small choices too, and small choices are where character is made
  (VL1-32).

# Part 2: For students

## S1. Summary

The Bhagavad-gita (an ancient Indian text) is about a man, Arjuna, who had to make one choice, and after teaching him everything Krsna says: "Deliberate on this fully, and then do what you wish to do" (BG 18.63). The choice is yours; God does not force it. The Gita also gives three tools for choosing well.

First, a ten-second test: does this option feel like
nectar now and poison later, or poison now and nectar later (BG 18.37–38)? Good choices usually cost something
at the start; bad ones usually pay at the start.

Second, check whether your judgement is clear or clouded: clear
judgement knows what to do, what to fear, and what will bind or free you (BG 18.30); clouded judgement inverts
these (BG 18.32), and ordinary fogs like exhaustion, anger, intoxication and the wrong company bring it on.
One clear aim steadies a many-branched mind (BG 2.41). Third, before the hardest choices, do what Arjuna did:
ask someone who sees further than you and gains nothing from your decision (BG 2.7).

The texts call a human
life a rare, well-made boat that needs a captain (SB 11.20.17), and they honour every reason for turning toward
the question, whether you are stressed, ambitious, curious or searching (BG 7.16).

## S2. Key ideas

- The choice is yours: "do what you wish to do" (BG 18.63).
- The timing test: nectar-first choices lie; poison-first choices pay (BG 18.37–38).
- Clear judgement asks: ought I? should I fear this? will it bind or free me? (BG 18.30)
- Clouded judgement inverts truth and untruth (BG 18.32); postpone big decisions in a fog.
- One aim steadies many branches (BG 2.41).
- Choose whom to ask before you choose what to do (BG 2.7; SB 11.20.17).
- Every reason for being here is honoured (BG 7.16).

## S3. Verses to remember

- **BG 18.63:** "Deliberate on this fully, and then do what you wish to do." *For me:* think it through; the
  decision is mine.
- **BG 18.38:** happiness that "appears like nectar at first but poison at the end". *For me:* ask when an option
  feels good.
- **BG 2.7:** "In this condition I am asking You to tell me for certain what is best for me." *For me:* the
  strongest man on the field asked; so can I.

## S4. Words to know

- **Sattva (goodness, clarity):** the state in which judgement sees what ought to be done (BG 18.30).
- **Tamas (ignorance, fog):** the state in which judgement inverts right and wrong (BG 18.32).
- **The timing test:** asking whether a choice is nectar-first or poison-first (BG 18.37–38).
- **Captain:** the Bhagavatam's image for a trustworthy guide (SB 11.20.17).

## S5. Questions and answers

1. **If God doesn't force my choice, why do religious people tell me what to do?**
   The Gita's last word is "do what you wish to do" (BG 18.63). A good teacher explains and then respects your
   decision.
2. **Won't the poison-first test make me choose suffering?**
   No. It is about timing, not pain. Good choices often cost at the start; bad ones often pay at the start.
3. **How do I know if my judgement is clouded?**
   If you cannot calmly answer "ought I? should I fear this? will it bind or free me?" (BG 18.30), or you are
   exhausted, angry, intoxicated or in the wrong company, postpone and ask someone.
4. **Whom should I ask?**
   Someone who sees further than you and gains nothing from your decision. Arjuna chose his advisor first (BG 2.7).
5. **Are family and doctors "useless shelters"?**
   No. They are goods; the texts say they are not final. Keep them; add a captain.
6. **I'm only here because I'm stressed. Is that a bad reason?**
   The Gita counts the distressed among four kinds of pious people who turn toward God (BG 7.16). It is a good reason.

## S6. Check your understanding

1. In BG 18.63 Krsna tells Arjuna to: (a) obey without thinking, (b) deliberate fully and then do what he
   wishes, (c) give up deciding.
2. An option feels wonderful tonight and terrible tomorrow. Is it BG 18.37 or 18.38?
3. Name the three pairs clear judgement distinguishes in BG 18.30.
4. What did Arjuna do in BG 2.7 before deciding?
5. In your own words: what does the boat image in SB 11.20.17 say about a human life?

**Answers:** 1. (b) (BG 18.63). 2. BG 18.38 (nectar first, poison later). 3. What ought and ought not to be done;
what is and is not to be feared; what binds and what frees (BG 18.30). 4. He admitted his confusion and asked
someone he trusted for guidance (BG 2.7). 5. It is rare and well made, and with a good captain and the Lord's
instructions as favourable winds it can cross the ocean of material existence (SB 11.20.17).
