# HAPPINESS

> **VL1 · 45–60 min · review: sources checked, awaiting senior review.** Sensitive: the topic touches
> disappointment and, in IYF's slides, suicide. Read the care note in K before teaching.

## A. Topic overview

| | |
|---|---|
| Batch / level | VL1 (Vedic Level 1), a stand-alone talk |
| Suggested duration | 45–60 min |
| Preparation time | About 90 min, the first time |
| Difficulty | Introductory |
| Prerequisites | None |
| Audience | Students and young professionals, many with no settled faith; some may be anxious, low, or under exam and career pressure |
| Teaching objective | Help listeners ask, calmly and without pressure, "What do I really want when I say I want to be happy, and are my methods likely to get it?", and show that the Gita offers a thoughtful answer they can test |

## B. Why this topic belongs at this level

Everybody wants to be happy, so this is the widest door into spiritual thought. At VL1 the aim is **attraction
through a shared question**, not a defence of any doctrine.

IYF's own notes (Happiness Module 1 and 2) are built the same way. Module 1's slide reads "To be happy you have to be a thinker or follower": either examine the ideas of
happiness we absorb, as an expert would, or consult a real expert, as we do in any technical field. Module 2 is titled "Searching for happiness in the wrong place and in the wrong
way" and separates what we *like* (immediate cravings, *preyas*) from what is *good* for us (ultimate needs,
*sreyas*). This page keeps that flow and supports every scriptural claim from Srila Prabhupada's books.

| Level | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **VL1** (here) | Experiential. Where do I look for happiness? Does it last? Is there a higher kind? |
| VL2 | The self and the body argued out; the Lord as the source of happiness |
| VL3 | Practice: how a higher taste is trained, step by step |
| SPS | Keeping the higher taste alive when routine tires |
| DPS | Happiness in service: working for others without exhaustion |
| BSS | Simple living as the base of a happier brahmacari life |

**Not done here:**
- Telling people their pursuit of success, money or relationships is wrong.
- Claiming that wealth or fame makes people unhappy (the research in O does not say that).
- Suggesting that spirituality cures depression.

## C. Central message

Everyone is looking for lasting happiness, but pleasures that come from contact with the senses begin and end.
The Gita invites us to test whether we are looking in the right place, and describes a deeper, steadier
happiness found in the self.

## D. Learning outcomes

By the end, participants should be able to:
1. Say in their own words the difference between what they *like* right now and what is *good* for them in the long run.
2. Explain the Gita's observation that pleasures from the senses begin and end (BG 5.22) and that striving to satisfy desires does not bring peace (BG 2.70).
3. Describe the Gita's three kinds of happiness in plain terms (BG 18.37–39).
4. Name one thing they would like to test about their own search for happiness.

## E. Scriptural foundation

**Use:** five references are enough for the talk (BG 5.22, 2.70, 18.37, 18.38, 6.20–23). The rest are for questions.

**BG 5.22**: Verified (exists; translation read) · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/5/22/
> "…such pleasures have a beginning and an end, and so the wise man does not delight in them."
- **Context:** Krsna describes the person who has stopped being driven by sense pleasure.
- **Teaching:** Pleasures born of sense contact are temporary and are called "sources of misery" in the verse.
- **Purport point:** The purport says material pleasures are temporary because the body is temporary, and that
  a liberated person has tasted a higher pleasure.
- **Application (preacher's):** Invite the listener to check it: how long does the joy of a new phone, a
  result or a meal last? *(The verse does not say these things are bad. It says they begin and end.)*
- **Use:** in the talk.

**BG 2.70**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/2/70/
> "A person who is not disturbed by the incessant flow of desires… can alone achieve peace, and not the man who strives to satisfy such desires."
- **Context:** The end of Krsna's description of a steady mind in Chapter 2.
- **Teaching:** Satisfying desires one by one does not bring peace. The image is the ocean that is always filled yet always still.
- **Application (preacher's):** Desire often returns as soon as it is fulfilled. This is an observation the
  listener can check against their own experience.
- **Use:** in the talk.

**BG 18.37, 18.38, 18.39**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/18/37/ (and 38, 39)
> 18.38: "That happiness which is derived from contact of the senses with their objects and which appears like nectar at first but poison at the end is said to be of the nature of passion."
- **Teaching:** Three kinds of happiness. In 18.37 (goodness) it is like poison at first and nectar at the end
  and awakens self-realisation. In 18.38 (passion) it is nectar at first and poison at the end. In 18.39
  (ignorance) it is blind to self-realisation, "delusion from beginning to end", arising from sleep, laziness and illusion.
- **Context:** 18.36 introduces them as "the three kinds of happiness by which the conditioned soul enjoys".
  The purport to 18.36 says the conditioned soul "chews the chewed" and may be relieved by good association.
- **Application (preacher's):** This is a ready framework for *preyas* and *sreyas*: the first sweet then
  costly; the second demanding then rewarding. Use "like and good" in the talk and name the Gita's wording once.
  *(The words* preyas *and* sreyas *come from IYF's slides and Vedic literature in general; this page does not
  cite a verse for them, so do not attribute them to a specific verse.)*
- **Use:** in the talk.

**BG 6.20–23**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/6/20-23/
> "…one is situated in boundless transcendental happiness… This indeed is actual freedom from all miseries arising from material contact."
- **Teaching:** The yoga tradition speaks of a happiness experienced by the pure mind in the self, which does
  not shake "even in the midst of greatest difficulty".
- **Application (preacher's):** Present this as what the tradition holds is available, not as something the
  listener must already believe. VL1 has not yet taught how it is reached.
- **Use:** in the talk, lightly.

**BG 5.21**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/5/21/ — a self-realised person "enjoys unlimited
happiness, for he concentrates on the Supreme". Use: background.

**For questions (K):**
- **BG 2.66** (Verified): without a steady mind there is "no possibility of peace. And how can there be any
  happiness without peace?" The purport links this to BG 5.29.
- **BG 15.7** (Verified): living beings are the Lord's "eternal fragmental parts" struggling hard with the six
  senses including the mind. Helps with "why do we want unlimited happiness?"
- **SB 1.5.18** (Verified): "As far as happiness derived from sense enjoyment is concerned, it can be obtained
  automatically in course of time, just as in course of time we obtain miseries even though we do not desire
  them." Helps with "so should I not work for anything?" (see K 3).

## F. Philosophical explanation

1. **The principle.** Seeking happiness is natural. The Gita does not condemn it; it asks whether the *means*
   fit the *end*. Pleasure from the senses is real, but it begins and ends (BG 5.22), and the desire it feeds is
   not satisfied by feeding it (BG 2.70). A different kind of happiness, in the self, is described in BG 6.20–23.
2. **Why it matters.** If the means do not fit the end, effort turns to disappointment. IYF's slides call
   the result "hollow" happiness and "unfulfilment". A listener who sees this early can choose carefully.
3. **Connections.** To the self as distinct from the body (VL1, VL2), to the mind (VL1-03), to habits (VL1-04),
   and later to devotional service as the source of the higher happiness (VL3 and above).
4. **Consequences.** People who distinguish "I like it" from "it is good for me" make steadier choices about
   study, friends, screens, food and money.
5. **Misunderstandings to prevent.**
   - **"Spirituality means giving up enjoyment."** Not what this talk says. It asks about duration and
     depth, not about guilt.
   - **"Success makes you miserable."** Not supported. See O: research finds that more money is generally
     linked with more happiness, not less.
   - **"Happiness is a feeling to be switched on by belief."** The Gita speaks of a result of practice (BG 6.20).
   - **Spiritual bypassing.** Spiritual ideas never replace friendship, rest, medical or psychological help.
6. **Pitch for VL1.** Everyday words: "happiness", "lasting", "like vs good". Use *atma* once if needed. No
   appeal to authority: "Here is what an ancient text observed. Check it against your own life."

## H. Opening and engagement

**H.1 How to start (first 5 minutes)**
1. Greet, give your name, and say this is a shared question, not a lecture on what to believe.
2. Hand out slips or ask everyone to write one thing they hope will make them happy this year.
3. Collect three or four answers on the board, without comment, thanking each speaker.
4. Ask H.2 question 1 and take two answers.
5. Give the transition line below and begin "Where do ideas of happiness come from?"

**H.2 Opening questions** (about the room's own life)
1. Think of a moment this month when you felt properly happy. What was happening?
2. What did you buy or achieve last year that you thought would change your mood? Did it?
3. If your best friend asked "how do I become happy?", what would you say?

*Adapting:* "last year's purchase" can be a phone, a motorbike, a field tool or a promotion; use what fits your listeners.

- **Opening question:** "Write down one thing you think will make you really happy in the next year. Now, how
  long do you think the happiness will last after you get it?" Pause. "Today we will look at what an ancient
  text noticed about exactly this."
- **Relatable scenario (invented):** A student saves for months to buy a phone. Two weeks after it arrives,
  he is looking at the next model. *(Invented case for discussion; not a real incident.)*
- **Transition:** "Maybe wanting more is simply human. The question is whether we are looking in the right place."
- **Why it matters to this audience:** Exams, jobs, relationships, social media comparison. These are the
  places where young people actually look for happiness.

## I. Core teaching points

1. **We are told what happiness is.** Ideas of happiness reach us from adverts, films and friends. Test them (IYF Module 1).
   *Example:* ask what an advert promises and what you felt a week later. *Transition:* so how do we test?
2. **Sense pleasure begins and ends.** BG 5.22. *Example:* the first and the tenth bite of a favourite dish.
   *Transition:* if it ends, what do we do?
3. **Satisfying desire does not end desire.** BG 2.70. *Example:* "just one more episode". *Transition:*
   so there must be a difference between kinds of happiness.
4. **Like vs good.** BG 18.37–38. *Example:* a late-night scroll versus a morning walk or study that feels
   hard at first. *Transition:* is there something better still?
5. **A higher happiness is possible.** BG 6.20–23. *Example:* people who sit quietly with a deep interest
   and lose track of time. *(Application, preacher's.)* *Transition:* to the takeaway.

## J. Stories and illustrations

- **The queen and her lost necklace** is on IYF's Module 2 slide (title only; the notes do not give the text).
  If you know the story, tell it briefly as an *illustration* of searching in the wrong place, and say
  where it comes from. This page does not cite a source for it, so do not present it as scripture.
- **The ocean of desires** (BG 2.70): use Krsna's own image of the ocean that is always filled and always still.
- **Two analogies from the Preaching Encyclopedia** (HG Radheshyam Das, VOICE; IYF Books ch. 3, "The Quest for
  Happiness", which opens with the same BG 5.22 this page leads with): the child's **sandcastle** that the parents
  smile at while forgetting that time will take their own acquisitions; and the **passengers** who, warned that the
  train carries a bomb, discuss curtains and cushioned seats instead of getting off. Both are the author's
  teaching analogies, not scripture; say so, and use them for "searching in the wrong place".
- **Contemporary:** the "next phone" experience (invented, labelled as such).

IYF's slides also name well-known people who died by suicide. **Do not name individuals, and do not draw a
conclusion about their reasons.** See K.

## K. Anticipated questions and objections

> **Care note:** Someone in the room may be struggling with low mood or may have lost someone to suicide. Do
> not describe methods, do not name individuals, and do not claim that money or fame causes suicide; suicide
> has many causes. If a participant seems distressed, speak to them privately and encourage them to contact a
> doctor or a counsellor or a helpline. Professional help is fully compatible with spiritual life
> *(pastoral guidance, not a scriptural claim)*.

1. **"Isn't wanting to be happy natural? What's wrong with it?"**
   - Nothing. The Gita asks whether our methods fit the goal. The verse says sense pleasures are temporary
     (BG 5.22); it does not say the wish is wrong.
2. **"Money makes me happy. Isn't that obvious?"**
   - Research suggests money is often linked with happiness (O). The Gita's point is narrower: it does not
     say wealth is bad; it says peace does not come from striving to satisfy desires (BG 2.70).
3. **"So should I stop working for anything?"**
   - No. Arjuna is told to act. SB 1.5.18 says that the happiness sense enjoyment gives comes "automatically
     in course of time", as miseries do, and counsels effort for what is lasting. Present it as an idea to
     weigh, not an order.
4. **"Is this just a religion's way of selling its product?"**
   - Fair question. Invite testing: "Here is the claim. Check it." We are not asking for belief today.
5. **"How can anyone prove a higher happiness exists?"**
   - Not provable in one talk. The tradition holds it is experienced through practice (BG 6.20–23). Offer
     the later sessions (VL1-03, VL1-04) as ways to start testing.
6. **"I'm unhappy and nothing helps."**
   - *Pastoral guidance:* thank them, speak privately, suggest professional support, and do not treat the
     talk as therapy.

## L. Case studies

*Invented cases for discussion. Not real incidents.*

**Case 1: The saved-up phone.** Dev works part-time for three months to buy a phone. A fortnight later, he is
unhappy again and eyeing a newer one.
- **Principles:** BG 5.22; BG 2.70.
- **Questions:** What did Dev expect? What *did* he get? What would "good", not just "liked", look like for him?
- **Suggested analysis:** The purchase was fine; the expectation (lasting happiness from it) was the issue.
  The talk's question is about what the thing can and cannot give.
- **Takeaway:** Enjoy things; do not ask them to carry all your hopes.

**Case 2: Late night or early start.** Meera has a deadline. Scrolling feels good tonight; working feels
heavy. She notices she feels better after a hard study session than after hours online.
- **Principles:** BG 18.37–38.
- **Questions:** Which is "poison first, nectar later"? Is the reverse also true sometimes?
- **Takeaway:** Notice which choices pay later.

## M. Discussion and reflection

**M.1 Closing check questions** (easy first; praise every try)
1. Does the Gita tell us to stop wanting happiness? *No; it asks whether our methods fit the goal.*
2. What does BG 5.22 say about pleasures from the senses? *They have a beginning and an end.*
3. What does BG 2.70 say about satisfying desires? *Feeding desire does not make desire stop; peace comes from steadiness.*
4. In your own words, what is "like" and what is "good"? *Like attracts now; good benefits in the long run (BG 18.37-38).*
5. Which kind of happiness is "nectar first, poison later"? *The kind from sense contact (BG 18.38).*
6. Does the income research say money makes people miserable? *No; more income is generally linked with more reported happiness.*

**M.2 Activity (about 8 minutes): "Like or good?"**
1. Write six everyday choices on the board (late-night scroll, morning walk, extra sleep, exam revision, a sweet snack, calling a friend).
2. 3 min: in pairs, sort each as "like", "good" or "both" and give one reason.
3. 3 min: two or three pairs share one that surprised them; thank each pair.
4. 2 min: link back to BG 18.37-38 and invite one "good" choice for this week (private).

**M.3 Reflection**
- **Reflection:** Think of one thing you wanted a lot and then got. What happened to the excitement after a week?
- **Application (private, optional):** Pick one daily habit. Is it "nectar first, poison later" or the reverse? *(Nobody needs to share.)*

# Part 2: For students

## S1. Summary

Everyone wants to be happy. The Bhagavad-gita does not tell us to stop wanting it. It asks a question: are we looking in the right way? Ideas about happiness reach us from adverts, films and friends, so it is worth testing them against our own experience. Think of something you wanted badly and got: how long did the happiness last?

The Gita says pleasures that come from the senses have a beginning and an end (BG 5.22), and that feeding a desire does not make desires stop; peace comes to the one who stays steady (BG 2.70). So there is a difference between what we *like* now and what is *good* for us in the long run. Some happiness is nectar at first and poison at the end; some feels hard at first but is rewarding later (BG 18.37-38). Money and success help, and research finds more income is generally linked with more reported happiness; the Gita's point is only that sense pleasure alone does not satisfy.

The Gita also describes a deeper happiness found in the self (BG 6.20-23). You do not have to believe any of this today. You can test it against your own life, and if you feel low most of the time, talk to someone you trust or a counsellor; that fits well with spiritual life.

## S2. Key ideas

- What we *like* right now and what is *good* for us are not always the same.
- Pleasure from the senses begins and ends (BG 5.22).
- Feeding a desire does not make desire stop (BG 2.70).
- Some happiness is sweet first and costly later; some is demanding first and rewarding later (BG 18.37–38).
- A deeper happiness is described, which we can explore (BG 6.20–23).

## S3. Verses to remember

- **BG 5.22:** Pleasures from contact with the senses "have a beginning and an end". *For me:* enjoy them, but do not expect them to last.
- **BG 2.70:** Peace comes to the one who is not disturbed by the flow of desires, "and not the man who strives to satisfy such desires". *For me:* more is not always enough.
- **BG 18.38:** Happiness from the senses "appears like nectar at first but poison at the end". *For me:* think about how a choice will feel later.

## S4. Words to know

- **Preyas / sreyas:** the pleasant and the good. A pair of ideas used in Vedic teaching; here: "what I like now" and "what is good for me".
- **Self (atma):** the real "I", who is more than the body.

## S5. Questions and answers

1. **Is it wrong to want to be happy?** No. The question is whether our methods fit the goal.
2. **Does money make people happy?** Research finds more money is generally linked with more happiness. The Gita's point is that sense pleasure alone does not satisfy desire.
3. **Should I stop working for things?** No. Arjuna was told to act. The idea is to think about what lasts.
4. **Can anyone prove a higher happiness exists?** Not in a talk. The tradition says it is experienced through practice. You can start to test.
5. **What if I feel low most of the time?** That is not a sign of failure. Speak to someone you trust, and consider a doctor or counsellor. Seeking help fits well with spiritual life.

## S6. Check your understanding

1. According to BG 5.22, pleasures from the senses: (a) last forever, (b) have a beginning and an end, (c) are always sinful.
2. In BG 18.38, which happiness is "nectar at first but poison at the end"?
3. True or false: the Gita says wanting to be happy is wrong.
4. In your own words: what is the difference between "like" and "good"?
5. Does the research on income say that money makes people miserable?

**Answers:** 1. (b) (BG 5.22). 2. Happiness from contact of the senses with their objects (BG 18.38). 3. False; it asks if our means fit our goal. 4. "Like" is what attracts me now; "good" is what benefits me in the long run. 5. No; more income is generally linked with more reported happiness (Killingsworth, Kahneman & Mellers, 2023).
