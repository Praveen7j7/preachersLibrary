# Why do bad things happen to good people

> **VL1 · 45–60 min · PILOT · review: sources checked, awaiting senior review.** Sensitive: some listeners
> may be grieving. Read the note in K before teaching.

## A. Topic overview

| | |
|---|---|
| Batch / level | VL1 (Vedic Level 1), a stand-alone talk |
| Suggested duration | 45–60 min (adjust to the slot; G gives a 30-minute cut) |
| Preparation time | About 90 min, the first time |
| Difficulty | Introductory |
| Prerequisites | None |
| Audience | Students and young professionals, many with no settled faith; some may have lost someone or be in a hard time now |
| Teaching objective | Take a question everyone asks seriously and show that the Bhagavad-gita offers a deeper way to see it (who I am, what this world is, how to carry pain), without claiming to explain every tragedy |

## B. Why this topic belongs at this level

Suffering is often the moment a young person first asks spiritual questions. The Gita itself names "the
distressed" among the four kinds of people who begin to turn to God (BG 7.16). At VL1 the aim is
**attraction through honesty**:
- show that this tradition faces the question squarely;
- give the listener one or two ideas that change how they see their own difficulties;
- leave them curious.

How the subject grows at other levels:

| Level | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **VL1** (here) | Experiential. The soul is not the body; pain is temporary; not every suffering can or should be explained; blaming sufferers is wrong |
| VL2 | Argued philosophy: reincarnation (VL2-01) and karma as a system. *Proposed:* a VL2 topic "Karma: what it explains and what it does not" (Stage 2 §7.1) |
| VL3 | Taking shelter of the holy name in difficulty, as a practice to try |
| SPS | Tolerance as a daily practice, and the devotee's mood of seeing difficulty as mercy |
| DPS | Carrying others' pain: care for those you serve without absorbing it |
| BSS | Austerity in brahmacari life (celibate student life): hardship that trains rather than breaks |

**Not done here:**
- A full theory of karma.
- Asking for faith in scripture as a premise.
- Suggesting that anyone in the room deserves what they suffer.

## C. Central message

We cannot always explain why a particular person suffers. Even great sages could not. But the Gita
changes the question from *"Why me?"* to *"Who am I, and what is this world?"*, and it teaches us how to
carry pain without being destroyed by it.

## D. Learning outcomes

By the end, participants should be able to:
1. Name three common answers to suffering (it is all random; it is all my fault; God is unfair) and say
   why each falls short.
2. Explain in plain words the Gita's teaching that the self is not the body, and that happiness and
   distress come and go like seasons (BG 2.13–14).
3. Recognise why judging *why someone else suffers* is wrong and unkind (the King Pariksit narrative).
4. Choose one way to respond differently to a current difficulty.

## E. Scriptural foundation

**Use:** six references are enough for the talk. The rest prepare you for questions.

**BG 2.13**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/2/13/
> "As the embodied soul continuously passes, in this body, from boyhood to youth to old age, the soul similarly passes into another body at death. A sober person is not bewildered by such a change."
- **Context:** Krsna's first teaching to Arjuna, who is overwhelmed by grief before the battle.
- **Teaching:** The self continues through all changes of the body.
- **Application (preacher's):** Whatever happens to my body, my health or my circumstances, *I* am not
  reduced to it.
- **Use:** in the talk.

**BG 2.14**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/2/14/
> "…the nonpermanent appearance of happiness and distress, and their disappearance in due course, are like the appearance and disappearance of winter and summer seasons… one must learn to tolerate them without being disturbed."
- **Teaching:** Pleasure and pain come and go; tolerance is to be *learned*.
- **Purport point:** Prabhupada's examples are duties done despite the weather, such as bathing early in a
  cold month or cooking in the hottest season. Tolerance means carrying on with what matters, not feeling
  nothing.
- **Application (preacher's):** Tolerance is a skill, built like any skill. It is not denial.
- **Use:** in the talk.

**SB 1.17.18–20**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/sb/1/17/18/ (and 19, 20)
- **Context:** King Pariksit finds a bull (the personality of religion) beaten and asks who did it.
- **Teaching:** The bull answers that it is hard to say who caused his suffering, because philosophers
  disagree:
  - some say one's own self is responsible;
  - others say superhuman powers;
  - others say activity (karma);
  - others say nature;
  - and some say the cause cannot be known by argument, imagination or words (1.17.19–20).

  He then asks the king to judge for himself.
- **Application (preacher's):** The scripture itself admits that the question is hard. That honesty is
  the opening of the talk.
- **Use:** in the talk (as a story).

**SB 1.17.22**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/sb/1/17/22/
- **Teaching:** The king praises the bull's principle: "the destination intended for the perpetrator of
  irreligious acts is also intended for one who identifies the perpetrator."
- **Purport point:** A devotee does not hold anyone directly responsible without the Lord's sanction; he
  tolerates reverses, and "the identifier is equally a sinner, like the mischief-monger."
- **Application (preacher's):** If even naming the cause of another's suffering is treated so seriously,
  we should never tell a grieving person "it is your karma". *(The verse is about not identifying a
  wrongdoer. Using it against victim-blaming is our application, and should be presented that way.)*
- **Use:** in the talk.

**SB 1.9.12, 1.9.16**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/sb/1/9/16/
> 1.9.16: "O King, no one can know the plan of the Lord [Śrī Kṛṣṇa]. Even though great philosophers inquire exhaustively, they are bewildered."
- **Context:** Bhisma, dying on the bed of arrows, speaks to Yudhisthira. He laments the "terrible
  sufferings and terrible injustices" the Pandavas endured although they were righteous (1.9.12).
- **Purport point (1.9.16):** Prabhupada states plainly: "The sufferings of the Pāṇḍavas were never due to
  their past deeds." Good people can suffer for reasons that are not punishment.
- **Application (preacher's):** "Bad things happen because of bad karma" is not the whole teaching.
  Scripture gives a famous counter-example.
- **Use:** in the talk. This is the strongest single reference for this topic.

**SB 1.8.25**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/sb/1/8/25/
> "I wish that all those calamities would happen again and again so that we could see You again and again, for seeing You means that we will no longer see repeated births and deaths."
- **Context:** Queen Kunti, after a lifetime of dangers, prays to Krsna.
- **Purport point:** For one who turns to the Lord, calamities become an opportunity to remember Him.
- **Application (preacher's):** Present this as *Kunti's* realisation, not as a demand on the audience.
  Nobody should be told to welcome their pain.
- **Use:** in the talk, carefully, near the end.

**BG 7.16**: Verified · https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/7/16/
- **Teaching:** Four kinds of pious people begin devotional service: the distressed, the desirer of wealth,
  the inquisitive and the seeker of knowledge.
- **Purport point:** These are not yet pure devotees, since they come with some aspiration. It is a
  beginning, not a judgement.
- **Use:** background (explains why this topic is a natural VL1 entry).

**For questions (K):** BG 5.15 (the Lord does not take on anyone's sinful or pious acts; ignorance covers
knowledge); BG 9.29 (the Lord is equal to all); BG 13.22 (contact with nature's modes brings "good and
evil among various species"); BG 4.17 (the intricacies of action are very hard to understand). All Verified.

SB 10.14.8 is for higher levels only and is kept in R.1.

## F. Philosophical explanation

1. **The principle.** Suffering in this world is real. Its causes are many: one's own past actions,
   others' actions, nature, time, and the Lord's plan, which we cannot see in full (SB 1.17.19–20;
   SB 1.9.16). The Gita's answer is less a full explanation than a **change of standpoint**: I am an eternal
   self passing through a temporary body (BG 2.13), in a world where pleasure and pain come and go
   (BG 2.14).
2. **Why it matters.** Every popular answer fails someone:
   - "It's random" leaves no meaning.
   - "It's your fault" crushes the grieving.
   - "God is unfair" leaves only anger.

   A deeper identity gives a place to stand.
3. **Connections.** It leads to reincarnation and karma (VL2), the modes of nature (BG 13.22), and later
   tolerance as devotion.
4. **Consequences.** People who see themselves as more than their circumstances can grieve fully without
   losing themselves. They become slower to judge others, and some start asking what is permanent.
5. **Misunderstandings to prevent.**
   - **Karma as blame.** Karma is real (BG 4.17 says it is intricate), but scripture itself shows suffering
     that is not punishment (SB 1.9.16 purport). Never use karma to explain someone else's tragedy.
   - **Tolerance as numbness.** BG 2.14 asks us to keep doing what matters, not to stop feeling.
   - **Spiritual bypassing.** Spiritual ideas never replace grief, care, or medical and psychological help.
6. **Pitch for VL1.** Speak of "the self", "the body", "a deeper identity". The words *atma* and *karma*
   can be used once each and defined. No appeal to authority: "Here is how an ancient text approached
   this. See if it makes sense to you."

**Main plan (one flow, 45–60 min; extra time goes to the Pandavas and quiet questions)**

| Time | Segment | Notes |

| 5–12 | Three common answers and why each falls short | Write them up: "random", "my fault", "God is unfair" |
| 12–20 | The bull and King Pariksit (SB 1.17) | Scripture admits the question is hard; the king refuses to judge |
| 20–30 | The shift: "Who am I?" | BG 2.13 (seasons of the body), BG 2.14 (seasons of experience); use the diagram in N |
| 30–36 | Good people suffer too: the Pandavas | SB 1.9.12, 16 and the purport line "never due to their past deeds" |
| 36–41 | What we can do: tolerance as a skill; turning towards something lasting | Kunti's prayer as *her* realisation (SB 1.8.25) |
| 41–45 | Summary + one action + invitation | P |

**30-minute cut:** drop the Pandavas section and shorten the three answers to two minutes.

**Alternative structure (dialogue):** two volunteers read a short invented exchange between a grieving
student and a friend who keeps giving glib answers. The group critiques the friend. The preacher then
introduces BG 2.13–14 and SB 1.9.16 as better answers. This works well for groups that dislike lectures.

## H. Opening and engagement

**H.1 How to start (first 5 minutes)**
1. Greet, give your name, and say plainly that some in the room may be carrying a loss; nobody has to share.
2. Say the talk will not pretend to a neat answer, and that listening is enough.
3. Ask for a show of hands for "that wasn't fair" (H.2 question 1); thank the room.
4. Give the one-line scenario below (exam, or crop and harvest for a rural audience).
5. Use the transition and move to the three common answers.

**H.2 Opening questions** (about the room's own life; hands up, no stories)
1. Have you ever thought: "that wasn't fair"?
2. Who do you know, a good person, who went through a hard time?
3. What helped you, or them, carry it?

- **Opening question:** "Raise your hand if you've ever thought: *that wasn't fair*." Pause. "Today we
  won't pretend to have a neat answer. But we'll look at a way of seeing that has helped people for
  thousands of years."
- **Relatable scenario (invented; adapt to your listeners, e.g. two farmers or two colleagues):** Two classmates prepare for an exam. One studies honestly and falls
  ill the week before; the other cheats and tops the class. What do you feel? What do you conclude about
  life?
- **Transition:** "A five-thousand-year-old text begins in exactly this place: a good man, Arjuna,
  overwhelmed by a situation he finds unbearable. Its first answer is not about the situation. It is about
  *him*."
- **Why it matters to this audience:** Exams, health, family problems, breakups, a friend's death. Nobody
  in the room is untouched. How we understand suffering shapes how we live.

## I. Core teaching points

1. **Honesty first.** Even scripture says the cause of suffering is hard to pin down (SB 1.17.18–20).
   *Example:* the bull's answer. *Transition:* if the cause is hard to see, perhaps the first question is a
   different one.
2. **You are more than what is happening to you.** The self passes through childhood, youth and age, and
   beyond (BG 2.13). *Example:* the person in your childhood photos is "you", though every cell has
   changed. *Transition:* if I am not only this body, then what happens to it is not all of me.
3. **Experiences come and go like seasons.** Tolerance is a learned skill (BG 2.14). *Example:* athletes
   train in heat and cold; they feel it and continue. *Transition:* but what about suffering that seems to
   punish the innocent?
4. **Suffering is not always punishment.** The Pandavas suffered "never due to their past deeds"
   (SB 1.9.16 purport). Don't judge others' suffering (SB 1.17.22). *Example:* how it feels to be told "it's
   your karma" when you are grieving. *Transition:* so what can we do with pain?
5. **Pain can turn us toward what lasts.** The distressed are among those who begin to seek God (BG 7.16).
   Kunti saw her calamities as chances to remember Krsna (SB 1.8.25). *Example:* many people trace their
   most important life changes to a hard period. *Transition:* to the takeaway.

## J. Stories and illustrations

- **The bull, the cow and King Pariksit** (SB 1.17.18–22). Tell it in your own words. Keep to what the text
  says: the bull refuses to name his attacker and lists the philosophers' theories; the king recognises
  him as religion personified. *Connection:* honesty about the question; restraint in judging.
- **The Pandavas' sufferings and Bhisma's words** (SB 1.9.12–16). Stick to Bhisma's words and the purport's
  conclusion; don't add details. *Connection:* the righteous suffer; the plan is larger than we see.
- **Kunti's prayer** (SB 1.8.25). One sentence of context from her own words (SB 1.8.24): Krsna had
  protected the family from a poisoned cake, a great fire, cannibals, a vicious assembly, exile in the
  forest and the battle, and still she prayed this way. *Connection:* her realisation, offered, not imposed.
- **Contemporary:** the photo-album illustration for BG 2.13 (a common teaching image, not a claim about any
  real person).

## K. Anticipated questions and objections

> **Before you teach:** someone in the room may be grieving or depressed. If a question becomes personal,
> acknowledge the person, don't analyse their situation in public, and offer to talk afterwards. If you see
> signs of serious distress, encourage professional help; it is fully compatible with spiritual life. Never
> say "it is your karma" about anyone's loss.

1. **"If God is good, why does He allow suffering at all?"**
   - The Gita says the Lord is equal to everyone (BG 9.29) and does not take on anyone's sinful or pious
     acts. We are bewildered because ignorance covers our knowledge (BG 5.15). The purport adds that the
     Lord "does not create a particular situation for any living entity"; the living being's own desires
     set the chain of action and reaction moving.
   - Be honest that this is a *direction*, not a proof that removes every difficulty. Sages themselves call
     the Lord's plan beyond full understanding (SB 1.9.16).
2. **"So is everything karma? Did a child with cancer deserve it?"**
   - No one has the right to say that. Karma is real but "very hard to understand" (BG 4.17), and scripture
     gives cases of suffering not caused by past deeds (SB 1.9.16 purport). Our part is compassion, not
     verdicts (SB 1.17.22).
   - *Supporting (added 28 Oct 2026, attributed):* Chaitanya Charan Das's **grain-store analogy** (O): in a
     village store fresh grain goes in at the top and old grain comes out at the bottom, so what reaches a person
     now may be from long ago; and most people's record is mixed, not purely good. It makes "hard to understand"
     (BG 4.17) picturable without claiming to explain any one case. His talk on the same title ends where this
     page does: "the focus needs to be on not why this happened but what to do."
3. **"Isn't this just a way to make people accept injustice?"**
   - No. Arjuna is told to act, not to withdraw. Tolerance in BG 2.14 is about carrying on with one's duty
     despite discomfort, not about tolerating wrongdoing against others. Fighting injustice and keeping
     inner steadiness go together.
4. **"Why should I believe in a soul at all?"**
   - At VL1, invite exploration rather than demand belief: the continuity of "I" through bodily change
     (BG 2.13) is something to reflect on. The full discussion is at VL2 (VL2-01 "Reincarnation: fact or
     fiction").
5. **"What if I feel angry with God?"**
   - *Pastoral guidance, not a scriptural claim:* say that honest feelings are better than pretended peace,
     and that questions asked sincerely are the start of inquiry, not its failure. Arjuna's own crisis
     (BG 2.13 is Krsna's reply to it) is the frame of this whole talk. *(If you want to add a scriptural
     example of a devotee voicing pain to God, verify it first; none is included here.)*

## L. Case studies

*Invented cases for discussion. Not real incidents.*

**Case 1: The phone call.** Riya's friend Aman lost his father suddenly. At the funeral, a relative tells
Aman, "It must be some karma from a past life." Aman is hurt and angry, and later asks Riya, "Is that true?"
- **Dilemma:** What should Riya say, and not say?
- **Principles:** SB 1.17.22 (restraint in assigning cause); SB 1.9.16 and purport (suffering is not always
  punishment); BG 2.13 (the father's self continues).
- **Questions:**
  - What was wrong with the relative's statement, even if karma is real?
  - What does Aman need right now?
  - When, if ever, is it appropriate to talk philosophy with him?
- **Suggested analysis:** Presence before explanation. Riya can say that nobody can know why, that she is
  there for him, and, if he asks later, that many traditions (including the Gita) see the self as
  continuing beyond the body.
- **Takeaway:** Philosophy serves compassion, never replaces it.

**Case 2: The unfair exam.** Kabir studied honestly and failed because he fell ill; a classmate cheated and
topped the exam. Kabir concludes, "Being good is pointless."
- **Principles:** BG 2.14 (temporary results); BG 2.13 (deeper identity); BG 4.17 (intricate action and
  reaction).
- **Questions:**
  - Is Kabir's conclusion logical?
  - What does "success" mean if the self is not just this body and this term?
  - What can Kabir do this week?
- **Takeaway:** One result doesn't define a life; character is something we keep.

## M. Discussion and reflection

**M.1 Closing check questions** (easy first; praise every try)
1. In BG 2.14, happiness and distress are compared to what? *The seasons: they come and go.*
2. Name one of the three common answers to suffering. *Random, my fault, or God is unfair.*
3. Why did King Pariksit praise the bull who would not name his attacker? *Judging the cause of another's suffering is serious (SB 1.17).*
4. Do the scriptures say every suffering is a punishment? *No; the Pandavas' sufferings were not due to past deeds (SB 1.9.16, purport).*
5. What does "Who am I?" change? *We see ourselves as the self who lives through bodily changes (BG 2.13).*
6. Does tolerance mean accepting injustice? *No; Arjuna is asked to act and stay steady inside.*

**M.2 Activity (about 8 minutes): "What I would say to a friend"**
1. Read a short invented exchange: a friend gives glib answers to a grieving person (use an invented name, no real case).
2. 3 min: in pairs, rewrite the friend's lines to be kind and honest.
3. 3 min: volunteers read a rewritten line; thank each; praise listening over fixing.
4. 2 min: link to BG 2.14 and SB 1.9.16 as better answers.

**M.3 Reflection**
- **Reflection:** Which of the three common answers have you believed at some point? What changes if "I" am more than my body?
- **Application (private, optional):** Think of one difficulty you're carrying now. What would "tolerance as a skill" look like with it this week? *(Nobody needs to share.)*

## P. Practical takeaway

- **Main lesson:** I am more than what is happening to me, and I am not the judge of why others suffer.
- **One action (VL1):** Next time something goes wrong this week, pause and say to yourself, "This is a
  season, not my identity." Notice what changes.
- **Reflection question:** If I am not only this body, what part of me is untouched by today's problem?
- **Follow-up:** VL1-05 "Absolute necessity of spirituality"; at VL2, VL2-01 "Reincarnation: fact or
  fiction". Reading: *Teachings of Queen Kunti* (selected chapters).

## Q. Preacher's self-review checklist

- [ ] I can state the central message without notes.
- [ ] I have read SB 1.9.16 and its purport myself, and can quote the line about the Pandavas accurately.
- [ ] I can explain BG 2.13–14 without Sanskrit.
- [ ] I am ready for "Did a child deserve cancer?" without blaming anyone.
- [ ] I have not presented Kunti's prayer as an instruction to the audience.

# Part 2: For students

## S1. Summary

Everyone meets something that feels unfair: illness, failure, losing someone. Even the ancient texts admit that the cause of suffering is hard to pin down, and great sages said no one can fully know the Lord's plan. Three common answers fall short: "it is random" leaves no meaning, "it is my fault" crushes the grieving, and "God is unfair" leaves only anger.

In the Bhagavatam, King Pariksit (a righteous ruler) meets a bull being beaten, and the bull refuses to name his attacker; the king praises him, because deciding why someone suffers is itself serious (SB 1.17.22). The Pandavas, Arjuna's family, were good people who suffered greatly, and the text says their sufferings were not due to their past deeds (SB 1.9.16, purport). So we must never say a sufferer deserved it.

The Bhagavad-gita changes the question from "Why me?" to "Who am I?". I am the self who lives through the changes of the body, as I stayed the same person from childhood until now (BG 2.13). Happiness and distress come and go like winter and summer, and we can learn to carry them (BG 2.14). Tolerance does not mean accepting injustice; Arjuna is asked to act, steady inside. If you are grieving, talk to someone you trust or a counsellor.

## S2. Key ideas

- It's honest to say: we can't always explain why a particular person suffers.
- I am more than my body and my circumstances (BG 2.13).
- Good and bad times come and go like seasons; tolerance is a skill we can build (BG 2.14).
- Suffering is not always a punishment. Don't judge others' pain.
- Hard times can turn us toward what lasts.

## S3. Verses to remember

- **BG 2.13:** The self passes from childhood to youth to old age, and on beyond this body. *For me:* whatever
  happens to my body or situation, it is not all of me.
- **BG 2.14:** Happiness and distress come and go like winter and summer; "one must learn to tolerate them
  without being disturbed." *For me:* tolerance is learned, like any skill.
- **SB 1.9.16:** "No one can know the plan of the Lord." *For me:* I can be humble about what I don't
  understand.

## S4. Words to know

- **Self (ātmā):** the real "I", which lives in the body but is not the body.
- **Karma:** action and its reaction. Real, but "very hard to understand" (BG 4.17), so it is never a reason to
  blame someone who is suffering.
- **Tolerance:** carrying on with what matters while feeling the difficulty. Not the same as feeling nothing.

## S5. Questions and answers

1. **If God is good, why is there suffering?**
   The Gita says the Lord is equal to everyone (BG 9.29) and does not take on anyone's sinful or pious acts.
   We are confused because ignorance covers our knowledge (BG 5.15). It is a direction for thinking, not a
   formula that answers every case.
2. **Does everyone who suffers deserve it?**
   No one has the right to say that. Scripture tells of the Pandavas, good people whose sufferings were "never
   due to their past deeds" (SB 1.9.16, purport).
3. **Does tolerance mean I should accept injustice?**
   No. Arjuna is asked to act, not to give up. Tolerance means staying steady inside while doing what is right.
4. **Why should I believe in a soul?**
   Start with an experiment, not a belief. Notice that "I" stayed the same person through every change from
   childhood until now (BG 2.13). VL2 discusses this in depth.

## S6. Check your understanding

1. In BG 2.14, happiness and distress are compared to: (a) day and night, (b) winter and summer seasons,
   (c) rivers and the sea.
2. True or false: the scriptures say every suffering is a punishment for past deeds.
3. In SB 1.17, why did King Pariksit praise the bull who would not name his attacker?
4. In your own words: what is the difference between tolerance and not feeling anything?
5. Name one of the three common answers to suffering, and say why it falls short.

**Answers:** 1. (b) (BG 2.14). 2. False: the Pandavas suffered not because of past deeds (SB 1.9.16, purport).
3. Because judging the cause of another's suffering is itself serious; the destination of the wrongdoer is
also for one who identifies him (SB 1.17.22). 4. Tolerance means continuing what matters while feeling the
difficulty (BG 2.14 and purport). 5. "Random" leaves no meaning; "my fault" crushes the grieving; "God is
unfair" leaves only anger.
