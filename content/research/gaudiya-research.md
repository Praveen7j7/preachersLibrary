# Gaudiya Research

A hub of scholarly and empirical research relevant to Gaudiya Vaisnava teaching and practice, for IYF preachers. Every paper below was checked against Crossref (`tools/find_research.py doi`). Where one of our topic pages already cites a paper, the topic ids are listed under *Cited on*.

**Research supports educational claims only, never theology.** Studies can show what chanting, diet or values do for people, or what scholars find in the texts. They cannot prove or disprove siddhanta. Most studies are not of Gaudiya practice; where a study is of another tradition, its entry says so.

## How a preacher should use this

- State findings at the strength the entry gives: say "is associated with" for correlational work, and name the group studied.
- Offer research as support for an educational point (habit, attention, gratitude), then let the shastra carry the siddhanta; never say "science proves".
- Quote the *Limits* line when asked; admitting limits builds trust with sceptical and youth audiences. Not a substitute for medical or psychological care.

## Gaudiya Vaisnava theology and history

**Gupta, R. M. (2007). "The Chaitanya Vaishnava Vedanta of Jiva Gosvami." Routledge (monograph).** https://doi.org/10.4324/9780203500682
*What it shows:* A book-length study of the philosophical foundations of Chaitanya Vaishnavism, examining Jiva Gosvami's hermeneutical tools, historical sources and the structure of his arguments.  
*Limits:* A single scholar's analysis of a philosophical tradition; written for specialists.

**Uskokov, A. (2018). "The Long and Short of It: Maha-vakya from Mimamsa to Jiva Gosvamin, from the Veda to the Bhagavata." The Journal of Hindu Studies.** https://doi.org/10.1093/jhs/hiy013
*What it shows:* Shows how Jiva drew on Mimamsa and Advaita Vedanta ideas of maha-vakya to argue that the whole Veda, a Bhagavata quarter-verse and the pranava Om each denote Krishna (centred on BhP 1.3.28).  
*Limits:* Textual and hermeneutical analysis of Jiva's argument; it explains how the claim is built, not whether it is true.

**Holdrege, B. A. (2024). "Sex, Gender, and Devotional Desire: Refiguring Bodily Identities in Gaudiya Vaisnava Discourse." International Journal of Hindu Studies.** https://doi.org/10.1007/s11407-024-09361-w
*What it shows:* Discusses sex, gender and the body in Gaudiya Vaisnava discourse, set against debates in feminist and gender studies.  
*Limits:* Scholarly interpretation of texts and discourse; abstract does not report any empirical data.

**Surpi, N. K. (2021). "Filsafat Ketuhanan Vaisnava Perspektif Pemikiran Caitanya Mahaprabhu: Doktrin Acintya Bhedabheda dan Budaya Bhakti dalam Teks Sri Siksastakam." Sanjiwani: Jurnal Filsafat.** https://doi.org/10.25078/sjf.v12i1.1823
*What it shows:* Presents acintya-bhedabheda as the central doctrine of Caitanya's teaching and links it to bhakti culture through the eight verses of the Siksastaka.  
*Limits:* Philosophical exposition in Indonesian; no empirical component.

## ISKCON and Srila Prabhupada

**Vande Berg, T. & Kniss, F. (2008). "Iskcon and Immigrants: The Rise, Decline, and Rise Again of a New Religious Movement." The Sociological Quarterly.** https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1533-8525.2007.00107.x
*What it shows:* A case study of the Chicago ISKCON temple: after decline in the 1980s, Indian immigration gave ISKCON new resources and a new target population, reviving it as a movement that differs from the original.  
*Limits:* One temple case study in the US; about membership and institution, not belief or practice quality.

**Rochford, E. B. (2000). "Demons, Karmies, and Non-devotees: Culture, Group Boundaries, and the Development of Hare Krishna in North America and Europe." Social Compass.** https://doi.org/10.1177/003776800047002003
*What it shows:* Argues that ISKCON's communal structure is eroding because it did not develop an internal culture supporting family life, so householders must seek work and raise families outside the movement.  
*Limits:* Sociological argument about North America and Europe; written in 2000 and may not describe ISKCON in India today.

**Dein, S. & Barlow, H. (1999). "Why do people join the Hare Krishna Movement? Deprivation theory revisited." Mental Health, Religion & Culture.** https://doi.org/10.1080/13674679908406334
*What it shows:* Reviews why people join new religious movements: rejects the view that members are mentally ill and finds relative and existential deprivation a useful (though problematic) psychological account of conversion.  
*Limits:* A theoretical discussion, not a new study; covers new religious movements in general.

**Pandya, S. P. (2017). "Food, faith, social service and institution building: the Annamrita programme of the Hare Krishna movement in India." Religion, State and Society.** https://doi.org/10.1080/09637494.2017.1281598
*What it shows:* Fieldwork on the Annamrita programme, ISKCON's food-relief service partnered with the government midday meal scheme, showing how food, faith and service build the institution.  
*Limits:* Fieldwork on one programme; a sociological account, not an evaluation of its outcomes.

**Fahy, J. (2018). "The Constructive Ambiguity of Vedic Culture in ISKCON Mayapur." The Journal of Hindu Studies.** https://doi.org/10.1093/jhs/hiy008
*What it shows:* Examines how ISKCON, which Prabhupada brought back to India in the early 1970s and which is now gaining momentum there, understands and uses 'Vedic culture' at Mayapur.  
*Limits:* Case study of one centre (Mayapur); not the whole movement.

## Bhakti and the Bhagavata (Indology and religious studies)

**Gupta, R. M. & Valpey, K. R. (2018). "Introduction: New Directions in Bhagavata Purana Studies." The Journal of Hindu Studies.** https://doi.org/10.1093/jhs/hiy006
*What it shows:* Introduces a set of studies on the Bhagavata Purana, describing its unusual place in Sanskrit literature as narrative woven with philosophical and theological reflection.  
*Limits:* Short editorial introduction to a journal issue; orients the reader, presents no new evidence.

**Edelmann, J. (2021). "Bhakti, Rasa, and Organizing Character Experience: Vopadeva, Sridhara, and Sanatana on Bhagavata Purana X.43.17." Journal of Dharma Studies.** https://doi.org/10.1007/s42240-021-00110-5
*What it shows:* Compares three commentators (Vopadeva, Sridhara, Sanatana) on Bhagavata Purana X.43.17, concerning bhakti and rasa (from the title; the publisher's abstract was not available to check).  
*Limits:* Close reading of a single verse and its commentaries; narrow in scope.

**Gupta, R. M. (2020). "Why Sridhara Svami? The Makings of a Successful Sanskrit Commentary." Religions.** https://doi.org/10.3390/rel11090436
*What it shows:* Asks why Sridhara Svami's Bhagavata commentary (Bhavartha-dipika) became so influential, focusing on Jiva Gosvami and the Caitanya tradition, for whom Sridhara is foundational.  
*Limits:* Historical and textual study of a commentary; not a study of practice.

**Chilcott, T. (2016). "Transforming Adverse Cognition on the Path of Bhakti: Rule-Based Devotion, "My-Ness," and the Existential Condition of Bondage." Religions.** https://doi.org/10.3390/rel7050049
*What it shows:* Describes how early Gaudiya theologians held that regulated devotional practices free the practitioner from possessive thoughts and feelings ('my-ness'), the cognitive core of bondage.  
*Limits:* Interpretation of the tradition's texts; no empirical test.

## Mantra meditation and kirtan

**Brown, S. B. (2021). "From Meditation to Bliss: Achieving the Heights of Progressive Spiritual Energy through Kirtan Singing in American Gaudiya Vaishnava Hinduism." Religions.** https://doi.org/10.3390/rel12080600
*What it shows:* Describes Gaudiya Vaishnava kirtan as call-and-response chanting that moves participants through a progression of states from meditation to bliss, in an American setting.  
*Limits:* Descriptive, qualitative account; measures no outcomes.

**Perry, G., Polito, V. & Thompson, W. F. (2021). "Rhythmic Chanting and Mystical States across Traditions." Brain Sciences.** https://doi.org/10.3390/brainsci11010101
*What it shows:* Survey of 464 regular chanters in 33 countries: 60% reported mystical states during chanting; these were linked to higher absorption, altruism and religiosity, with no difference between chanting traditions or between vocal, silent, group or solo chanting.  
*Limits:* Self-report survey, correlational; mixed traditions, not specific to the maha-mantra.

**Perry, G., Polito, V., Sankaran, N. & Thompson, W. F. (2022). "How Chanting Relates to Cognitive Function, Altered States and Quality of Life." Brain Sciences.** https://doi.org/10.3390/brainsci12111456
*What it shows:* Global survey of 456 regular chanters in 32 countries: stronger intentionality (devotion, intention, sound) and greater engagement went with altered states and cognitive benefits, which in turn went with quality of life.  
*Limits:* Self-report and correlational: cannot show that chanting caused the benefits; English-speaking respondents of many traditions.

**Gao, J. et al. (2019). "The neurophysiological correlates of religious chanting." Scientific Reports.** https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-019-40200-w
*What it shows:* Using electrophysiological and neuroimaging methods, shows that during religious chanting the posterior cingulate cortex shows the largest decrease in eigenvector centrality, and that this is likely different from other meditation.  
*Limits:* Lab neuroscience of one kind of chanting; the abstract gives no sample size here and the mantra studied is not the maha-mantra.

**Matko, K., Sedlmeier, P. & Bringmann, H. C. (2021). "Differential Effects of Ethical Education, Physical Hatha Yoga, and Mantra Meditation on Well-Being and Stress in Healthy Participants." Frontiers in Psychology.** https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.672301
*What it shows:* Forty-two healthy participants were randomised to mantra meditation alone or with yoga and/or ethical education for 8 weeks; almost all except the meditation-only group showed increased wellbeing, most with ethical education, and stress tended to fall.  
*Limits:* Single-case multiple-baseline design on healthy volunteers; mantra meditation of a yoga programme, not devotional chanting.

**Lynch, J. et al. (2018). "Mantra meditation for mental health in the general population: A systematic review." European Journal of Integrative Medicine.** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eujim.2018.09.010
*What it shows:* Systematic review of 37 studies of mantra meditation in non-clinical groups: may have minimal to moderate beneficial effects on anxiety, stress, depression and similar outcomes.  
*Limits:* 78% of studies used Transcendental Meditation and over 90% were rated weak quality.

**Alvarez-Perez, Y. et al. (2022). "Effectiveness of Mantra-Based Meditation on Mental Health: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis." International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health.** https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph19063380
*What it shows:* Meta-analysis of randomised trials of mantra-based meditation: small-to-moderate reductions in anxiety (g = -0.46), depression (-0.33), stress (-0.45) and post-traumatic stress (-0.59).  
*Limits:* Authors note risk of study bias and few studies with psychiatric samples or long follow-up; mostly Transcendental Meditation and similar techniques.

**Benson, H., Beary, J. F. & Carol, M. P. (1974). "The Relaxation Response." Psychiatry.** https://doi.org/10.1080/00332747.1974.11023785
*What it shows:* Classic paper on the 'relaxation response' associated with meditative repetition (the indexed abstract is a book blurb, so details were not checked).  
*Limits:* Early paper; abstract unavailable for verification, so no figures are given. Non-devotional technique.
  
*Cited on:* [vl1-25](../topics/vl1-25/preacher.html)

## Vegetarian and sattvic diet and health

**Orlich, M. J. et al. (2013). "Vegetarian Dietary Patterns and Mortality in Adventist Health Study 2." JAMA Internal Medicine.** https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2013.6473
*What it shows:* Prospective cohort of 96,469 Adventists (73,308 analysed): all vegetarians combined had an adjusted all-cause mortality hazard ratio of 0.88 (95% CI 0.80-0.97) versus non-vegetarians.  
*Limits:* Observational cohort of Seventh-day Adventists in North America; diet from a food-frequency questionnaire; mean follow-up 5.79 years; includes egg and dairy vegetarians.

**Dinu, M., Abbate, R., Gensini, G. F., Casini, A. & Sofi, F. (2017). "Vegetarian, vegan diets and multiple health outcomes: A systematic review with meta-analysis of observational studies." Critical Reviews in Food Science and Nutrition.** https://doi.org/10.1080/10408398.2016.1138447
*What it shows:* Meta-analysis of 86 cross-sectional and 10 cohort studies: vegetarians and vegans had lower BMI, cholesterol and glucose; ischemic heart disease risk was lower (RR 0.75) and total cancer incidence lower (RR 0.92).  
*Limits:* No significant association with all-cause mortality or cancer mortality; observational, so association not cause.

**Key, T. J., Appleby, P. N. & Rosell, M. S. (2006). "Health effects of vegetarian and vegan diets." Proceedings of the Nutrition Society.** https://doi.org/10.1079/pns2005481
*What it shows:* Review: vegetarians have relatively low BMI and cholesterol, a moderate reduction in mortality from IHD, but little difference in all-cause mortality compared with health-conscious non-vegetarians; vitamin B12 is a concern.  
*Limits:* Narrative review of mainly Western, well-educated vegetarians; the authors call for more data on vegans.

**Appleby, P. N. & Key, T. J. (2016). "The long-term health of vegetarians and vegans." Proceedings of the Nutrition Society.** https://doi.org/10.1017/s0029665115004334
*What it shows:* Summary of large Western cohorts: vegetarians have lower overweight and obesity and lower risk of IHD, and possibly slightly lower cancer risk; overall mortality is similar to comparable non-vegetarians.  
*Limits:* Narrative summary; findings for individual cancers and stroke are inconclusive; the authors say more research is needed on vegans.

## Values, character and youth

**Emmons, R. A. & McCullough, M. E. (2003). "Counting blessings versus burdens: An experimental investigation of gratitude and subjective well-being in daily life." Journal of Personality and Social Psychology.** https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.84.2.377
*What it shows:* Three randomised studies (weekly and daily records, and people with neuromuscular disease): gratitude listing groups showed heightened wellbeing on several, though not all, measures.  
*Limits:* Short experiments; benefits appeared on some measures only.
  
*Cited on:* [vl1-11](../topics/vl1-11/preacher.html), [vl1-28](../topics/vl1-28/preacher.html)

**Portocarrero, F. F., Gonzalez, K. & Ekema-Agbaw, M. A. (2020). "A meta-analytic review of the relationship between dispositional gratitude and well-being." Personality and Individual Differences.** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.paid.2020.110101
*What it shows:* A meta-analytic review of the relationship between dispositional gratitude and wellbeing (from the title; the abstract was not available to check).  
*Limits:* Meta-analysis of relationships between traits, so correlational by nature.

**Moffitt, T. E. et al. (2011). "A gradient of childhood self-control predicts health, wealth, and public safety." Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.** https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1010076108
*What it shows:* A cohort of 1,000 children followed from birth to 32: childhood self-control predicted health, substance dependence, finances and criminal offending, in a gradient, independent of intelligence and social class.  
*Limits:* One birth cohort; observational.
  
*Cited on:* [vl1-09](../topics/vl1-09/preacher.html), [vl1-23](../topics/vl1-23/preacher.html)

**Kasser, T. & Ryan, R. M. (1993). "A dark side of the American dream: Correlates of financial success as a central life aspiration." Journal of Personality and Social Psychology.** https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.65.2.410
*What it shows:* Three studies: when money-related values are more central than other values, they go with lower wellbeing and mental health in college students and in a non-college sample.  
*Limits:* Correlational; participants' values and wellbeing measured by questionnaire.
  
*Cited on:* [vl2-03](../topics/vl2-03/preacher.html)

**Moldes, O. & Ku, L. (2020). "Materialistic cues make us miserable: A meta-analysis of the experimental evidence for the effects of materialism on individual and societal well-being." Psychology & Marketing.** https://doi.org/10.1002/mar.21387
*What it shows:* Meta-analysis of 27 experimental studies (N = 3,649) priming materialism: materialistic cues lowered individual (delta = -0.39) and societal (-0.41) wellbeing.  
*Limits:* Priming experiments; the authors discuss limitations and research gaps.
  
*Cited on:* [vl2-03](../topics/vl2-03/preacher.html)

**Diener, E., Lucas, R. E. & Scollon, C. N. (2006). "Beyond the hedonic treadmill: Revising the adaptation theory of well-being." American Psychologist.** https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.61.4.305
*What it shows:* Argues that the hedonic treadmill needs five revisions: set points are not neutral, differ between people, differ by component of wellbeing, can change, and adaptation varies between individuals.  
*Limits:* Review of empirical work; a theoretical revision.
  
*Cited on:* [vl1-48](../topics/vl1-48/preacher.html), [vl2-03](../topics/vl2-03/preacher.html)

**Steger, M. F. & Frazier, P. (2005). "Meaning in Life: One Link in the Chain From Religiousness to Well-Being." Journal of Counseling Psychology.** https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0167.52.4.574
*What it shows:* In two studies, meaning in life mediated the link between religiousness and life satisfaction, self-esteem and optimism, and between daily religious behaviour and wellbeing.  
*Limits:* Correlational/mediation design; the abstract does not report sample sizes.
  
*Cited on:* [vl1-13](../topics/vl1-13/preacher.html)

## Consciousness and the self

**Koch, C., Massimini, M., Boly, M. & Tononi, G. (2016). "Neural correlates of consciousness: progress and problems." Nature Reviews Neuroscience.** https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn.2016.22
*What it shows:* Review: the anatomical correlates of consciousness lie mainly in a posterior cortical hot zone rather than a fronto-parietal network; some candidate markers proved illusory.  
*Limits:* Identifies brain correlates, not why there is experience; does not settle materialist versus non-materialist views.
  
*Cited on:* [vl2-08](../topics/vl2-08/preacher.html)

**van Lommel, P., van Wees, R., Meyers, V. & Elfferich, I. (2001). "Near-death experience in survivors of cardiac arrest: a prospective study in the Netherlands." The Lancet.** https://doi.org/10.1016/s0140-6736%2801%2907100-8
*What it shows:* Prospective study of 344 resuscitated cardiac patients in ten Dutch hospitals: 62 (18%) reported a near-death experience, 41 (12%) a core experience; occurrence was not linked to duration of arrest, medication or fear of death.  
*Limits:* Patients reported afterwards; the authors note the frequency depended on how NDE was defined; interpretation is contested.

**van Lommel, P. (2011). "Near-death experiences: the experience of the self as real and not as an illusion." Annals of the New York Academy of Sciences.** https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1749-6632.2011.06080.x
*What it shows:* Argues that prospective NDE studies in cardiac arrest survivors show the NDE cannot be reduced to imagination, fear, hallucination, drugs or oxygen deficiency, and that consciousness may need new hypotheses.  
*Limits:* An author's argument and interpretation, not a new study; a contested position.

**Greyson, B. & Stevenson, I. (1980). "The phenomenology of near-death experiences." American Journal of Psychiatry.** https://doi.org/10.1176/ajp.137.10.1193
*What it shows:* Retrospective study of 78 near-death experience reports from narratives, questionnaires, interviews and medical records; cultural, psychological and physiological factors explained some but not all features.  
*Limits:* Retrospective and self-reported.

**Tucker, J. B. (2008). "Children's Reports of Past-Life Memories: A Review." EXPLORE.** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.explore.2008.04.001
*What it shows:* Review of 45 years of research on young children's reports of past-life memories, where many children gave enough detail for a deceased person to be identified; cases occur worldwide.  
*Limits:* Review by a researcher in the field; cases are reports, and the question is unresolved. Present as evidence under study, not as proof.
  
*Cited on:* [vl2-01](../topics/vl2-01/preacher.html)

## Meditation, prayer and mental health

**Goyal, M. et al. (2014). "Meditation Programs for Psychological Stress and Well-being." JAMA Internal Medicine.** https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2013.13018
*What it shows:* Review of 47 trials (3,515 participants): moderate evidence of improved anxiety, depression and pain after mindfulness programmes; low evidence for stress and mental health quality of life.  
*Limits:* Concerns mindfulness programmes in clinical groups (a secular form), compared with active controls.
  
*Cited on:* [vl1-19](../topics/vl1-19/preacher.html)

**Hofmann, S. G., Sawyer, A. T., Witt, A. A. & Oh, D. (2010). "The effect of mindfulness-based therapy on anxiety and depression: A meta-analytic review." Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology.** https://doi.org/10.1037/a0018555
*What it shows:* Meta-analysis of 39 studies (1,140 participants): mindfulness-based therapy was moderately effective for anxiety (g = 0.63) and mood (g = 0.59); larger in patients with anxiety and mood disorders.  
*Limits:* Pre-post effect sizes in clinical samples; secular mindfulness-based therapy.
  
*Cited on:* [vl1-18](../topics/vl1-18/preacher.html)

**Galante, J., Galante, I., Bekkers, M.-J. & Gallacher, J. (2014). "Effect of kindness-based meditation on health and well-being: A systematic review and meta-analysis." Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology.** https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037249
*What it shows:* Meta-analysis of 22 randomised trials: kindness-based meditation moderately decreased depression and increased mindfulness and compassion versus passive controls.  
*Limits:* The authors report low-to-moderate methodological quality, imprecision from small studies and inconclusive results versus active controls.
  
*Cited on:* [vl1-07](../topics/vl1-07/preacher.html)

**Chiesa, A., Calati, R. & Serretti, A. (2011). "Does mindfulness training improve cognitive abilities? A systematic review of neuropsychological findings." Clinical Psychology Review.** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2010.11.003
*What it shows:* Review of 23 studies: mindfulness practice was associated with improvements in selective and executive attention and possibly working memory.  
*Limits:* The authors note many studies had methodological limitations and some results were negative.
  
*Cited on:* [vl1-03](../topics/vl1-03/preacher.html)

**Koenig, H. G. (2009). "Research on Religion, Spirituality, and Mental Health: A Review." The Canadian Journal of Psychiatry.** https://doi.org/10.1177/070674370905400502
*What it shows:* Review of religion, spirituality and mental health (depression, suicide, anxiety, psychosis, substance abuse): religion can be a resource for coping with stress.  
*Limits:* The author notes religion is often entangled with disorders, so it can be a resource or a liability.
  
*Cited on:* [vl1-05](../topics/vl1-05/preacher.html)

**Goncalves, J. P. B., Lucchetti, G., Menezes, P. R. & Vallada, H. (2015). "Religious and spiritual interventions in mental health care: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled clinical trials." Psychological Medicine.** https://doi.org/10.1017/s0033291715001166
*What it shows:* Systematic review and meta-analysis of 23 randomised trials: religious/spiritual interventions had significant effects on anxiety, and in general decreased stress, alcoholism and depression.  
*Limits:* The authors note diverse protocols and outcomes and lack of standardisation.

**Moreira-Almeida, A., Lotufo Neto, F. & Koenig, H. G. (2006). "Religiousness and mental health: a review." Revista Brasileira de Psiquiatria.** https://doi.org/10.1590/s1516-44462006000300018
*What it shows:* Review: most well-conducted studies found higher religious involvement positively associated with wellbeing and less depression, suicidal behaviour and substance use; more robust under stress.  
*Limits:* Review of mostly observational studies; the authors call for understanding of mediating factors.

**Carsley, D., Khoury, B. & Heath, N. L. (2018). "Effectiveness of Mindfulness Interventions for Mental Health in Schools: a Comprehensive Meta-analysis." Mindfulness.** https://doi.org/10.1007/s12671-017-0839-2
*What it shows:* A meta-analysis of mindfulness interventions for mental health in schools (from the title; the abstract was not available to check).  
*Limits:* No figures given because the abstract was not available.
